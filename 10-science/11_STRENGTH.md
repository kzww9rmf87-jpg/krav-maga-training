# STRENGTH

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Strength model defines how maximal force production contributes to combat performance.

Strength is one of the primary physical capabilities within the Combat Athlete System.

It provides the foundation upon which many other athletic qualities are built.

---

# Definition

Strength is the ability to produce force against external resistance.

Strength is task-specific.

It depends on:

The movement performed.

The velocity of contraction.

The direction of force.

The athlete.

---

# Philosophy

Strength is not an objective.

Strength is a tool.

The objective is improved combat performance.

Every strength recommendation should therefore improve the athlete's ability to perform in combat-specific situations.

---

# Biological Foundations

Strength depends on multiple interacting systems.

Neural System

Muscular System

Connective Tissue

Bone

Motor Coordination

Psychological Readiness

No single system determines strength.

---

# Neural Factors

Neural adaptations include:

Motor unit recruitment.

Motor unit synchronization.

Rate coding.

Reduced neural inhibition.

Intermuscular coordination.

These adaptations generally occur before significant hypertrophy.

---

# Muscular Factors

Strength is influenced by:

Cross-sectional area.

Muscle architecture.

Fiber composition.

Pennation angle.

Muscle quality.

Muscle size contributes to strength,

but is not sufficient on its own.

---

# Connective Tissue

Strong tendons improve:

Force transmission.

Elastic energy storage.

Joint stability.

Explosive performance.

Connective tissue adapts more slowly than muscle.

Programming should respect this difference.

# Types of Strength

CAS distinguishes several forms of strength.

Maximum Strength

Relative Strength

Absolute Strength

Explosive Strength

Starting Strength

Reactive Strength

Strength Endurance

Every type serves different purposes.

---

# Maximum Strength

Definition

Highest force that can be voluntarily produced.

Primary Adaptations

Neural.

Muscular.

Typical Methods

Heavy compound lifts.

Low repetitions.

Long rest intervals.

Combat Transfer

Clinch.

Throws.

Power foundation.

Grip.

Striking force.

---

# Relative Strength

Definition

Strength relative to body mass.

Critical for:

Combat sports with weight classes.

Pull-ups.

Gymnastic movements.

Acceleration.

Movement efficiency.

---

# Absolute Strength

Definition

Total force regardless of body mass.

Important when:

Moving external loads.

Controlling opponents.

Generating impact force.

---

# Explosive Strength

Definition

Ability to develop force rapidly.

Examples

Olympic lifts.

Medicine ball throws.

Jump squats.

Combat Transfer

Punches.

Kicks.

Shots.

Sprawls.

Explosive transitions.

---

# Starting Strength

Definition

Force produced during the initial phase of movement.

Critical for:

First step.

First punch.

Initial takedown.

Reaction speed.

---

# Reactive Strength

Definition

Ability to rapidly transition from eccentric to concentric action.

Examples

Plyometrics.

Depth jumps.

Bounding.

Combat Transfer

Footwork.

Counter-attacks.

Direction changes.

---

# Isometric Strength

Definition

Force production without visible movement.

Examples

Planks.

Overcoming isometrics.

Yielding isometrics.

Combat Transfer

Grip fighting.

Clinch.

Positional control.

---

# Strength Endurance

Definition

Ability to repeatedly produce force over time.

Important for:

Long exchanges.

Repeated takedowns.

Extended clinch work.

Later rounds.

---

# Transfer to Combat Sports

Strength contributes to:

Punching power.

Kicking power.

Grip.

Throwing.

Ground control.

Takedown ability.

Postural control.

Movement efficiency.

Strength should never be developed independently from sport requirements.
