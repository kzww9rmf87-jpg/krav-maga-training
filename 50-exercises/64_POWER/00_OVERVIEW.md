# POWER OVERVIEW

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Power category defines loaded explosive exercises used to improve the athlete’s ability to produce high levels of force rapidly.

Within CAS, power exercises bridge the gap between maximal strength and fast athletic action.

They are selected to improve acceleration, rate of force development, intermuscular coordination and the rapid transfer of force through the entire kinetic chain.

They are not selected primarily for fatigue, hypertrophy or conditioning.

Their value depends on maximal intent, appropriate loading, technical quality and the preservation of movement velocity.

---

# Philosophy

Combat athletes rarely have unlimited time to apply force.

They must strike, shoot, sprawl, clinch, throw, escape and change direction within very short movement windows.

High force capacity is useful only when it can be expressed quickly enough to influence these actions.

CAS therefore treats power training as the development of fast force rather than simply the performance of fast-looking exercises.

The governing principle is:

> Move the appropriate load with maximal intent while preserving technical quality and acceleration.

When bar speed, coordination, posture or intent deteriorates meaningfully, the useful power stimulus has ended.

# Core Principle

Mechanical power depends on the interaction between force and velocity.

Very heavy loads allow high force but low movement velocity.

Very light loads allow high velocity but may provide insufficient force demand.

Power training occupies the region where force and velocity are combined effectively for the intended adaptation.

CAS does not assume that one universal load maximizes power for every exercise or athlete.

The appropriate load depends on:

- the exercise,
- the athlete’s strength level,
- technical proficiency,
- movement amplitude,
- training objective,
- fatigue state,
- and the measurement method available.

Maximal intent is required even when the external load is moderate.

---

# Strength–Power–Speed Continuum

CAS places loaded power exercises between maximal-strength work and unloaded speed or plyometric work.

## Strength-Dominant Power

Higher external load with lower movement velocity.

Primary qualities:

- explosive strength,
- force production under time pressure,
- strength-speed.

Typical examples:

- heavier Push Press variations,
- heavier Hang High Pull variations.

## Balanced Power

Moderate external load with high acceleration.

Primary qualities:

- peak mechanical power,
- coordinated whole-body propulsion,
- rapid force transfer.

Typical examples:

- Push Press,
- Hang Power Clean,
- Hang High Pull.

## Speed-Dominant Power

Lower external load with maximal velocity.

Primary qualities:

- speed-strength,
- rapid acceleration,
- technical velocity.

Typical examples:

- Jump Shrug,
- lighter ballistic derivatives.

These zones overlap and must not be treated as rigid categories.

---

# Loaded Power Categories

## Upper-Body Propulsive Power

Exercises in which the lower body and trunk transfer force into an explosive upper-body action.

Primary qualities:

- vertical force transfer,
- overhead acceleration,
- lower-to-upper kinetic-chain sequencing.

CAS example:

- Push Press

## Pulling Power

Exercises that accelerate the bar vertically through aggressive hip and knee extension followed by an upper-body pull.

Primary qualities:

- triple extension,
- vertical acceleration,
- posterior-chain power,
- upper-back involvement.

CAS examples:

- Hang High Pull
- Jump Shrug

## Receiving Power

Exercises that require the athlete to accelerate the implement and then receive it in a stable position.

Primary qualities:

- explosive extension,
- rapid transition under the bar,
- deceleration,
- positional stability.

CAS example:

- Hang Power Clean

## Ballistic Derivatives

Exercises in which the athlete continues accelerating through the movement instead of deliberately decelerating the load near the end of the range.

Primary qualities:

- maximal intent,
- high acceleration,
- speed-strength.

CAS examples:

- Jump Shrug
- selected Push Press executions

# Exercises Included

The Power category currently contains:

- Push Press
- Hang High Pull
- Hang Power Clean
- Jump Shrug

Each exercise has a distinct technical demand and risk profile.

They must not be treated as interchangeable simply because all are explosive.

# Global Exercise Roles

## Push Press

Primary role:

- whole-body vertical pressing power.

Primary characteristics:

- leg-driven overhead propulsion,
- force transfer through the trunk,
- moderate technical complexity,
- useful bridge between strict pressing strength and ballistic upper-body action.

## Hang High Pull

Primary role:

- explosive pulling power without a catch.

Primary characteristics:

- aggressive triple extension,
- high vertical bar acceleration,
- substantial upper-back contribution,
- lower receiving-skill demand than the Hang Power Clean.

## Hang Power Clean

Primary role:

- explosive triple extension combined with rapid receiving skill.

Primary characteristics:

- high technical complexity,
- force production and absorption,
- rapid transition under the bar,
- strong athletic coordination demand.

## Jump Shrug

Primary role:

- simple, high-velocity triple-extension power.

Primary characteristics:

- minimal catch complexity,
- strong emphasis on lower-body projection,
- useful regression or alternative to more technical Olympic-lift derivatives,
- relatively direct expression of lower-body power.

# Biomechanical Profile

Loaded power exercises are generally multi-joint movements performed with maximal voluntary acceleration.

Common biomechanical features include:

- rapid hip and knee extension,
- force transfer through a braced trunk,
- coordinated sequencing from proximal to distal segments,
- high intent during the concentric phase,
- controlled repositioning or receiving after acceleration,
- and a strong requirement for balance over the base of support.

The movement must remain organized despite high acceleration.

Power is not improved by moving chaotically.

---

# Physiological Profile

Loaded power training primarily stresses the neuromuscular system.

Its useful stimulus depends more on repetition quality than on metabolic accumulation.

Typical characteristics include:

- low to moderate repetitions per set,
- long enough recovery to preserve output,
- moderate total volume,
- high motor-unit recruitment,
- limited local muscular failure,
- and low tolerance for velocity loss.

Power training can create substantial neural and connective-tissue stress even when the athlete does not feel heavily fatigued metabolically.

---

# Neurological Profile

The principal neurological demands are:

- rapid motor-unit recruitment,
- high discharge frequency,
- precise intermuscular coordination,
- timing of proximal-to-distal force transfer,
- and maintenance of maximal intent.

Technical familiarity affects expression.

An athlete who is still consciously solving the movement cannot express the same power as an athlete with stable motor patterns.

CAS therefore distinguishes technical learning from true power training.

---

# Muscular Profile

The exact contribution varies by exercise, but the category commonly involves:

- gluteus maximus,
- quadriceps,
- hamstrings,
- plantar flexors,
- spinal erectors,
- abdominal wall,
- trapezius,
- rhomboids,
- deltoids,
- triceps,
- forearm and grip musculature.

Loaded power is fundamentally a coordinated whole-body quality.

Isolated muscle fatigue must not become the dominant limiter.

---

# Joint Demand

## Ankle

The ankle contributes to the final phase of triple extension and must tolerate rapid plantar-flexion forces.

Reduced ankle control may impair projection and landing stability.

## Knee

The knee contributes strongly to propulsion and, in receiving movements, to force absorption.

Pain, instability or poor alignment can make loaded explosive work inappropriate.

## Hip

The hip is a principal source of force in pulling derivatives.

Effective power requires rapid extension without premature lumbar compensation.

## Lumbar Spine

The lumbar spine should transmit force through a stable trunk rather than generate excessive movement.

Loss of bracing under acceleration increases risk and reduces transfer.

## Shoulder

Shoulder demand is highest in the Push Press and in the receiving phase of the Hang Power Clean.

Adequate overhead or front-rack tolerance is required depending on the exercise.

## Wrist and Elbow

The Hang Power Clean requires sufficient front-rack mobility and receiving tolerance.

The Hang High Pull requires controlled elbow elevation without forcing painful positions.

---

# Technical Complexity Classification

## Low to Moderate Complexity

- Jump Shrug
- Push Press

These exercises still require coaching but are generally learned faster than catch-based Olympic derivatives.

## Moderate Complexity

- Hang High Pull

This exercise requires coordinated extension and pulling mechanics but removes the catch.

## High Complexity

- Hang Power Clean

This exercise requires propulsion, bar path control, rapid turnover and stable receiving mechanics.

Technical proficiency must be demonstrated before loading is progressed aggressively.

---

# Intensity Determinants

Power-training intensity is not defined by load alone.

CAS considers:

- external load,
- movement velocity,
- movement intent,
- technical complexity,
- range of motion,
- receiving demand,
- starting position,
- repetition count,
- fatigue level,
- and athlete proficiency.

A technically complex exercise performed with a moderate load may impose a higher total demand than a simpler exercise with a heavier load.

---

# Velocity Principle

Power training requires the athlete to attempt to move the load as rapidly as possible during the propulsive phase.

CAS uses velocity loss as a key termination signal when measurement is available.

When direct measurement is unavailable, the engine uses observable proxies:

- reduced bar height,
- slower lockout,
- delayed extension,
- shortened range,
- loss of coordination,
- excessive grinding,
- or visible reduction in intent.

A repetition that becomes a slow strength repetition no longer provides the intended power stimulus.

---

# General Prescription Ranges

Prescription must remain exercise-specific and athlete-specific.

The following ranges are general CAS boundaries rather than universal rules.

## Peak Power Development

Typical structure:

- 2 to 5 repetitions per set,
- 3 to 6 work sets,
- 2 to 4 minutes of rest,
- moderate loading,
- maximal concentric intent,
- low velocity loss.

## Strength-Speed Development

Typical structure:

- 2 to 4 repetitions per set,
- 3 to 5 work sets,
- moderate to moderately heavy loading,
- complete technical control,
- sufficient rest to preserve acceleration.

## Speed-Strength Development

Typical structure:

- 2 to 5 repetitions per set,
- lighter loading,
- maximal movement velocity,
- longer rest than conditioning work,
- immediate termination when speed declines.

## Technical Acquisition

Typical structure:

- 2 to 5 repetitions per set,
- low load,
- multiple brief sets,
- frequent coaching feedback,
- no fatigue-driven repetition targets.

Technical practice must not be misclassified as high-output power work.

---

# Volume Metrics

CAS tracks power volume using one or more of the following:

- total explosive repetitions,
- total sets,
- external load,
- average velocity,
- peak velocity,
- peak power,
- velocity loss,
- technical success rate,
- and number of high-quality contacts or catches.

Total tonnage alone is insufficient because it does not describe movement speed or output quality.

---

# Placement Within the Session

Loaded power exercises are generally placed early in the session.

Preferred sequence:

1. general preparation,
2. movement-specific preparation,
3. power exercise,
4. maximal-strength work,
5. accessory work,
6. conditioning.

Power may follow low-volume activation or plyometric preparation when the pairing is intentional and fatigue remains minimal.

Power work should not normally follow exhausting conditioning, high-volume hypertrophy or repeated failure sets.

---

# Frequency

Typical exposure is:

- 1 to 3 sessions per week,
- with 1 to 2 loaded power exercises per session,
- depending on technical level, total combat workload and recovery capacity.

Higher frequency may be appropriate when volume per session is low and technical practice is the primary objective.

Lower frequency may be required when the athlete is also performing high volumes of sparring, sprinting, jumping or heavy strength training.

---

# Readiness Requirements

Before prescribing loaded power exercises, CAS evaluates:

- pain status,
- training age,
- movement competency,
- strength foundation,
- technical familiarity,
- sleep and recovery,
- recent combat load,
- joint tolerance,
- and available coaching or supervision.

The Hang Power Clean requires the highest technical-readiness threshold in the category.

The Jump Shrug generally requires the lowest.

---

# Progression Logic

Progression should occur through one variable at a time.

Possible progressions include:

- improved technical consistency,
- increased movement velocity,
- increased external load while preserving speed,
- increased set count,
- increased range of motion,
- transition from simpler to more complex derivatives,
- improved receiving depth or stability,
- or reduced velocity loss across the session.

Load must not increase merely because the athlete completed all prescribed repetitions.

The athlete must complete them with the intended power characteristics.

---

# Regression Logic

Regression is required when:

- technique is unstable,
- pain appears,
- the athlete cannot maintain acceleration,
- receiving positions are unsafe,
- fatigue is high,
- mobility restrictions alter mechanics,
- or the exercise exceeds the athlete’s current skill level.

Regression options include:

- reducing load,
- reducing repetitions,
- simplifying the starting position,
- removing the catch,
- reducing range of motion,
- using a simpler derivative,
- or replacing loaded power with lower-risk ballistic or plyometric work.

---

# Exercise Selection Logic

## Select Push Press When

- the objective is whole-body overhead power,
- the athlete can tolerate overhead loading,
- lower-to-upper force transfer is a priority,
- technical complexity must remain moderate,
- and strict pressing alone does not provide the desired acceleration stimulus.

## Select Hang High Pull When

- the objective is explosive pulling power,
- triple extension is a priority,
- the athlete is not ready for or does not need a catch,
- upper-back power is useful,
- and a moderate-complexity Olympic-lift derivative is appropriate.

## Select Hang Power Clean When

- the athlete has adequate technical proficiency,
- explosive extension and receiving skill are both objectives,
- front-rack tolerance is sufficient,
- coaching quality is available,
- and the benefit of the catch justifies the added complexity.

## Select Jump Shrug When

- the objective is simple, high-velocity triple extension,
- the athlete needs a lower-complexity power derivative,
- receiving skill is not required,
- maximal acceleration is the priority,
- or fatigue and technical constraints make more complex derivatives inappropriate.

---

# Power Versus Plyometrics

Loaded power and plyometric training are related but distinct.

Plyometrics emphasize rapid force production through jumps, hops, bounds and stretch-shortening-cycle actions.

Loaded power exercises emphasize acceleration of an external resistance.

Plyometrics usually impose greater landing or ground-contact demands.

Loaded power exercises usually impose greater external-load and technical barbell demands.

CAS may use both within the same programme, but their total neural and mechanical stress must be managed together.

---

# Power Versus Maximal Strength

Maximal-strength training improves the ceiling of force production.

Power training improves how rapidly useful force can be expressed.

A stronger athlete does not automatically become more powerful if movement velocity and coordination are not trained.

A fast athlete with insufficient force capacity may also have limited power potential.

CAS therefore develops strength and power as complementary capabilities.

---

# Power Versus Conditioning

Power training is not conditioning.

Short rest periods, large circuits and high repetition counts may create fatigue but usually reduce output quality.

When the objective is power:

- repetitions remain low,
- rest remains sufficient,
- movement quality remains high,
- and the session stops before substantial velocity deterioration.

Exercises from this category may appear in conditioning only when the engine explicitly changes the adaptation target and the safety profile remains acceptable.

---

# Combat Transfer

Loaded power training supports combat performance by improving the athlete’s ability to generate and transmit force rapidly.

Potential transfers include:

- faster lower-body drive,
- stronger kinetic-chain sequencing,
- more forceful hip extension,
- improved projection from the floor,
- increased ability to accelerate the arms after leg drive,
- stronger clinch and throwing actions,
- and improved explosive transitions.

Transfer is indirect.

Power exercises build physical capability; technical combat practice teaches the athlete how to apply it.

# Fatigue Cost

The fatigue cost of loaded power training depends on:

- load,
- total repetitions,
- technical complexity,
- eccentric demand,
- receiving demand,
- proximity to failure,
- and overlap with other high-intensity work.

General classification:

- Jump Shrug: low to moderate,
- Push Press: moderate,
- Hang High Pull: moderate,
- Hang Power Clean: moderate to high when technically or neurally demanding.

A session can produce low metabolic fatigue while still creating meaningful neural fatigue.

---

# Recovery Interaction

Loaded power training overlaps with:

- maximal-strength training,
- sprinting,
- plyometrics,
- heavy bag power work,
- hard sparring,
- wrestling exchanges,
- and explosive technical practice.

CAS must manage these stressors as part of one power and neural-load budget.

High-output loaded power should be reduced when the athlete has:

- substantial lower-body soreness,
- poor sleep,
- reduced coordination,
- high sparring fatigue,
- grip exhaustion,
- or joint irritation.

---

# Safety Constraints

Loaded power exercises must be regressed, substituted or removed when:

- pain alters movement,
- the athlete cannot control the implement,
- the training area is unsafe,
- equipment is unsuitable,
- receiving mechanics are unstable,
- overhead or front-rack positions are not tolerated,
- fatigue causes repeated technical breakdown,
- or the athlete lacks the required coaching and experience.

Explosive intent does not justify uncontrolled execution.

---

# Common Errors Across the Category

Common errors include:

- using excessive load,
- turning the repetition into a slow grind,
- chasing fatigue instead of output,
- bending the arms before completing lower-body extension,
- losing trunk stiffness,
- allowing the bar to drift away from the body,
- extending through the lumbar spine instead of the hips,
- landing or receiving without control,
- performing too many repetitions per set,
- reducing rest excessively,
- and progressing complexity before competency.

---

# Coaching Priorities

CAS prioritizes the following cues:

1. establish a stable starting position,
2. brace before force production,
3. accelerate with maximal intent,
4. complete lower-body extension in the correct sequence,
5. keep the load close to the body when applicable,
6. receive or finish in a controlled position,
7. reset fully before the next repetition,
8. stop when speed or coordination declines.

The best cue is the one that improves output without creating unnecessary conscious complexity.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine must:

- select loaded power only when it matches the target adaptation,
- verify exercise-specific technical readiness,
- place power work before fatigue-producing work unless an explicit exception exists,
- prescribe low-repetition sets,
- provide sufficient recovery,
- avoid muscular failure,
- limit velocity loss,
- account for overlap with plyometrics, sprints, sparring and heavy strength work,
- prefer simpler derivatives when complexity offers no clear benefit,
- and substitute safely when pain, equipment or skill constraints are present.

The engine must never select the Hang Power Clean merely because it appears more athletic or advanced.

Complexity must earn its place through a specific adaptation benefit.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When selecting an exercise from the Power category, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the target capability,
- why loaded power is appropriate,
- why the selected exercise is preferable to alternatives,
- required technical prerequisites,
- chosen loading logic,
- repetition and rest logic,
- fatigue constraints,
- relevant combat-training overlap,
- substitutions considered,
- and the termination criteria for quality loss.

A valid Decision Trace must explain both selection and exclusion.

---

# Summary

The Power category develops the ability to express force rapidly against external resistance.

Its exercises combine maximal intent, controlled loading, precise coordination and low-fatigue repetition structures.

Push Press develops whole-body overhead propulsion.

Hang High Pull develops explosive pulling power without a catch.

Hang Power Clean combines explosive extension with rapid receiving skill.

Jump Shrug provides a simpler high-velocity triple-extension option.

Within CAS, loaded power training must remain fast, technically stable, appropriately loaded and integrated with the athlete’s total strength, plyometric and combat workload.
