# HANG HIGH PULL

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Hang High Pull is a loaded whole-body power exercise used to develop rapid force production through the lower body, hips and trunk while transferring that force into an explosive vertical pull.

Within CAS, it is selected primarily to improve lower-body power, hip extension velocity, vertical force expression, rate of force development and coordinated force transfer through the kinetic chain.

It is not treated as an upper-body pulling-strength exercise or as a technical substitute for a complete Olympic lift when the athlete lacks the required receiving skill.

Its training value depends on an aggressive extension, close bar path, relaxed arms during the initial drive, high movement velocity and a controlled return to the hang position.

---

# Philosophy

The Hang High Pull teaches the athlete to accelerate an external load through coordinated extension of the ankles, knees and hips, then continue the movement with an explosive upward pull.

The governing principle is:

> Extend violently through the floor, keep the bar close and allow the upper body to continue—not initiate—the acceleration.

The exercise is valuable because it preserves much of the power-development stimulus associated with weightlifting derivatives without requiring the athlete to catch the bar in a front-rack or overhead position.

It loses its primary value when the athlete bends the arms early, turns the movement into an upright row, jumps forward, overextends the lumbar spine or continues repetitions after velocity and coordination deteriorate.

# Biomechanical Profile

The Hang High Pull begins with the bar held above or near the knees in a balanced hang position.

The athlete loads the posterior chain through a controlled hip hinge while maintaining a stable trunk, neutral head position and full-foot pressure.

The movement is initiated by forcefully extending the hips and knees against the ground.

The ankles complete the extension as the athlete reaches a tall, powerful finish.

The bar should travel vertically and remain close to the body.

Only after the lower body has produced maximal acceleration do the elbows rise outward and upward to continue the bar path.

The arms do not curl the bar.

The athlete does not receive the bar in a rack position.

Effective execution requires:

- a stable and balanced hang position,
- controlled pre-tension before acceleration,
- aggressive hip and knee extension,
- coordinated triple extension,
- delayed elbow flexion,
- a close and predominantly vertical bar path,
- a tall finish without excessive backward lean,
- and a controlled return to the starting position.

The Hang High Pull differs from the Hang Power Clean because the bar is not received on the shoulders.

It differs from the Upright Row because the movement is driven primarily by lower-body power rather than shoulder and elbow flexion.

It differs from the Clean Pull because the elbows continue upward after extension rather than remaining relatively straight.

# Loading Profile

Primary Loading Zone

Light to moderate external load

Typical Loading Orientation

Strength-speed to speed-strength, depending on the selected load

Common Reference Range

Approximately 30 to 60 percent of an athlete's conventional clean one-repetition maximum when such a reference is technically valid

Alternative Reference

A load that permits maximal acceleration, stable mechanics and consistent bar height across repetitions

The correct load is not defined by percentage alone.

CAS should prioritize execution velocity, coordination and repeatability over absolute load.

Loads that cause early arm pull, incomplete extension, grinding or excessive backward lean are too heavy for the intended power objective.

Very light loads that provide no meaningful external resistance may be useful for technical learning but may not provide sufficient strength-speed stimulus.

# Execution Standard

## Starting Position

Stand with the feet approximately hip-width apart.

Hold the bar with a clean-width or slightly wider overhand grip.

Keep the arms long and the shoulders positioned slightly in front of or directly above the bar.

Maintain a neutral head position and a braced trunk.

Distribute pressure through the whole foot.

## Hang Position

Hinge at the hips and allow the knees to bend slightly.

Lower the bar to a position above or near the knees according to the programmed variation.

Keep the bar close to the thighs.

Maintain a stable spine and balanced foot pressure.

Create tension through the posterior chain without pausing excessively.

## Extension

Drive forcefully into the floor.

Extend the knees and hips rapidly.

Finish tall through coordinated ankle, knee and hip extension.

Do not bend the elbows before the lower body has completed the main acceleration phase.

## High Pull

Allow the elbows to rise outward and upward after full extension.

Keep the bar close to the torso.

Guide the bar vertically rather than curling it toward the chest.

The exact bar height may vary with load, limb proportions and athlete power.

## Peak Position

Reach a tall, balanced position.

Avoid excessive lumbar extension or backward displacement.

Maintain control of the bar and body alignment.

## Return

Allow the bar to descend under control.

Absorb the load through the hips and knees.

Re-establish the hang position before the next repetition.

Do not perform a rapid uncontrolled drop into the thighs or knees.

# Technical Failure Criteria

The repetition is considered technically failed when one or more of the following occur:

- the athlete bends the elbows before meaningful hip and knee extension,
- the movement becomes an upright row,
- the bar drifts substantially away from the body,
- the athlete jumps forward or backward excessively,
- the heels leave the floor before force has been effectively transmitted,
- hip extension is incomplete,
- the athlete hyperextends the lumbar spine at the top,
- the bar collides heavily with the thighs or pelvis,
- the athlete loses neutral spinal control,
- the return to the hang position is uncontrolled,
- bar height or velocity falls substantially,
- grip failure alters the bar path,
- or pain occurs.

A technically successful repetition must preserve both acceleration and sequencing.

Completion alone is not sufficient.

# Progression Model

## Stage 1 — Hinge Competency

The athlete demonstrates a stable hip hinge with neutral spinal control and whole-foot pressure.

## Stage 2 — Loaded Hinge Competency

The athlete performs controlled Romanian Deadlifts or similar movements without losing trunk position.

## Stage 3 — Extension Drill

The athlete learns to accelerate through the floor using jump shrugs or clean pulls with long arms.

## Stage 4 — Light Hang High Pull

The athlete performs low-load repetitions with delayed elbow flexion and a close bar path.

## Stage 5 — Standard Hang High Pull

The athlete uses moderate loads for repeated high-quality power sets.

## Stage 6 — Strength-Speed Hang High Pull

The athlete increases external load while preserving complete extension and acceptable velocity.

## Stage 7 — Advanced Monitoring or Variation

The athlete uses velocity monitoring, cluster sets, block variations or individualized load zones.

Progression should stop or regress when technical sequencing deteriorates.

# Combat Transfer

The Hang High Pull has moderate to high general transfer to combat sports because it develops rapid force production through the lower body and hips, which contribute to explosive striking, level changes, takedown entries, throws and rapid positional actions.

Potential transfer qualities include:

- explosive hip extension,
- rapid lower-body force production,
- force transfer through a braced trunk,
- whole-body sequencing,
- vertical and upward propulsive force,
- posterior-chain power,
- and the ability to accelerate an external resistance quickly.

The exercise does not reproduce the exact vectors, timing or technical patterns of punches, kicks or grappling actions.

Its transfer is therefore general rather than skill-specific.

CAS should use it to build physical capacity that supports combat performance, not to replace technical practice.

# CAS Selection Logic

CAS may select the Hang High Pull when:

- the primary objective includes power or explosive strength,
- lower-body or hip-extension power is relevant,
- the athlete has adequate hinge and barbell competency,
- a loaded whole-body power exercise is appropriate,
- front-rack receiving skill is unnecessary or undesirable,
- sufficient equipment and space are available,
- and current readiness supports high-velocity execution.

CAS should deprioritize or reject the exercise when:

- the athlete is a true barbell novice without supervision,
- hinge mechanics are unstable,
- acute low-back, hamstring, shoulder or elbow symptoms are present,
- fatigue prevents maximal intent,
- the session already contains excessive posterior-chain loading,
- the training objective is primarily conditioning,
- or a lower-skill option provides similar adaptation with less risk.

Selection priority increases when the athlete needs a bridge between general strength exercises and more technical weightlifting derivatives.

# Evidence Classification

Evidence Level

Moderate

The Hang High Pull belongs to the family of weightlifting derivatives commonly used to develop power, rate of force development and explosive extension.

Its exact superiority over other loaded power exercises is context-dependent.

The strongest rationale supports its use when:

- the athlete can execute the movement competently,
- the load allows high acceleration,
- volume remains low,
- and the exercise fits the wider training plan.

CAS should avoid treating the movement as universally necessary.

It is one option among several for developing loaded whole-body power.

# Decision Summary

The Hang High Pull is a moderate- to high-skill loaded power exercise that develops explosive triple extension, hip power, vertical force production and whole-body coordination without requiring a catch.

CAS should select it when the athlete has competent hinge mechanics, adequate barbell control and a clear need for loaded lower-body power or strength-speed development.

It should be performed early in the session with low repetitions, maximal intent, controlled loading and sufficient recovery.

The exercise should be rejected or regressed when early arm pull, unstable spinal position, bar drift, fatigue or pain prevents high-quality execution.

Its governing rule is:

> Extend first, pull second, keep the bar close and stop before speed or sequencing deteriorates.
