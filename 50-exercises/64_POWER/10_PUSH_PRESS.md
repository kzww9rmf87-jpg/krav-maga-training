# PUSH PRESS

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Push Press is a loaded whole-body power exercise used to develop rapid force transfer from the lower body through the trunk into an explosive overhead press.

Within CAS, it is selected primarily to improve upper-body propulsive power, lower-to-upper kinetic-chain sequencing, rate of force development and the ability to express force quickly through the arms.

It is not treated as a strict shoulder-strength exercise or as a high-fatigue conditioning movement.

Its training value depends on an efficient leg drive, a rigid force-transfer position, maximal acceleration and a stable overhead finish.

---

# Philosophy

The Push Press teaches the athlete to create force against the ground and transmit that force through the body into an external load.

This sequence resembles the general organization of many combat actions: force begins at the floor, is amplified by the hips and legs, transferred through the trunk and expressed through the upper limbs.

The governing principle is:

> Drive with the legs, transmit through a rigid trunk and accelerate the bar continuously into a stable overhead position.

The exercise loses its primary value when it becomes a slow press, a deep squat-to-press, an uncontrolled backward lean or a fatigue-driven series of repetitions.

# Biomechanical Profile

The Push Press begins with the bar supported in the front-rack position.

The athlete performs a short, controlled dip through the knees and hips while maintaining an upright torso and balanced pressure through the feet.

The dip is immediately reversed into an aggressive leg drive.

Force generated by the lower body is transmitted through a rigid trunk into the bar.

The shoulders and arms continue the acceleration as the bar leaves the rack position.

The movement finishes with the elbows extended and the load stacked over the shoulders, trunk and base of support.

Effective execution requires:

- a vertical dip,
- minimal loss of trunk stiffness,
- rapid transition from dip to drive,
- strong ground reaction force,
- continuous bar acceleration,
- efficient clearance of the face,
- and stable overhead alignment.

The Push Press differs from the Strict Press because the lower body contributes directly to bar acceleration.

It differs from the Push Jerk because the athlete does not deliberately re-bend the knees to receive the bar.

# Loading Profile

Typical Loading Zone

Moderate load relative to the athlete’s pressing strength

Common Practical Range

Approximately 30 to 70 percent of Strict Press one-repetition maximum, depending on objective, technical level and measurement method

Alternative Reference

A load that permits maximal intent, clear acceleration and a stable overhead finish for every repetition

CAS does not require a universal percentage because optimal loading varies with:

- athlete strength,
- pressing proficiency,
- rack mobility,
- limb proportions,
- barbell experience,
- fatigue state,
- and whether velocity data are available.

The selected load must not force the athlete into a slow press or unstable lumbar extension.

# Execution Standard

## Starting Position

Stand with the feet approximately hip- to shoulder-width apart.

Place the bar across the front of the shoulders in a secure rack position.

Use a full-hand or controlled fingertip grip according to mobility and technical competence.

Keep the elbows slightly forward of the bar without forcing an exaggerated front-squat rack.

Brace the trunk.

Maintain full-foot pressure and a neutral head position.

## Dip

Bend the knees and hips slightly while keeping the torso nearly vertical.

Keep the heels in contact with the floor during the initial dip.

Maintain the bar vertically over the mid-foot.

Use a short and controlled depth.

Do not allow the knees to collapse inward or the torso to tip forward.

## Drive

Reverse the dip immediately.

Extend the knees and hips aggressively.

Drive the floor away.

Transfer force through a rigid trunk into the bar.

Allow the heels to rise naturally only as a consequence of forceful extension.

## Press

As the bar leaves the shoulders, continue accelerating it with the arms.

Move the head slightly back to clear the bar path.

Press the bar vertically rather than around the face.

Return the head through once the bar passes forehead height.

## Overhead Finish

Reach full elbow extension.

Stack the bar over the shoulders and mid-foot.

Maintain ribcage and pelvis control.

Avoid excessive lumbar extension.

Demonstrate stable overhead control before lowering the bar.

## Return

Lower the bar under control to the shoulders.

Absorb the returning load with a small coordinated knee bend when appropriate.

Reset posture and breathing before the next repetition.

Each repetition should begin from a stable front-rack position.

# Technical Failure Criteria

CAS considers the repetition technically failed when one or more of the following occur:

- the dip becomes excessively deep,
- the torso tips forward during the dip,
- the heels rise before the drive,
- the knees collapse inward,
- the bar rolls away from the shoulders,
- leg drive and arm action occur out of sequence,
- the bar travels substantially forward,
- the athlete converts the movement into a slow Strict Press,
- the athlete re-bends the knees to receive the bar unintentionally,
- the athlete uses excessive lumbar extension,
- full elbow extension is not achieved,
- the overhead position is unstable,
- the bar is dropped uncontrollably onto the shoulders,
- or the athlete cannot safely return the load.

The set should be terminated when technical errors repeat or bar speed decreases materially.

Three to five minutes for heavier strength-speed work

Forty-eight to seventy-two hours depending on load, total volume, shoulder stress and surrounding training.

Recovery demand increases when the Push Press is combined with heavy pressing, striking volume, clinch work or overhead strength training.

# Progression Model

## Stage 1 — Overhead Position Competency

Demonstrate pain-free shoulder flexion, trunk control and stable lockout.

## Stage 2 — Strict Press Competency

Develop a controlled vertical press and safe bar return.

## Stage 3 — Dip-and-Drive Drill

Learn a short vertical dip and aggressive leg extension without losing trunk position.

## Stage 4 — Light Push Press

Coordinate leg drive and arm action with an empty bar or light load.

## Stage 5 — Standard Push Press

Use moderate loading for low-repetition maximal-intent sets.

## Stage 6 — Strength-Speed Push Press

Increase load while preserving clear acceleration and technical quality.

## Stage 7 — Advanced Monitoring or Variation

Use velocity tracking, contrast pairing, unilateral implements or Push Jerk progression where appropriate.

# Programming Applications

## General Power Development

Use low repetitions and full recovery to develop whole-body propulsive power.

Typical Prescription

Three to six sets of two to five repetitions

## Strength-Speed Development

Use moderately heavy loading with maximal concentric intent.

Typical Prescription

Three to five sets of two to four repetitions

## Speed-Strength Development

Use lighter loading and prioritize acceleration.

Typical Prescription

Three to six sets of two to four repetitions

## Contrast Training

Pair with a biomechanically related ballistic action.

Possible Pairings

Push Press followed by Medicine-Ball Overhead Throw

Push Press followed by Plyometric Push-Up

The pairing must not create excessive shoulder fatigue or compromise safety.

## Combat Athlete General Preparation

Use to develop rapid lower-to-upper force transfer and robust overhead force expression.

## Repeated-Power Development

Use only with carefully controlled density and a strict velocity-loss threshold.

This application is secondary to the primary power objective.

# Combat Transfer

The Push Press has indirect but meaningful transfer to combat performance.

Potential Transfer Qualities

Rapid force production through the entire kinetic chain

Coordination of leg drive with upper-limb force expression

Explosive extension during hand fighting and frame creation

General propulsive strength for clinch actions

Overhead and shoulder-girdle robustness

Ability to maintain trunk stiffness while expressing force through the arms

The exercise does not reproduce punching mechanics directly.

It should therefore be treated as a general power-development tool rather than a sport-specific striking drill.

Its transfer is highest when integrated with technical striking, medicine-ball work, plyometrics and combat practice.

# Substitution Logic

CAS substitutes the Push Press when:

- overhead mobility is insufficient,
- the front rack is painful,
- shoulder or wrist symptoms are present,
- the athlete cannot coordinate the dip and drive,
- equipment or ceiling height is inadequate,
- or the target adaptation can be obtained more safely through another power exercise.

Preferred Substitutions by Limitation

Overhead restriction

Landmine Push Press or Medicine-Ball Chest Pass

Front-rack restriction

Dumbbell Push Press or Kettlebell Push Press

Low technical competency

Medicine-Ball Overhead Throw or Dip-and-Drive Drill

Need for unilateral force transfer

Single-Arm Dumbbell Push Press or Single-Arm Landmine Push Press

Need for release-based ballistic action

Medicine-Ball Overhead Throw

# CAS Selection Logic

CAS may select the Push Press when all of the following are true:

- upper-body or whole-body power is a current objective,
- the athlete can support the implement safely,
- overhead range is adequate,
- the athlete can maintain trunk position,
- sufficient freshness is available,
- equipment and overhead space are appropriate,
- and the session can provide adequate recovery between sets.

CAS should deprioritize or reject the Push Press when:

- the athlete reports relevant pain,
- technical proficiency is insufficient,
- the session objective is maximal strength without a power component,
- shoulder fatigue is already high,
- the required movement velocity cannot be maintained,
- or a lower-complexity exercise offers a better risk-to-benefit ratio.

Selection Priority Increases When

- rapid lower-to-upper force transfer is needed,
- the athlete has adequate pressing strength but limited power expression,
- the program lacks loaded upper-body power work,
- or a moderate-complexity alternative to Olympic lifting is required.

# Decision Summary

The Push Press is a moderate-complexity loaded power exercise used to develop rapid lower-to-upper force transfer and explosive overhead propulsion.

CAS selects it when the athlete has adequate front-rack mobility, overhead control, trunk stiffness and sufficient freshness to move the load with maximal intent.

The exercise should be programmed with low repetitions, moderate loading and full recovery.

Its quality is determined by a vertical dip, aggressive leg drive, efficient sequencing, continuous bar acceleration and a stable stacked finish.

CAS terminates the set when bar velocity declines, the movement becomes a slow press, trunk position deteriorates or overhead control is lost.
