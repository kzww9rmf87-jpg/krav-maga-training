# JUMP SHRUG

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Jump Shrug is a loaded whole-body power exercise used to develop rapid force production, explosive hip and knee extension and high-velocity vertical force expression without requiring the athlete to receive the bar in a front-rack position.

Within CAS, it is selected primarily to improve lower-body power, rate of force development, strength-speed, triple-extension coordination and the ability to accelerate an external load vertically with maximal intent.

It is not treated as a conditioning exercise, a high-repetition fatigue drill or a complete substitute for all Olympic-lifting derivatives.

Its training value depends on a stable start position, aggressive extension through the floor, long arms during propulsion, a close bar path, controlled elevation and a balanced return to the ground.

---

# Philosophy

The Jump Shrug teaches the athlete to apply force rapidly into the ground and transfer that force into vertical acceleration of the body and bar.

The governing principle is:

> Push the floor away, finish tall and let the bar rise because the body accelerated it.

The exercise is valuable because it preserves the high-intent propulsion of a weightlifting derivative while removing the technical demand of turnover and reception.

This makes it useful when the engine requires explosive lower-body loading but the athlete lacks front-rack mobility, clean-receiving skill or sufficient technical readiness for the Hang Power Clean.

It loses its primary value when the athlete bends the elbows early, jumps forward, overextends the lumbar spine, crashes heavily into the floor, uses excessive load or continues after bar speed and movement quality deteriorate.

# Biomechanical Profile

The Jump Shrug normally begins from a hang position with the bar held above or near the knees.

The athlete establishes a controlled hip hinge with the trunk braced, shoulders slightly in front of the bar, arms long and pressure distributed through the whole foot.

The propulsive phase begins by driving forcefully against the ground through coordinated extension of the hips and knees.

The ankles complete the action as the athlete reaches a tall position and may briefly leave the ground.

The bar remains close to the thighs and torso and travels predominantly vertically.

The shoulders elevate naturally near the completion of lower-body extension, but the shrug does not initiate the movement.

The elbows remain extended throughout the primary acceleration phase.

After the body and bar reach peak elevation, the athlete returns to the ground with soft knees and balanced foot pressure, then lowers the bar under control into the next repetition or resets.

Effective execution requires:

- a stable hang position,
- posterior-chain pre-tension,
- full-foot pressure before propulsion,
- aggressive hip and knee extension,
- coordinated ankle extension,
- long arms during acceleration,
- a close and predominantly vertical bar path,
- natural shoulder elevation after extension,
- balanced take-off,
- and controlled landing mechanics.

The Jump Shrug differs from the Hang High Pull because the elbows do not drive high and outside to continue pulling the bar upward.

It differs from the Hang Power Clean because there is no turnover or receiving phase.

It differs from a loaded jump squat because the bar is held in the hands rather than supported across the shoulders and the exercise includes a vertical shrug after lower-body extension.

# Loading Profile

Light to moderate relative load

Primary Loading Objective

Maximize external-load acceleration while preserving complete and coordinated extension

Suggested Loading Reference

Approximately 20 to 50 percent of a technically established clean one-repetition maximum when that reference is available

Alternative Loading Reference

A load the athlete can accelerate explosively for every prescribed repetition without early arm bend, forward displacement or hard landing

CAS does not require a clean one-repetition maximum to prescribe the exercise.

Technical quality and velocity preservation take priority over percentage-based loading.

# Execution Standard

1. Stand with the bar held at arm's length and the feet approximately hip-width apart.
2. Brace the trunk and keep the shoulders set without rigidly retracting the scapulae.
3. Hinge at the hips and allow the knees to flex until the bar reaches the selected hang position above or near the knees.
4. Keep the bar close, the arms long and pressure through the whole foot.
5. Drive forcefully through the floor and extend the hips and knees as rapidly as possible.
6. Complete the extension through the ankles and allow the feet to leave the ground only if it occurs naturally.
7. Let the shoulders shrug upward after lower-body extension while the elbows remain straight.
8. Keep the bar travelling close to the body and predominantly vertically.
9. Return to the floor with soft knees and balanced foot pressure.
10. Lower the bar under control and reset the start position before the next repetition when required.

Every repetition must begin from a reproducible hang position.

# Technical Failure Criteria

The repetition is considered technically unsuccessful when one or more of the following occurs:

- early elbow flexion,
- the athlete pulls primarily with the arms,
- incomplete hip or knee extension,
- excessive lumbar extension at the finish,
- the bar moves significantly away from the body,
- the athlete jumps forward or backward,
- the feet spread excessively during landing,
- the athlete lands stiff-legged,
- the landing is loud, unstable or uncontrolled,
- the bar strikes the thighs aggressively rather than remaining close,
- grip failure alters the bar path,
- or repetition velocity declines substantially.

The set should end when technical failure becomes likely, not after it becomes repeated.

# Contraindications and Restrictions

Avoid or restrict when the athlete presents:

- acute lower-back pain,
- acute hip, knee or ankle injury,
- inability to land safely,
- poor balance under loaded jumping conditions,
- unresolved Achilles tendon or patellar tendon irritation aggravated by jumping,
- inability to maintain a neutral and braced trunk during the hang,
- severe grip limitation that compromises bar control,
- uncontrolled cardiovascular or neurological conditions incompatible with explosive lifting,
- or insufficient technical supervision for the athlete's current skill level.

Use caution when cumulative lower-body plyometric volume is already high.

Medical restrictions override performance objectives.

# Progression Model

Stage 1

Demonstrate a stable hang position and controlled hip hinge.

Stage 2

Perform explosive extension with long arms and no jump.

Stage 3

Add coordinated ankle extension and natural heel rise.

Stage 4

Allow controlled foot clearance with balanced landing.

Stage 5

Increase load gradually while preserving bar speed and vertical displacement.

Stage 6

Introduce velocity thresholds, contrast methods or progression to more complex weightlifting derivatives.

The athlete progresses only when every repetition remains fast, balanced and technically repeatable.

# Substitution Logic

Substitute the Jump Shrug with the Hang High Pull when:

- a more complete vertical pulling action is desired,
- the athlete can preserve delayed arm action,
- and elbow or shoulder tolerance is adequate.

Substitute it with the Hang Power Clean when:

- the athlete has sufficient technical skill,
- front-rack mobility is adequate,
- and force reception is part of the objective.

Substitute it with a Trap-Bar Jump when:

- simpler bar-path mechanics are preferred,
- the athlete has limited Olympic-lifting experience,
- or the engine prioritizes loaded jumping over weightlifting transfer.

Substitute it with an unloaded jump when:

- external loading is contraindicated,
- landing quality is still being developed,
- or maximal movement velocity is the dominant priority.

Substitute it with a clean pull or mid-thigh pull when:

- landing impact must be minimized,
- the athlete should remain grounded,
- or heavier strength-speed loading is required.

---

# CAS Selection Logic

Select the Jump Shrug when:

- the primary objective is lower-body power or strength-speed,
- the athlete can safely hinge, jump and land,
- a loaded triple-extension exercise is appropriate,
- maximal acceleration is required,
- front-rack reception is unnecessary or unsuitable,
- and adequate coaching or prior technical competency is present.

Prioritize the Jump Shrug over the Hang Power Clean when:

- clean-receiving skill is insufficient,
- front-rack mobility is restricted,
- wrist, elbow or shoulder tolerance limits the catch,
- the session seeks propulsion without reception,
- or technical complexity must be reduced.

Prioritize the Jump Shrug over the Hang High Pull when:

- early arm pulling is a recurrent problem,
- the engine wants to emphasize lower-body extension rather than upper-body continuation,
- or shoulder and elbow loading should be minimized.

Do not select the Jump Shrug when:

- the athlete cannot land safely,
- active pain is aggravated by loaded jumping,
- fatigue prevents maximal intent,
- the environment does not allow safe bar control,
- or a simpler regression better satisfies the objective.

# Evidence Classification

Evidence Level

Moderate

Rationale

Loaded jump and weightlifting-derivative research supports the use of explosive triple-extension exercises for lower-body power and rate-of-force-development qualities.

Direct evidence isolating the Jump Shrug from closely related derivatives is more limited than evidence for the broader categories of loaded jumps, pulls and Olympic-lifting derivatives.

CAS therefore treats the exercise as a valid loaded-power option when its biomechanical demands, athlete skill and programming context match the intended adaptation.

---

# Decision Summary

The Jump Shrug is a loaded explosive extension exercise that develops lower-body power, strength-speed and rapid vertical force production without requiring bar turnover or front-rack reception.

It is especially useful as an intermediate technical option between simple loaded jumps and more complex Olympic-lifting derivatives.

Within CAS, it should be selected when the athlete can safely hinge, accelerate and land, when maximal movement intent can be preserved and when the program benefits from loaded triple extension with reduced receiving complexity.

It should be performed early in the session, with low repetition counts, adequate rest and immediate termination when speed, bar path or landing quality deteriorates.
