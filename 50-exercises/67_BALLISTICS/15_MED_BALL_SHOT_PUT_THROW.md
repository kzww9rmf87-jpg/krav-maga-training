# Purpose

The Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw develops unilateral horizontal ballistic power through coordinated force transfer from the lower body, trunk and pressing arm.

Within CAS, it is used when the athlete must improve the ability to project force rapidly forward from an asymmetrical stance.

The exercise is particularly useful for developing:

- unilateral horizontal power,
- punch-supportive force projection,
- staggered-stance explosiveness,
- hip-to-hand sequencing,
- side-to-side assessment,
- and rapid upper-body force expression.

It must not be selected merely because it resembles a punch.

Its value depends on whether the athlete can produce a fast, direct throw through coordinated lower-body and trunk contribution without converting the movement into a slow press.

# Movement Description

The athlete holds a medicine ball near one shoulder or chest with one hand as the primary projecting arm.

From a staggered or split stance, the athlete drives through the floor, rotates or shifts through the hips and trunk, and projects the ball forward in a shot-put-like action.

The ball is released decisively toward the target.

The athlete finishes balanced and resets before the next repetition.

The throw should be generated through the whole body rather than by the arm alone.

---

# Starting Position

The athlete should begin with:

- the body positioned side-on or slightly angled to the target,
- the medicine ball held near the rear shoulder or chest,
- the projecting hand behind or under the ball,
- the opposite hand lightly supporting the ball if required,
- feet in a staggered stance,
- knees and hips slightly flexed,
- ribs stacked over the pelvis,
- and eyes focused on the target.

The stance should permit force transfer without excessive rotation or loss of balance.

---

# Execution

1. Hold the medicine ball securely near the throwing shoulder.
2. Establish a stable staggered stance.
3. Load the rear leg and hip.
4. Brace the trunk.
5. Drive through the floor.
6. Transfer force through the hips and trunk.
7. Project the throwing shoulder forward.
8. Extend the arm explosively.
9. Release the ball directly toward the target.
10. Finish in balance.
11. Retrieve or receive the ball safely.
12. Reset fully before the next repetition.

---

# Key Technical Cues

- Drive from the rear leg.
- Throw through the target.
- Sequence hip, trunk and arm.
- Keep the ball close before release.
- Do not push slowly.
- Keep the vector forward.
- Finish balanced.
- Reset before repeating.

# Force Vector

Primary vector:

- horizontal and forward.

The ball should travel:

- directly toward the target,
- with minimal unintended upward arc,
- without excessive lateral drift,
- and without forcing the athlete out of balance.

A consistent vector is essential for power assessment and training quality.

---

# Rotational and Translational Contribution

The Shot-Put Throw may emphasize:

- forward translation,
- transverse-plane rotation,
- or a combination of both.

The selected emphasis depends on stance and objective.

## Translation-Dominant Variation

Primary characteristics:

- stronger rear-to-front weight transfer,
- more direct forward projection,
- lower rotational amplitude.

## Rotation-Dominant Variation

Primary characteristics:

- greater hip and trunk turn,
- stronger transverse-plane contribution,
- higher similarity to rotational striking mechanics.

CAS should define the intended strategy before prescription.

---

# Lower-Body Contribution

Lower-body contribution is moderate to high.

The athlete should:

- push through the rear foot,
- transfer force through the hips,
- maintain stable front-side support,
- and avoid collapsing into the front knee.

The exercise should not become a purely arm-dominant throw.

---

# Technical Complexity

Moderate

The exercise requires coordination between:

- stance,
- rear-leg drive,
- hip and trunk sequencing,
- unilateral arm projection,
- and balance after release.

Complexity increases with:

- step-in execution,
- reactive catching,
- deeper rotational loading,
- or single-arm ball support.

---

# Prerequisites

The athlete should demonstrate:

- pain-free unilateral pressing,
- pain-free trunk rotation,
- stable staggered stance,
- adequate shoulder and elbow tolerance,
- safe medicine-ball handling,
- and ability to finish in balance.

---

# Eligibility Criteria

The Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw is eligible when:

- unilateral horizontal power is a target,
- side-specific force production is relevant,
- the athlete can coordinate lower-body and upper-body force transfer,
- the throwing area is safe,
- the ball mass preserves velocity,
- and both sides can be trained safely.

---

# Ineligibility Criteria

The exercise is not eligible when the athlete presents:

- acute shoulder pain,
- elbow pain,
- wrist pain,
- low-back pain,
- rib pain,
- hip pain,
- unstable staggered stance,
- inability to release the ball safely,
- unsafe wall or open-space setup,
- or severe fatigue that suppresses velocity.

---

# Relative Contraindications

Use caution when the athlete has:

- anterior shoulder irritation,
- elbow tendinopathy,
- thoracic stiffness,
- lumbar rotation sensitivity,
- hip-rotation restriction,
- or very high striking volume.

The engine should reduce ball mass, rotational amplitude or total volume when needed.

---

# Absolute Contraindications

Do not prescribe the exercise when there is:

- acute upper-limb injury,
- acute spinal injury,
- acute rib injury,
- inability to stand safely in a staggered position,
- or an unsuitable throwing environment.

---

# Common Errors

## Arm-Dominant Throw

The athlete pushes the ball using mainly the arm.

Risk:

- reduced whole-body power,
- excessive shoulder stress,
- poor transfer.

Correction:

- cue rear-leg drive and hip-to-hand sequencing.

## Using a Ball That Is Too Heavy

The throw becomes slow and press-like.

Risk:

- reduced ballistic effect,
- poor release speed.

Correction:

- reduce medicine-ball mass.

## Excessive Lumbar Rotation

The athlete rotates mainly through the lower back.

Risk:

- increased spinal stress,
- reduced hip contribution.

Correction:

- rotate through the hips and thoracic region.

## Front-Knee Collapse

The front knee moves inward or the athlete loses front-side support.

Risk:

- reduced force transfer,
- lower-body instability.

Correction:

- reduce stance length or load and stabilize the front leg.

## Inconsistent Release Direction

The ball travels upward, sideways or across the body.

Risk:

- poor standardization,
- unsafe rebound.

Correction:

- use a clear target and direct release path.

## Excessive Countermovement

The athlete winds up too far.

Risk:

- slower action,
- loss of posture,
- reduced specificity.

Correction:

- use a short, controlled load.

## Losing Balance After Release

The athlete falls forward or rotates excessively.

Risk:

- poor force transfer,
- unsafe finish.

Correction:

- reduce ball mass, stance length or rotational amplitude.

## Opposite Hand Dominance

The support hand contributes too much to the throw.

Risk:

- reduced unilateral stimulus,
- inaccurate side-to-side assessment.

Correction:

- use only light support before unilateral release.

# Coaching Priorities

1. Establish a stable staggered stance.
2. Load the rear leg.
3. Keep the ball close.
4. Sequence rear leg, hip, trunk and arm.
5. Accelerate through release.
6. Maintain a direct horizontal vector.
7. Finish on a stable front side.
8. Train both sides.
9. Reset fully.
10. Stop before velocity declines.

---

# Prescription Variables

## Repetitions

Typical range:

- 2 to 5 repetitions per side for maximal power,
- 4 to 8 repetitions per side for repeated ballistic effort.

## Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 6 sets.

## Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 4 minutes.

## Ball Mass

Typical range:

- light to moderate medicine ball,
- generally 1 to 4 kilograms depending on athlete and variation.

The selected mass must preserve unilateral release speed.

## Stance

Possible options:

- staggered stance,
- split stance,
- step-in stance,
- half-kneeling stance.

---

# Maximum-Power Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 6 sets,
- 2 to 5 repetitions per side,
- maximal intent,
- 2 to 4 minutes rest,
- complete reset between repetitions.

---

# Strength-Speed Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 5 sets,
- 3 to 6 repetitions per side,
- moderate ball mass,
- 90 seconds to 3 minutes rest,
- no visible velocity loss.

---

# Repeated-Effort Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 4 sets,
- 5 to 8 repetitions per side,
- light ball,
- controlled rhythm,
- no technical breakdown.

# Standing Shot-Put Throw

Primary role:

- basic unilateral horizontal power.

Primary characteristics:

- staggered stance,
- moderate lower-body contribution,
- clear side-to-side comparison.

This is the default variation for most athletes.

---

# Half-Kneeling Shot-Put Throw

Primary benefits:

- reduced lower-body contribution,
- greater trunk and shoulder emphasis,
- improved pelvic control,
- useful technical regression.

---

# Step-In Shot-Put Throw

Primary benefits:

- greater forward momentum,
- higher lower-body contribution,
- stronger whole-body sequencing,
- greater projection output.

This variation is reserved for athletes with established basic mechanics.

---

# Split-Stance Shot-Put Throw

Primary benefits:

- stronger front-side stabilization,
- greater anti-rotation demand,
- closer resemblance to combat stance.

---

# Reactive Shot-Put Throw

The athlete catches or receives the ball and immediately redirects it.

Primary benefits:

- reactive unilateral power,
- rapid force reversal,
- advanced shoulder and trunk coordination.

This variation requires safe rebound behavior and excellent technical control.

---

# Progressions

Progressions include:

1. improved release speed,
2. greater projection distance,
3. slightly greater ball mass,
4. greater rear-leg contribution,
5. increased but controlled trunk rotation,
6. step-in execution,
7. reactive execution,
8. more demanding stance,
9. single-arm unsupported hold,
10. repeated high-quality throws.

---

# Regressions

Regressions include:

- lighter ball,
- half-kneeling variation,
- reduced stance length,
- reduced rotational amplitude,
- shorter throwing distance,
- lower repetition count,
- submaximal technical throws,
- non-rebounding ball.

---

# Recommended Progression Sequence

1. Light half-kneeling Shot-Put Throw.
2. Standing staggered-stance throw.
3. Split-stance Shot-Put Throw.
4. Greater projection distance.
5. Slightly heavier ball.
6. Step-In Shot-Put Throw.
7. Reactive Shot-Put Throw.
8. Advanced unsupported unilateral variation.

# Recovery Cost

Recovery cost is generally low to moderate.

It increases with:

- heavier balls,
- excessive repetition count,
- reactive catching,
- large rotational amplitude,
- and concurrent pressing or striking volume.

---

# Session Placement

The Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw is usually placed:

- after the warm-up,
- after movement preparation,
- before heavy pressing,
- before hypertrophy work,
- and before high-fatigue conditioning.

It may be paired with:

- unilateral presses,
- bench press,
- or striking drills in carefully designed contrast sequences.

It should not be placed after severe upper-body or trunk fatigue when power quality is the objective.

---

# Weekly Placement

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 3 times per week.

Higher frequency may be appropriate when:

- volume is low,
- both sides are balanced,
- ball mass is light,
- and shoulder and trunk readiness remain high.

The exercise may support:

# Combat Transfer

The Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw supports combat performance by improving:

- unilateral forward projection,
- staggered-stance power,
- rear-leg-to-hand force transfer,
- rapid arm extension,
- and side-specific explosive strength.

Its transfer is strongest for actions involving:

- straight punches,
- unilateral pushing,
- posting,
- and directional force from a combat stance.

It does not replace technical striking practice.

# Substitution Logic

## Substitute Chest Pass When

- bilateral horizontal power is the primary objective,
- lower technical complexity is preferred,
- or side-specific loading is unnecessary.

## Substitute Rotational Throw When

- transverse-plane power is the main target,
- hook or turning-force transfer is required.

## Substitute Scoop Toss When

- greater lower-body contribution is required,
- diagonal or upward projection is preferred.

## Substitute Landmine Push Press When

- the athlete should retain control of the load,
- unilateral strength-speed is preferred,
- or throwing space is unavailable.

## Substitute Cable Punch When

- controlled unilateral resistance is required,
- ballistic release is inappropriate,
- or precise load progression is necessary.

# Progression Criteria

The athlete may progress when:

- rear-leg-to-hand sequencing remains consistent,
- the force vector remains direct,
- the trunk stays controlled,
- the athlete finishes in balance,
- both sides perform similarly,
- no pain occurs,
- and there is no meaningful velocity loss.

---

# Regression Criteria

The exercise should be regressed when:

- the throw becomes arm-dominant,
- the ball moves slowly,
- lumbar rotation dominates,
- release direction becomes inconsistent,
- front-side stability deteriorates,
- pain appears,
- or fatigue reduces output.

- low-back pain,

- staggered-stance stability,
- trunk-rotation tolerance,

# Safety Rules

- Use an appropriate medicine ball.
- Verify the wall or open-space setup.
- Keep the throwing lane clear.
- Use a safe rebound distance.
- Train both sides.
- Do not force lumbar rotation.
- Maintain front-side balance.
- Stop before technical failure.
- Retrieve the ball under control.
- Terminate immediately if sharp pain, dizziness, numbness or sudden weakness occurs.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine may select the Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw when:

- unilateral horizontal ballistic power is the intended adaptation,
- staggered-stance force transfer is relevant,
- the athlete can coordinate rear-leg, hip, trunk and arm sequencing,
- the ball mass preserves release speed,
- both sides can be trained safely,
- and total pressing and striking volume remains recoverable.

The engine must avoid selecting it when:

- bilateral horizontal power is the actual target,
- rotational control is insufficient,
- the athlete cannot stabilize the front side,
- or the selected ball mass suppresses speed and turns the throw into a press.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When the Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the targeted unilateral horizontal vector,
- the intended power adaptation,
- the selected stance,
- the ball mass,
- the prescribed repetitions per side,
- the expected limiting factor,
- the interaction with pressing and striking volume,
- relevant shoulder, trunk, hip or balance constraints,
- and the progression criterion.

Example:

> Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw selected to develop unilateral horizontal power and rear-leg-to-hand sequencing relevant to straight strikes. A light medicine ball is used to preserve maximal release speed. Low-repetition sets are performed on both sides before pressing work, with the set terminated at the first clear decline in projection velocity or front-side stability.

---

# Summary

The Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw is a moderate-complexity unilateral ballistic exercise that develops forward force projection from a staggered stance.

Its primary value lies in improving:

- unilateral horizontal power,
- punch-supportive force transfer,
- rear-leg-to-hand sequencing,
- side-specific explosive strength,
- and rapid arm extension.

Within CAS, it is appropriate when the athlete needs asymmetrical forward ballistic power and can maintain stable stance, trunk control and direct release mechanics.

It must be programmed with a ball light enough to preserve speed and with equal attention to both sides.
