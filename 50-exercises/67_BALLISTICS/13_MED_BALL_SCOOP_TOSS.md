# Purpose

The Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss develops hip-driven ballistic power through rapid force transfer from the lower body into the trunk, shoulders and arms.

Within CAS, it is used when the athlete must improve the ability to project force diagonally, upward or rotationally through coordinated whole-body sequencing.

The exercise is particularly useful for developing:

- hip extension power,
- rotational or diagonal projection,
- lower-to-upper-body force transfer,
- unilateral explosive strength,
- and high-velocity trunk-to-arm sequencing.

It must not be selected merely because it resembles a rotational throw.

Its value depends on whether the intended adaptation requires greater lower-body contribution and a scoop-like projection path.

# Movement Description

The athlete holds a medicine ball low near the hip or between the legs, depending on the selected variation.

From a loaded position, the athlete rapidly extends through the hips and knees while rotating or driving diagonally toward the target.

The ball is projected with a scoop-like path rather than pushed from the chest.

The movement should be driven by the lower body and trunk, with the arms guiding and releasing the implement.

The athlete finishes balanced and resets before the next repetition.

---

# Starting Position

The athlete should begin with:

- feet in a stable athletic stance,
- the medicine ball held low near the hip or between the legs,
- knees and hips slightly flexed,
- ribs stacked over the pelvis,
- shoulders relaxed but ready,
- trunk braced,
- and eyes directed toward the target area.

For rotational variations, the body should be positioned side-on or slightly angled to the target.

---

# Execution

1. Hold the medicine ball securely.
2. Establish a stable stance.
3. Load the hips and knees.
4. Rotate or shift away from the target if required.
5. Drive through the floor.
6. Extend the hips and knees rapidly.
7. Transfer force through the trunk.
8. Accelerate the ball in a scoop-like path.
9. Release toward the target.
10. Finish tall and balanced.
11. Retrieve or receive the ball safely.
12. Reset fully before the next repetition.

---

# Key Technical Cues

- Drive from the hips.
- Scoop through the target.
- Let the legs start the throw.
- Keep the ball close during the load.
- Accelerate through release.
- Do not arm the toss.
- Finish tall and balanced.
- Reset before repeating.

# Force Vector

The force vector may be:

- diagonal,
- rotational,
- upward,
- or upward and forward.

The exact vector depends on the selected variation.

The force path should remain consistent across repetitions.

---

# Rotational Sequencing

For rotational Scoop Toss variations, the preferred sequence is:

1. pressure into the floor,
2. hip extension and rotation,
3. pelvic rotation,
4. trunk rotation,
5. shoulder acceleration,
6. ball release.

The athlete should not begin the movement with the arms.

---

# Lower-Body Contribution

The Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss uses high lower-body contribution.

The athlete should generate force through:

- ankle extension,
- knee extension,
- hip extension,
- hip rotation,
- trunk transfer,
- and final release.

This distinguishes the Scoop Toss from more arm-dominant or chest-level throws.

---

# Technical Complexity

Moderate

The exercise requires coordinated integration of:

- lower-body loading,
- hip extension,
- rotation or diagonal projection,
- trunk control,
- and release timing.

Complexity increases with:

- step-behind entries,
- larger rotational amplitudes,
- unilateral stance,
- or reactive execution.

---

# Prerequisites

The athlete should demonstrate:

- pain-free hip and trunk rotation,
- competent squat or hinge mechanics,
- stable stance,
- adequate shoulder and elbow tolerance,
- safe medicine-ball handling,
- and the ability to finish in balance.

---

# Eligibility Criteria

The Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss is eligible when:

- hip-driven ballistic power is a target,
- diagonal or rotational projection is relevant,
- the athlete can sequence the lower and upper body,
- the throwing area is safe,
- the ball mass preserves speed,
- and technical quality remains high.

---

# Ineligibility Criteria

The exercise is not eligible when the athlete presents:

- acute low-back pain,
- hip pain,
- knee pain,
- shoulder pain,
- elbow pain,
- uncontrolled rotation,
- poor balance,
- inability to hinge or squat safely,
- unsafe wall or open-space setup,
- or severe fatigue that suppresses velocity.

---

# Relative Contraindications

Use caution when the athlete has:

- lumbar rotation sensitivity,
- thoracic stiffness,
- hip internal-rotation restriction,
- knee irritation,
- anterior shoulder discomfort,
- or high lower-body power volume.

The engine should reduce ball mass, movement depth, rotational amplitude or total volume when necessary.

---

# Absolute Contraindications

Do not prescribe the exercise when there is:

- acute spinal injury,
- acute hip injury,
- acute knee injury,
- acute upper-limb injury,
- inability to rotate or extend safely,
- or an unsuitable throwing environment.

# Common Errors

## Arm-Dominant Toss

The athlete lifts and throws primarily with the arms.

Risk:

- reduced whole-body power,
- poor hip contribution,
- excessive shoulder stress.

Correction:

- cue the legs and hips to initiate the movement.

## Excessive Lumbar Extension

The athlete arches the lower back during projection.

Risk:

- increased spinal stress,
- poor force transfer.

Correction:

- maintain rib control and finish through the hips.

## Excessive Lumbar Rotation

The athlete rotates primarily through the lower back.

Risk:

- increased spinal stress,
- reduced hip contribution.

Correction:

- rotate through the hips and thoracic spine.

## Ball Too Far From the Body

The athlete allows the medicine ball to drift away during the load.

Risk:

- slower acceleration,
- greater shoulder demand,
- reduced control.

Correction:

- keep the ball close before projection.

## Using a Ball That Is Too Heavy

The athlete moves slowly and cannot accelerate through release.

Risk:

- reduced ballistic effect,
- poor sequencing.

Correction:

- reduce medicine-ball mass.

## Deep and Slow Countermovement

The athlete descends too far before throwing.

Risk:

- slower movement,
- altered exercise objective.

Correction:

- use a short, rapid loading phase.

## Inconsistent Release Direction

The ball travels unpredictably.

Risk:

- poor standardization,
- unsafe rebound.

Correction:

- define a clear target and release path.

## Loss of Balance

The athlete steps or falls uncontrollably after release.

Risk:

- poor force transfer,
- unsafe finish.

Correction:

- reduce speed, range or ball mass.

# Coaching Priorities

1. Establish a stable stance.
2. Use a short lower-body load.
3. Initiate from the hips.
4. Maintain rib and pelvic control.
5. Sequence lower body before upper body.
6. Accelerate through release.
7. Preserve the intended diagonal or rotational vector.
8. Finish in balance.
9. Train both sides when applicable.
10. Stop before velocity declines.

---

# Prescription Variables

## Repetitions

Typical range:

- 2 to 5 repetitions per side for maximal power,
- 4 to 8 repetitions per side for repeated ballistic effort.

## Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 6 sets.

## Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 4 minutes.

## Ball Mass

Typical range:

- light to moderate medicine ball,
- generally 2 to 6 kilograms depending on athlete and variation.

The selected mass must preserve speed and sequencing.

## Throwing Direction

Options include:

- diagonal upward,
- rotational horizontal,
- upward and forward,
- backward diagonal.

---

# Maximum-Power Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 6 sets,
- 2 to 5 repetitions per side,
- maximal intent,
- 2 to 4 minutes rest,
- full reset between repetitions.

---

# Strength-Speed Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 5 sets,
- 3 to 6 repetitions per side,
- moderate ball mass,
- 90 seconds to 3 minutes rest,
- no visible velocity loss.

---

# Repeated-Effort Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 4 sets,
- 5 to 8 repetitions per side,
- lighter ball,
- controlled rhythm,
- no technical breakdown.

- hip extension power,
- whole-body projection,

---

# Lateral Scoop Toss

The athlete projects the ball laterally or diagonally toward a wall.

Primary benefits:

- transverse-plane power,
- hip rotation,
- unilateral force transfer.

---

# Tall-Kneeling Scoop Toss

Primary benefits:

- reduced lower-body contribution,
- greater trunk emphasis,

---

# Step-Behind Scoop Toss

Primary benefits:

- greater momentum,
- increased lower-body contribution,
- advanced sequencing,
- greater projection output.

# Progressions

Progressions include:

1. improved release speed,
2. greater projection distance,
3. slightly greater ball mass,
4. greater lower-body contribution,
5. larger but controlled hip rotation,
6. standing from kneeling,
7. step-behind execution,
8. reactive execution,
9. greater stance complexity,
10. repeated high-quality throws.

---

# Regressions

Regressions include:

- lighter ball,
- reduced movement depth,
- reduced rotational amplitude,
- tall-kneeling variation,
- shorter throwing distance,
- lower repetition count,
- submaximal technical throws,
- non-rebounding ball.

---

# Recommended Progression Sequence

1. Light tall-kneeling Scoop Toss.
2. Standing forward Scoop Toss.
3. Standing rotational Scoop Toss.
4. Greater projection distance.
5. Slightly heavier ball.
6. Step-behind Scoop Toss.
7. Reactive Scoop Toss.
8. Advanced unilateral or single-leg variation.

---

# Session Placement

The Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss is usually placed:

- after the warm-up,
- after hip and trunk movement preparation,
- before heavy strength work,
- before hypertrophy work,
- and before high-fatigue conditioning.

It may be paired with:

- squats,
- deadlifts,
- rotational strength work,
- or striking practice in carefully designed contrast sequences.

It should not be placed after severe lower-body or trunk fatigue when power quality is the objective.

---

# Weekly Placement

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 3 times per week.

Higher frequency may be appropriate when:

- volume is low,
- both sides are balanced,
- ball mass is light,
- and hip and trunk readiness remain high.

# Substitution Logic

## Substitute Rotational Throw When

- transverse-plane power is the primary objective,
- a more horizontal force vector is required,
- or lower-body contribution should be reduced.

## Substitute Overhead Throw When

- vertical or forward whole-body extension is the target,
- rotational demand is unnecessary.

## Substitute Shot-Put Throw When

- unilateral horizontal projection is the priority,
- punch-like asymmetry is required.

## Substitute Jump Shrug When

- hip extension power is required without medicine-ball release,
- or throwing space is unavailable.

## Substitute Cable Rotation When

- controlled rotational strength is needed,
- ballistic release is inappropriate,
- or precise loading is required.

# Progression Criteria

The athlete may progress when:

- lower-body initiation remains consistent,
- the force vector remains controlled,
- the trunk stays stable,
- the athlete finishes in balance,
- both sides perform similarly,
- no pain occurs,
- and there is no meaningful velocity loss.

---

# Regression Criteria

The exercise should be regressed when:

- the athlete arms the toss,
- lumbar extension or rotation dominates,
- the ball moves slowly,
- release direction becomes inconsistent,
- balance deteriorates,
- pain appears,
- or fatigue reduces output.

# Safety Rules

- Use an appropriate medicine ball.
- Verify the wall or open-space setup.
- Keep the throwing lane clear.
- Use a safe rebound distance.
- Train both sides when appropriate.
- Do not force lumbar rotation or extension.
- Stop before technical failure.
- Retrieve the ball under control.
- Do not throw toward another athlete.
- Terminate immediately if sharp pain, dizziness, numbness or sudden weakness occurs.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine may select the Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss when:

- hip-driven diagonal or rotational ballistic power is the intended adaptation,
- lower-to-upper-body sequencing is required,
- the athlete can extend and rotate safely,
- the ball mass preserves speed,
- both sides can be trained with quality when applicable,
- and total lower-body power and striking volume remains recoverable.

The engine must avoid selecting it when:

- a purely horizontal or vertical vector is the actual target,
- the athlete cannot control lumbar motion,
- hip or knee readiness is insufficient,
- or the selected ball mass suppresses velocity and sequencing.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When the Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the targeted diagonal or rotational force vector,
- the intended hip-driven power adaptation,
- the selected stance,
- the ball mass,
- the prescribed repetitions per side,
- the expected limiting factor,
- the interaction with lower-body power, striking and grappling,
- relevant spinal, hip, knee or shoulder constraints,
- and the progression criterion.

Example:

> Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss selected to develop hip-driven diagonal power and lower-to-upper-body sequencing relevant to rotational strikes and takedowns. A moderate medicine ball is used to preserve acceleration and prevent arm-dominant compensation. Low-repetition sets are placed before strength work, with the set terminated at the first clear decline in release velocity or balance.

---

# Summary

The Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss is a moderate-complexity ballistic exercise that develops hip-driven diagonal or rotational force projection.

Its primary value lies in improving:

- hip extension power,
- rotational sequencing,
- lower-to-upper-body force transfer,
- unilateral explosive strength,
- and whole-body acceleration.

Within CAS, it is appropriate when the athlete needs greater lower-body contribution than a chest-level throw and can maintain controlled hip, trunk and release mechanics.

It must be programmed with a ball light enough to preserve speed and with a volume low enough to maintain technical quality.
