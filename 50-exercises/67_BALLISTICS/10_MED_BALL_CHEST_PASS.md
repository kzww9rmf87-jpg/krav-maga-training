# Purpose

The Medicine-Ball Chest Pass develops bilateral horizontal upper-body power.

Within CAS, it is used when the athlete must improve the ability to project force rapidly forward through the upper body while maintaining whole-body alignment and continuous acceleration through release.

The exercise is particularly useful for developing:

- horizontal force projection,
- rapid upper-body force expression,
- bilateral pressing power,
- trunk-to-arm force transfer,
- and punch-supportive ballistic intent.

It must not be selected merely because it resembles a pressing action.

Its value depends on whether bilateral horizontal projection is the intended adaptation and whether the athlete can preserve speed, direction and balance.

# Movement Description

The athlete holds a medicine ball at chest level with both hands.

From a stable stance or selected body position, the athlete rapidly extends the arms and projects the ball forward.

The ball is released decisively rather than guided.

Depending on the variation, the athlete may use:

- minimal lower-body contribution,
- moderate lower-body drive,
- or a coordinated step.

The repetition ends when the ball has been released and the athlete regains a balanced position.

---

# Starting Position

The athlete should begin with:

- the medicine ball held close to the chest,
- both hands placed symmetrically,
- elbows slightly below or behind the ball,
- shoulders stable,
- ribs stacked over the pelvis,
- trunk braced,
- feet positioned according to the selected stance,
- and eyes directed toward the target.

The distance from the wall or partner must allow safe release and reception.

---

# Execution

1. Hold the medicine ball securely at chest level.
2. Establish a stable stance.
3. Brace the trunk.
4. Draw the ball slightly toward the chest if the variation permits.
5. Initiate the throw with rapid force production.
6. Extend the arms explosively.
7. Accelerate through the full movement.
8. Release the ball directly toward the target.
9. Finish with the trunk and shoulders controlled.
10. Regain balance.
11. Retrieve or receive the ball safely.
12. Reset fully before the next repetition.

---

# Key Technical Cues

- Throw through the target.
- Accelerate through release.
- Keep the ribs stacked.
- Use the whole body when prescribed.
- Do not guide the ball.
- Keep the release straight.
- Finish in balance.
- Reset before the next repetition.

# Force Vector

Primary vector:

- horizontal.

The throw should travel:

- directly forward,
- with minimal unintended upward arc,
- without lateral deviation,
- and without loss of trunk control.

A consistent vector is essential for valid comparison across repetitions.

---

# Lower-Body Contribution

## Minimal Lower-Body Contribution

Typical variations:

- seated Chest Pass,
- tall-kneeling Chest Pass,
- half-kneeling Chest Pass.

Primary use:

- isolate upper-body contribution,
- reduce leg assistance,
- simplify force-source analysis.

## Moderate Lower-Body Contribution

Typical variation:

- standing Chest Pass.

Primary use:

- integrate stance,
- maintain trunk involvement,
- preserve simple execution.

## High Lower-Body Contribution

Typical variation:

- step-in Chest Pass.

Primary use:

- increase whole-body sequencing,
- develop forward force transfer,
- integrate locomotor intent.

---

# Technical Complexity

Low

The exercise is simple to teach and easy to standardize.

However, technical quality still depends on:

- stable starting position,
- appropriate medicine-ball mass,
- direct release,
- continuous acceleration,
- and controlled finish.

The exercise becomes more complex when a step or reactive catch is added.

---

# Prerequisites

The athlete should demonstrate:

- pain-free pressing,
- pain-free shoulder horizontal adduction,
- adequate elbow tolerance,
- stable trunk control,
- safe medicine-ball handling,
- and ability to finish in balance.

No advanced strength prerequisite is required.

---

# Eligibility Criteria

The Medicine-Ball Chest Pass is eligible when:

- bilateral horizontal power is a target,
- upper-body ballistic work is appropriate,
- the athlete can throw safely,
- the target area is clear,
- shoulder and elbow readiness are adequate,
- and high-speed intent can be maintained.

---

# Ineligibility Criteria

The exercise is not eligible when the athlete presents:

- acute shoulder pain,
- elbow pain,
- wrist pain,
- chest or rib pain,
- inability to release the ball safely,
- uncontrolled balance,
- unsafe wall or partner setup,
- or severe fatigue that suppresses velocity.

---

# Relative Contraindications

Use caution when the athlete has:

- recent shoulder irritation,
- anterior shoulder sensitivity,
- elbow tendinopathy,
- thoracic discomfort,
- or high pressing volume.

The engine should reduce ball mass, total volume or range when needed.

---

# Absolute Contraindications

Do not prescribe the exercise when there is:

- acute upper-limb injury,
- acute rib or chest injury,
- neurological weakness affecting release,
- inability to throw safely,
- or an unsuitable target area.

---

# Common Errors

## Using a Ball That Is Too Heavy

The athlete pushes the ball slowly instead of throwing it.

Risk:

- reduced ballistic intent,
- slower movement,
- altered mechanics.

Correction:

- reduce medicine-ball mass.

## Guiding the Ball

The athlete controls the ball through release instead of projecting it.

Risk:

- reduced acceleration,
- weaker power stimulus.

Correction:

- cue a decisive release through the target.

## Excessive Trunk Extension

The athlete leans backward and flares the ribs.

Risk:

- lumbar compensation,
- poor force direction.

Correction:

- stack the ribs over the pelvis and reduce load.

## Inconsistent Release Angle

The ball travels upward, downward or sideways unpredictably.

Risk:

- poor standardization,
- reduced transfer.

Correction:

- use a clear wall target and consistent setup.

## Arms Dominating the Movement

The athlete produces little trunk or lower-body contribution when whole-body integration is intended.

Risk:

- reduced force transfer,
- limited total output.

Correction:

- cue coordinated force from the ground upward.

## Excessive Lower-Body Contribution

The athlete turns the throw into a jump or uncontrolled lunge.

Risk:

- loss of balance,
- altered exercise objective.

Correction:

- select the correct variation and constrain the stance.

## Catching a Fast Rebound Without Preparation

The athlete receives the ball unexpectedly.

Risk:

- wrist, elbow or shoulder injury.

Correction:

- use a non-rebounding ball or controlled retrieval setup.

# Coaching Priorities

1. Select the correct ball mass.
2. Establish a stable stance.
3. Keep the ball close to the chest.
4. Maintain stacked posture.
5. Accelerate through release.
6. Project the ball directly forward.
7. Finish in balance.
8. Reset fully.
9. Preserve repetition quality.
10. Stop before velocity declines.

---

# Prescription Variables

## Repetitions

Typical range:

- 2 to 5 repetitions for maximal power,
- 4 to 8 repetitions for repeated ballistic effort.

## Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 6 sets.

## Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 4 minutes.

## Ball Mass

Typical range:

- light to moderate medicine ball,
- generally 1 to 5 kilograms depending on athlete and variation.

The selected mass must preserve speed and clean release.

## Distance to Target

Distance is determined by:

- ball rebound characteristics,
- athlete power,
- throwing variation,
- safety,
- and target accuracy.

---

# Maximum-Power Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 6 sets,
- 2 to 5 repetitions,
- maximal intent,
- 2 to 4 minutes rest,
- complete reset between repetitions.

---

# Strength-Speed Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 5 sets,
- 3 to 6 repetitions,
- moderate ball mass,
- 90 seconds to 3 minutes rest,
- no visible velocity loss.

---

# Repeated-Effort Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 4 sets,
- 5 to 8 repetitions,
- light ball,
- controlled rhythm,
- no technical breakdown.

This prescription should not become a fatigue circuit unless conditioning is explicitly intended.

---

# Standing Chest Pass

Primary role:

- basic bilateral horizontal ballistic power.

Primary characteristics:

- simple stance,
- moderate whole-body integration,
- low technical complexity.

This is the default variation for most athletes.

---

# Seated Chest Pass

The athlete performs the throw from a seated position.

Primary benefits:

- reduced lower-body contribution,
- greater upper-body isolation,
- easier assessment of pressing power.

The athlete should maintain trunk control and avoid excessive backward lean.

---

# Tall-Kneeling Chest Pass

Primary benefits:

- reduced leg contribution,
- greater trunk-control demand,
- simple bilateral projection.

---

# Half-Kneeling Chest Pass

Primary benefits:

- asymmetrical stance,
- greater anti-rotation demand,
- side-to-side comparison.

---

# Step-In Chest Pass

The athlete steps toward the target before release.

Primary benefits:

- greater lower-body contribution,
- forward momentum integration,
- improved whole-body sequencing.

This variation is reserved for athletes who can preserve release direction and balance.

---

# Reactive Chest Pass

The athlete catches and immediately rethrows the ball.

Primary benefits:

- reactive upper-body power,
- rapid force reversal,
- reduced ground-contact delay.

This variation requires:

- appropriate ball rebound,
- safe receiving mechanics,
- strong shoulder readiness,
- and advanced technical control.

---

# Progressions

Progressions include:

1. improved release speed,
2. improved target accuracy,
3. greater projection distance,
4. slightly greater ball mass,
5. standing from kneeling,
6. step-in execution,
7. reactive execution,
8. staggered stance,
9. more demanding start position,
10. repeated high-quality throws.

Ball mass should increase only when speed remains high.

---

# Regressions

Regressions include:

- lighter ball,
- shorter throwing distance,
- seated variation,
- tall-kneeling variation,
- reduced repetition count,
- controlled wall target,
- submaximal technical throws,
- non-rebounding ball.

---

# Recommended Progression Sequence

1. Light seated Chest Pass.
2. Tall-kneeling Chest Pass.
3. Standing Chest Pass.
4. Greater projection distance.
5. Slightly heavier ball.
6. Step-in Chest Pass.
7. Staggered-stance Chest Pass.
8. Reactive Chest Pass.

---

# Fatigue Profile

## Systemic Fatigue

Low

The exercise creates limited whole-body fatigue when volume is appropriate.

## Local Fatigue

Low to Moderate

Primary local fatigue occurs in:

- chest,
- anterior shoulders,
- triceps,
- and trunk.

## Neurological Fatigue

Moderate

Maximal-intent throws require rapid recruitment and high coordination.

## Connective-Tissue Stress

Low to Moderate

Stress may accumulate in:

- shoulders,
- elbows,
- wrists,
- and chest structures.

---

# Recovery Cost

Recovery cost is generally low.

It increases with:

- heavier balls,
- excessive repetition count,
- reactive catching,
- high weekly frequency,
- and concurrent pressing or striking volume.

The athlete may feel little soreness despite reduced ballistic output.

---

# Session Placement

The Medicine-Ball Chest Pass is usually placed:

- after the warm-up,
- after movement preparation,
- before heavy pressing,
- before hypertrophy work,
- and before high-fatigue conditioning.

It may also be paired with a heavy press in contrast training when appropriate.

It should not be placed after severe upper-body fatigue when power quality is the objective.

---

# Weekly Placement

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 3 times per week.

Higher frequency may be appropriate when:

- volume is low,
- ball mass is light,
- shoulder readiness is good,
- and technical quality remains high.

---

# Interaction With Other Training

## Bench Press

The exercise pairs well with bench pressing when used:

- before the lift,
- or in contrast training.

Excessive pressing fatigue reduces throw velocity.

## Push Press

The Chest Pass emphasizes horizontal projection, while the Push Press emphasizes vertical force transfer.

The two may complement one another.

## Striking

The exercise has strong overlap with:

- straight-punch projection,
- rapid arm extension,
- trunk-to-hand force transfer.

Throwing volume should be reduced when striking volume is already very high.

## Grappling

The exercise creates relatively low interference with grappling but still adds shoulder and elbow stress.

---

# Combat Transfer

The Medicine-Ball Chest Pass supports combat performance by improving:

- bilateral horizontal force projection,
- rapid pressing force,
- trunk-to-arm sequencing,
- whole-body acceleration,
- and maximal intent.

Its transfer is strongest for actions requiring fast forward projection.

It does not replace technical punching practice.

# Substitution Logic

## Substitute Shot-Put Throw When

- unilateral horizontal power is the target,
- asymmetrical stance is relevant,
- or punch-like side specificity is desired.

## Substitute Rotational Throw When

- transverse-plane power is the primary objective,
- hook or rotational-strike transfer is required.

## Substitute Push Press When

- the athlete should retain control of the load,
- vertical power is more relevant,
- or throwing space is unavailable.

## Substitute Plyometric Push-Up When

- bodyweight pressing reactivity is the target,
- no medicine ball or throwing wall is available,
- or stretch-shortening-cycle emphasis is preferred.

## Substitute Cable Punch When

- controlled unilateral resistance is required,
- ballistic release is inappropriate,
- or load must be adjusted precisely.

# Progression Criteria

The athlete may progress when:

- release remains fast,
- the force vector remains horizontal,
- the trunk stays controlled,
- the athlete finishes in balance,
- no pain occurs,
- target accuracy remains consistent,
- and there is no meaningful velocity loss.

---

# Regression Criteria

The exercise should be regressed when:

- the throw becomes a slow push,
- release direction changes,
- the athlete loses balance,
- the ribs flare,
- shoulder or elbow discomfort appears,
- or fatigue reduces output.

---

# Readiness Screen

Before prescription, assess:

- shoulder pain,
- elbow pain,
- wrist pain,
- chest or rib discomfort,
- pressing tolerance,
- trunk control,
- ability to release safely,
- recent pressing volume,
- and current striking volume.

---

# Safety Rules

- Use an appropriate medicine ball.
- Verify the wall or partner setup.
- Keep the throwing lane clear.
- Do not use a damaged ball.
- Do not catch unpredictable rebounds.
- Maintain safe wall distance.
- Stop before technical failure.
- Retrieve the ball under control.
- Do not throw toward another athlete.
- Terminate immediately if sharp pain, numbness or sudden weakness occurs.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine may select the Medicine-Ball Chest Pass when:

- bilateral horizontal ballistic power is the intended adaptation,
- upper-body speed-strength is required,
- the athlete can throw safely,
- shoulder and elbow readiness are adequate,
- the ball mass preserves velocity,
- and total pressing and striking volume remains recoverable.

The engine must avoid selecting it when:

- unilateral or rotational power is the actual target,
- the athlete cannot maintain a horizontal vector,
- the ball is too heavy to preserve speed,
- or upper-body fatigue would suppress power quality.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When the Medicine-Ball Chest Pass is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the targeted horizontal force vector,
- the reason bilateral projection is preferred,
- the selected stance,
- the ball mass,
- the prescribed repetitions and sets,
- the expected limiting factor,
- the interaction with pressing and striking volume,
- relevant shoulder, elbow or trunk constraints,
- and the progression criterion.

Example:

> Medicine-Ball Chest Pass selected to develop bilateral horizontal upper-body power relevant to straight-punch projection. A light medicine ball is used to preserve maximal release speed. Low-repetition sets are placed before pressing work, with the set terminated at the first clear decline in projection velocity.

---

# Summary

The Medicine-Ball Chest Pass is a low-complexity horizontal ballistic exercise.

Its primary value lies in developing:

- bilateral horizontal force projection,
- upper-body speed-strength,
- rapid pressing force,
- trunk-to-arm force transfer,
- and maximal release intent.

Within CAS, it is appropriate when the athlete needs simple, high-velocity forward projection and can maintain a fast, direct release.

It must be programmed with a ball light enough to preserve speed and with low enough volume to maintain power quality.
