# BALLISTICS EXERCISE COMPARISON

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

This document compares the ballistic exercises currently included in the CAS Ballistics category.

Its purpose is to help the CAS Engine distinguish between medicine-ball exercises that all involve rapid force production but differ through:

- force vector,
- release direction,
- bilateral or unilateral execution,
- rotational demand,
- lower-body contribution,
- technical complexity,
- tissue stress,
- and combat transfer.

The exercises compared are:

- Medicine-Ball Chest Pass
- Medicine-Ball Overhead Throw
- Medicine-Ball Rotational Throw
- Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss
- Medicine-Ball Slam
- Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw
- Medicine-Ball Reverse Throw

The comparison must not be used to identify one universally superior ballistic exercise.

Each exercise is appropriate only when it matches:

- the intended adaptation,
- the required force vector,
- the athlete’s movement competency,
- tissue readiness,
- available space and equipment,
- and the surrounding training load.

---

# Core Principle

> Select the ballistic exercise whose force vector, sequencing demand and release pattern best match the intended adaptation.

Ballistic exercises are not interchangeable.

The same medicine ball can create very different adaptations depending on whether it is projected:

- forward,
- upward,
- backward,
- downward,
- rotationally,
- diagonally,
- bilaterally,
- or unilaterally.

The CAS Engine must identify the required projection pattern before selecting the exercise.

# Primary Adaptation Comparison

## Medicine-Ball Chest Pass

Primary adaptation:

- bilateral horizontal force projection.

Best suited for:

- upper-body speed-strength,
- rapid bilateral pressing force,
- straight-punch support,
- low-complexity ballistic work.

Not ideal when:

- unilateral or rotational power is the actual target,
- lower-body contribution should be high,
- or posterior-chain extension is the priority.

---

## Medicine-Ball Overhead Throw

Primary adaptation:

- whole-body extension and overhead projection.

Best suited for:

- vertical or forward projection,
- lower-to-upper-body sequencing,
- total-body extension power,
- explosive force transfer.

Not ideal when:

- overhead mobility is limited,
- horizontal or rotational specificity is required,
- or the athlete cannot control lumbar extension.

---

## Medicine-Ball Rotational Throw

Primary adaptation:

- transverse-plane force projection.

Best suited for:

- hooks,
- rotational strikes,
- throws,
- hip-to-hand sequencing,
- side-specific power.

Not ideal when:

- trunk rotation is not tolerated,
- the athlete cannot control lumbar motion,
- or a simpler bilateral vector is sufficient.

---

## Medicine-Ball Scoop Toss

Primary adaptation:

- hip-driven diagonal or rotational force projection.

Best suited for:

- whole-body rotational power,
- lower-body contribution,
- diagonal force transfer,
- takedown and throwing support.

Not ideal when:

- hip or knee readiness is poor,
- the athlete cannot coordinate lower-to-upper-body sequencing,
- or a simpler rotational throw is sufficient.

---

## Medicine-Ball Slam

Primary adaptation:

- downward ballistic force projection.

Best suited for:

- trunk and shoulder power,
- aggressive downward intent,
- simple whole-body ballistic work,
- low-complexity projection.

Not ideal when:

- maximal horizontal, vertical or rotational transfer is the priority,
- overhead motion is poorly tolerated,
- or the exercise would become conditioning-dominant.

---

## Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw

Primary adaptation:

- unilateral horizontal projection.

Best suited for:

- rear-hand power,
- staggered-stance force transfer,
- asymmetrical ballistic work,
- side-to-side assessment.

Not ideal when:

- bilateral projection is sufficient,
- stance stability is poor,
- or the athlete cannot coordinate rear-leg-to-hand sequencing.

---

## Medicine-Ball Reverse Throw

Primary adaptation:

- posterior-chain and whole-body extension power.

Best suited for:

- hip extension,
- lifting and projection capacity,
- backward and upward force expression,
- maximal total-body extension.

Not ideal when:

- open space is unavailable,
- spatial awareness is poor,
- or horizontal and rotational specificity is more important.

# Bilateral Versus Unilateral Comparison

## Bilateral Exercises

- Chest Pass
- Overhead Throw
- Slam
- Reverse Throw

Primary advantages:

- simpler force production,
- easier technical standardization,
- greater total output,
- lower side-specific complexity.

Primary limitations:

- reduced asymmetry detection,
- lower stance specificity.

## Unilateral or Side-Specific Exercises

- Rotational Throw
- Scoop Toss
- Shot-Put Throw

Primary advantages:

- side-to-side assessment,
- greater combat relevance,
- stronger stance specificity,
- improved unilateral sequencing.

Primary limitations:

- greater coordination demand,
- higher technical variability,
- lower absolute implement mass.

# Ball-Mass Comparison

## Lightest Typical Ball

Shot-Put Throw

Chest Pass

Reason:

- high release velocity,
- unilateral or upper-body dominant action.

## Moderate Ball

Rotational Throw

Scoop Toss

Overhead Throw

Reason:

- greater whole-body contribution,
- preserved ballistic speed.

## Moderate to Heavier Ball Tolerance

Slam

Reverse Throw

Reason:

- larger muscle mass contribution,
- strong whole-body force production.

Heavier does not automatically mean better.

The ball remains appropriate only while velocity and sequencing stay high.

- Overhead Throw

Prefer:

- Chest Pass

- Slam

Avoid or modify:

- Rotational Throw

---

## Hip or Knee Irritation

Prefer:

- seated or kneeling Chest Pass
- tall-kneeling Rotational Throw

Avoid or modify:

- Scoop Toss

- high lower-body contribution Overhead Throw

---

## High Striking Volume

Prefer:

- low-volume Reverse Throw
- low-volume Overhead Throw

Reduce:

- Chest Pass
- Shot-Put Throw
- Rotational Throw

when shoulder or trunk explosive volume is already high.

---

## High Grappling Volume

Prefer:

- low-volume Chest Pass
- low-volume Slam
- moderate Overhead Throw

Reduce:

- Scoop Toss
- Reverse Throw
- high-volume Rotational Throw

when hip, trunk and posterior-chain fatigue are elevated.

---

## Limited Space

Prefer:

- Chest Pass against wall
- Rotational Throw against wall
- Shot-Put Throw against wall
- Slam

Avoid:

- Reverse Throw

# Default CAS Selection Hierarchy

When multiple ballistic exercises are eligible, CAS should generally use the following hierarchy:

1. identify the intended force vector,
2. determine bilateral or unilateral demand,
3. determine the required lower-body contribution,
4. determine whether rotation is required,
5. assess shoulder, trunk, hip and knee readiness,
6. verify environmental safety,
7. select the lowest sufficient complexity,
8. choose the lightest effective ball mass,
9. prescribe low enough volume to preserve speed,
10. define clear set-termination criteria.

---

# Default Selection Rules

## Choose Chest Pass When

- bilateral horizontal power is the target,
- low complexity is preferred,
- and upper-body projection is the main objective.

## Choose Overhead Throw When

- whole-body extension is required,
- upward or forward-upward projection is relevant,
- and overhead mobility is adequate.

## Choose Rotational Throw When

- transverse-plane power is the priority,
- rotational strike or throwing transfer is desired,
- and the athlete can rotate safely.

## Choose Scoop Toss When

- hip-driven diagonal power is required,
- greater lower-body contribution is desired,
- and whole-body sequencing is a priority.

## Choose Slam When

- downward ballistic power is required,
- simple aggressive intent is desired,
- and the floor and ball are appropriate.

## Choose Shot-Put Throw When

- unilateral horizontal projection is required,
- staggered-stance transfer is relevant,
- and side-to-side assessment is useful.

## Choose Reverse Throw When

- posterior-chain extension power is the target,
- maximal whole-body projection is desired,
- and a safe open area is available.

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine must not select a ballistic exercise solely because it appears explosive or resembles a combat movement.

The engine must:

- identify the intended force vector,
- identify unilateral or bilateral demand,
- determine the required lower-body contribution,
- determine whether rotational movement is useful,
- select an implement mass that preserves speed,
- assess relevant joint and tissue readiness,
- verify the throwing environment,
- account for all striking, grappling, strength and plyometric exposure,
- and justify the selection in the Decision Trace.

The engine should prefer the simpler exercise when two options provide the same required adaptation.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

For every ballistic exercise selection, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the primary adaptation,
- the selected force vector,
- the reason the exercise is preferred over alternatives,
- the selected stance,
- unilateral or bilateral execution,
- medicine-ball mass,
- repetitions and sets,
- expected limiting factor,
- expected fatigue cost,
- interaction with combat and strength training,
- relevant shoulder, trunk, hip, knee or spinal constraints,
- environmental safety requirements,
- and progression or regression criteria.

Example:

> Medicine-Ball Shot-Put Throw selected instead of Chest Pass because the objective is unilateral horizontal power from a staggered stance rather than bilateral projection. A light medicine ball is used to preserve release velocity and rear-leg-to-hand sequencing. Low-repetition sets are placed before pressing work and terminated at the first clear decline in direction, balance or speed.

# Summary

The Ballistics category contains exercises that differ primarily through force vector, sequencing and release pattern.

Chest Pass develops bilateral horizontal power.

Overhead Throw develops upward or forward whole-body extension.

Rotational Throw develops transverse-plane power.

Scoop Toss develops hip-driven diagonal or rotational projection.

Slam develops downward ballistic power.

Shot-Put Throw develops unilateral horizontal projection from a staggered stance.

Reverse Throw develops posterior-chain and whole-body extension power.

The correct CAS selection depends on:

- the intended adaptation,
- the required force vector,
- unilateral or bilateral demand,
- lower-body contribution,
- rotational requirement,
- technical complexity,
- tissue readiness,
- environmental safety,
- and interaction with surrounding training.

No ballistic exercise is universally superior.

The best exercise is the one that allows the athlete to project force rapidly in the required direction while preserving technical quality, safety and recoverability.
