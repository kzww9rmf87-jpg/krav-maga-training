# BALLISTICS OVERVIEW

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Ballistics category defines exercises in which the athlete accelerates an external implement through the full movement and releases, projects or throws it.

Within CAS, ballistic exercises are used to develop high-velocity force production without requiring the athlete to deliberately decelerate the load at the end of the range of motion.

This category is primarily built around medicine-ball throws.

Ballistic exercises are selected according to:

- force vector,
- direction of projection,
- movement pattern,
- unilateral or bilateral execution,
- rotational demand,
- lower-body contribution,
- implement mass,
- technical complexity,
- and transfer to combat actions.

They are not selected merely because they appear explosive.

Their value depends on whether the exercise allows the athlete to produce force rapidly in the intended direction while preserving technical quality.

---

# Philosophy

Ballistic training differs from conventional strength training because the athlete is not required to slow the implement before the end of the movement.

The athlete can continue accelerating through release.

This makes ballistic exercises especially useful for developing:

- high movement velocity,
- rapid force expression,
- intermuscular coordination,
- force transfer through the kinetic chain,
- and projection of force into space.

The governing principle is:

> Accelerate through release while preserving direction, sequencing and intent.

The objective is not simply to throw the implement far.

The objective is to express force rapidly through a technically appropriate movement pattern.

# Core Principle

Ballistic exercises must be classified by force direction and release pattern.

The primary dimensions are:

- horizontal projection,
- vertical projection,
- rotational projection,
- diagonal projection,
- overhead projection,
- scoop projection,
- and slam projection.

The CAS Engine must identify the required vector before selecting the exercise.

---

# Ballistic Versus Plyometric Training

Ballistic and plyometric exercises overlap in their emphasis on speed and power, but they are not identical.

## Plyometric Training

Primary characteristics:

- rapid eccentric-to-concentric transition,
- stretch-shortening-cycle emphasis,
- reactive force production,
- landing or rebounding demands.

Typical examples:

- Box Jump,
- Depth Jump,
- Broad Jump,
- Lateral Bound.

## Ballistic Training

Primary characteristics:

- continuous acceleration,
- release or projection of an implement,
- no voluntary terminal deceleration,
- high intent through the full range.

Typical examples:

- Medicine-Ball Chest Pass,
- Rotational Throw,
- Overhead Throw,
- Scoop Toss,
- Slam.

Some exercises may contain both ballistic and plyometric elements.

CAS should classify the exercise according to the primary intended adaptation.

---

# Ballistic Versus Power Training

The Power category includes exercises that develop explosive force while the athlete continues to control the load.

Typical examples:

- Push Press,
- Hang High Pull,
- Hang Power Clean,
- Jump Shrug.

The Ballistics category is distinct because the implement is:

- released,
- projected,
- thrown,
- or driven into a surface.

This difference allows acceleration to continue through the end of the movement.

The CAS Engine should use Ballistics when release is central to the adaptation.

# Unilateral Versus Bilateral Ballistics

## Bilateral Exercises

Typical examples:

- Chest Pass,
- Overhead Throw,
- Slam,
- Reverse Throw.

Primary advantages:

- simple force production,
- greater total output,
- lower coordination demand,
- easy repetition standardization.

Primary limitations:

- lower side-specificity,
- reduced asymmetry detection.

## Unilateral or Asymmetrical Exercises

Typical examples:

- Shot-Put Throw,
- Rotational Throw,
- unilateral Scoop Toss.

Primary advantages:

- side-to-side assessment,
- greater combat specificity,
- stronger anti-rotation and force-transfer demand,
- improved unilateral sequencing.

Primary limitations:

- greater technical complexity,
- lower absolute implement mass,
- greater risk of compensatory trunk motion.

---

# Lower-Body Contribution

Ballistic exercises may be classified by the amount of lower-body involvement.

## Low Lower-Body Contribution

Typical examples:

- kneeling Chest Pass,
- seated Chest Pass,
- tall-kneeling Rotational Throw.

Primary use:

- isolate upper-body or trunk contribution,
- reduce lower-body compensation,
- simplify force-source analysis.

## Moderate Lower-Body Contribution

Typical examples:

- standing Chest Pass,
- standing Rotational Throw,
- Medicine-Ball Slam.

Primary use:

- integrate stance and trunk,
- maintain moderate whole-body involvement.

## High Lower-Body Contribution

Typical examples:

- Scoop Toss,
- Overhead Throw,
- Reverse Throw,
- step-behind Rotational Throw.

Primary use:

- maximize whole-body sequencing,
- develop force transfer from the ground,
- increase total ballistic output.

---

# Technical Complexity Classification

## Low Complexity

Typical examples:

- standing Chest Pass,
- basic Medicine-Ball Slam,
- basic Overhead Throw.

These exercises require:

- clear direction,
- stable setup,
- simple release mechanics.

## Moderate Complexity

Typical examples:

- Rotational Throw,
- Scoop Toss,
- Shot-Put Throw.

These require:

- sequencing,
- stance control,
- hip and trunk contribution,
- precise release direction.

## High Complexity

Typical examples:

- step-behind throws,
- reactive catch-and-throw drills,
- multi-directional combinations,
- single-leg ballistic throws.

These are appropriate only when the added complexity serves a clear sport or assessment purpose.

# Physiological Profile

Ballistic training primarily targets:

- rapid motor-unit recruitment,
- rate of force development,
- high-velocity force expression,
- intermuscular coordination,
- and neural intent.

Ballistic exercises generally create:

- low to moderate muscle damage,
- moderate neural demand,
- low to moderate metabolic fatigue,
- and high dependence on technical freshness.

The quality of each repetition matters more than fatigue accumulation.

---

# Neurological Profile

Effective ballistic training requires:

- maximal or near-maximal intent,
- rapid recruitment of high-threshold motor units,
- precise sequencing,
- stable proximal segments,
- and clear force direction.

Performance deteriorates when:

- movement velocity drops,
- release direction becomes inconsistent,
- the athlete begins forcing repetitions,
- or fatigue alters coordination.

The set should end before these changes become significant.

---

# Implement Selection

Medicine balls are the primary implements used in this category.

The implement should be:

- heavy enough to provide resistance,
- light enough to preserve speed,
- suitable for release,
- safe for the throwing surface,
- and appropriate for the movement pattern.

Other possible implements include:

- slam balls,
- soft wall balls,
- sand-filled medicine balls,
- light shot implements,
- or specialized ballistic devices.

The CAS exercise library should prioritize safe, repeatable and widely available options.

---

# Medicine-Ball Mass Selection

The correct medicine-ball mass depends on:

- exercise type,
- athlete strength,
- technical level,
- desired velocity,
- and intended adaptation.

General principle:

> Use the heaviest implement that preserves high velocity, complete acceleration and correct release mechanics.

The ball is too heavy when:

- movement becomes slow,
- the athlete pauses before release,
- posture changes,
- the arms dominate a whole-body movement,
- or projection distance collapses.

The ball is too light when:

- the athlete cannot feel meaningful resistance,
- technical control is lost through excessive speed,
- or the exercise no longer provides the intended force stimulus.

---

# General Loading Guidelines

Typical medicine-ball ranges:

- 1 to 3 kilograms for very high-speed upper-body throws,
- 2 to 5 kilograms for most rotational and horizontal throws,
- 3 to 6 kilograms for stronger athletes and whole-body throws,
- heavier implements only when speed and mechanics remain intact.

These are general ranges rather than fixed rules.

CAS should individualize loading according to movement quality.

---

# General Prescription Ranges

## Maximum Ballistic Power

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 5 repetitions,
- 3 to 6 sets,
- full intent,
- 2 to 4 minutes recovery,
- complete technical quality.

## Ballistic Strength-Speed

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 6 repetitions,
- 3 to 5 sets,
- moderate implement mass,
- 90 seconds to 3 minutes recovery.

## Ballistic Speed-Strength

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 5 repetitions,
- 3 to 6 sets,
- lighter implement,
- maximal velocity,
- long enough recovery to preserve speed.

## Repeated Ballistic Effort

Typical prescription:

- 5 to 10 repetitions,
- 2 to 4 sets,
- controlled submaximal density,
- no major velocity loss.

Repeated ballistic work should not be confused with conditioning unless conditioning is explicitly the target.

---

# Repetition Quality

A ballistic repetition is acceptable only when:

- acceleration is continuous,
- release is decisive,
- force direction is correct,
- the trunk remains controlled,
- sequencing is appropriate,
- and the athlete can reset safely.

A repetition is invalid when:

- the athlete pushes instead of throws,
- the ball is guided rather than released,
- the athlete loses balance,
- the release angle changes unintentionally,
- or velocity clearly declines.

---

# Set Termination Criteria

The set should end when:

- projection distance decreases meaningfully,
- velocity visibly drops,
- release becomes inconsistent,
- the athlete begins using compensatory motion,
- balance deteriorates,
- or maximal intent cannot be maintained.

Ballistic training should not be continued to technical failure.

---

# Rest Intervals

Typical rest intervals:

- 2 to 4 minutes for maximal power,
- 90 seconds to 3 minutes for strength-speed,
- 60 to 120 seconds for low-volume technical work.

Rest must be sufficient to preserve output.

Short rest is appropriate only when repeated-effort capacity is the intended adaptation.

---

# Session Placement

Ballistic exercises are usually placed:

- after the warm-up,
- after low-intensity movement preparation,
- before heavy strength work,
- before hypertrophy work,
- and before high-fatigue conditioning.

They may follow low-volume plyometric work when the sequence is designed carefully.

Ballistic exercises should not normally be performed after severe fatigue.

---

# Weekly Frequency

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 3 exposures per week.

Higher frequency may be appropriate when:

- volume is low,
- exercises vary by vector,
- recovery is strong,
- and technical quality remains high.

Rotational and high-velocity throwing volume should still be progressed conservatively.

---

# Progression Logic

Ballistic progression may occur through:

1. improved release mechanics,
2. improved force direction,
3. greater projection distance,
4. increased movement velocity,
5. improved sequencing,
6. slightly greater implement mass,
7. more demanding stance,
8. unilateral execution,
9. step-based execution,
10. reactive execution.

Complexity should increase only after the athlete demonstrates consistent basic mechanics.

---

# Regression Logic

Ballistic exercises may be regressed by:

- reducing implement mass,
- reducing range of motion,
- removing the step,
- using bilateral execution,
- using a kneeling or seated position,
- reducing rotational amplitude,
- throwing into a closer wall,
- lowering release height,
- or replacing the throw with a controlled power exercise.

---

# Readiness Requirements

Ballistic exercises should be reduced, modified or omitted when the athlete presents:

- acute shoulder pain,
- elbow pain,
- wrist pain,
- spinal pain,
- uncontrolled rotational discomfort,
- inability to decelerate the body safely,
- impaired balance,
- severe fatigue,
- or inability to release the implement safely.

Rotational throws require pain-free trunk and hip rotation.

Overhead throws require adequate shoulder and thoracic readiness.

---

# Safety Constraints

Ballistic exercises require:

- a clear throwing area,
- suitable wall or floor surfaces,
- appropriate medicine-ball construction,
- safe spacing between athletes,
- and controlled retrieval of the implement.

The athlete must not:

- throw toward an occupied area,
- use a rebounding ball without preparation,
- catch high-velocity rebounds unexpectedly,
- use damaged equipment,
- or continue after technical control is lost.

---

# Common Errors Across the Category

Common errors include:

- using a ball that is too heavy,
- prioritizing fatigue over velocity,
- guiding the ball instead of releasing it,
- failing to use the lower body when required,
- rotating through the lumbar spine instead of the hips and thoracic region,
- losing balance after release,
- excessive repetition count,
- poor wall distance,
- and inconsistent force direction.

---

# Coaching Priorities

Primary coaching priorities are:

1. define the intended force vector,
2. select the correct implement mass,
3. establish a stable starting position,
4. sequence the movement from the ground upward,
5. accelerate through release,
6. maintain clear projection direction,
7. finish in balance,
8. reset fully between repetitions,
9. stop before velocity loss,
10. preserve maximal intent.

---

# Combat Transfer

Ballistic exercises support combat performance through:

- rapid force expression,
- ground-to-hand force transfer,
- rotational power,
- horizontal projection,
- unilateral force production,
- trunk sequencing,
- and movement intent.

Transfer depends on force-vector similarity rather than visual resemblance alone.

A throw that matches the direction and sequencing of a combat action may provide useful physical transfer even when the movement is not technically identical.

# Fatigue Cost

Ballistic exercises generally create:

- low to moderate systemic fatigue,
- low to moderate local muscular fatigue,
- moderate neural demand,
- and low muscle damage when volume is appropriate.

Fatigue rises with:

- heavier implements,
- excessive repetition count,
- repeated high-output sets,
- complex rotational patterns,
- and insufficient rest.

The primary risk is loss of quality rather than excessive metabolic stress.

---

# Recovery Interaction

Ballistic training interacts with:

- plyometrics,
- Olympic-lifting derivatives,
- heavy strength work,
- striking practice,
- grappling,
- and high-intensity conditioning.

Ballistic volume should be reduced when:

- technical combat practice already contains high explosive volume,
- shoulder or trunk fatigue is elevated,
- or power output is clearly suppressed.

# Exercise Selection Logic

## Select Chest Pass When

- bilateral horizontal power is required,
- straight-punch force transfer is relevant,
- low technical complexity is preferred.

## Select Overhead Throw When

- whole-body extension is required,
- vertical or forward projection is relevant,
- the athlete has adequate shoulder mobility.

## Select Rotational Throw When

- transverse-plane power is the priority,
- rotational striking or throwing transfer is desired,
- the athlete can sequence hips and trunk safely.

## Select Scoop Toss When

- greater lower-body contribution is required,
- diagonal or rotational projection is desired,
- whole-body sequencing is a priority.

## Select Slam When

- downward projection is required,
- low-complexity total-body intent is desired,
- the floor and ball are appropriate.

## Select Shot-Put Throw When

- unilateral horizontal power is required,
- punch-like asymmetry is relevant,
- side-to-side comparison is useful.

## Select Reverse Throw When

- posterior-chain extension is the priority,
- backward projection is safe,
- the athlete can maintain balance and spatial awareness.

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine must:

- identify the intended force vector,
- identify bilateral or unilateral demand,
- determine the required lower-body contribution,
- select an implement that preserves speed,
- assess shoulder, trunk and hip readiness,
- protect higher-priority technical work,
- prescribe low enough volume to preserve output,
- and define clear set-termination criteria.

The engine must not select a ballistic exercise solely because it looks explosive.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When a ballistic exercise is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the targeted force vector,
- the intended power adaptation,
- the reason the selected throw is preferred,
- the selected implement mass,
- bilateral or unilateral execution,
- the selected stance,
- the prescribed repetitions and sets,
- the expected limiting factor,
- the interaction with combat practice and other power work,
- relevant shoulder, trunk or hip constraints,
- and the progression or regression rule.

Example:

> Medicine-Ball Rotational Throw selected to develop transverse-plane power relevant to hooks and rotational striking. A moderate ball is used to preserve velocity and hip-to-hand sequencing. Low-repetition sets are placed before strength work, with the set terminated at the first clear loss of projection speed.

---

# Summary

The Ballistics category develops high-velocity force production through exercises in which an external implement is released or projected.

Its primary value lies in developing:

- rapid force expression,
- rate of force development,
- force-vector specificity,
- ground-to-hand sequencing,
- rotational power,
- unilateral and bilateral projection,
- and maximal movement intent.

Within CAS, ballistic exercises are selected according to:

- direction,
- stance,
- implement mass,
- lower-body contribution,
- technical complexity,
- and combat transfer.

The correct ballistic exercise is the one that allows the athlete to accelerate through release with maximal intent, consistent direction and minimal unnecessary fatigue.
