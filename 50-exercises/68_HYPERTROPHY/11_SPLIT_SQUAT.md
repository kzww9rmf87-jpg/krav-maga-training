# SPLIT SQUAT

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Split Squat is a unilateral lower-body exercise performed in a stationary split stance with both feet on the ground.

Within CAS, it is the primary lower-body exercise available to an athlete training without equipment.

---

# Philosophy

The squat pattern is the foundation of lower-body preparation, and an athlete without a barbell still needs it.

The Split Squat trains that pattern one leg at a time, which raises the relative load on the working leg without any external resistance.

CAS selects the Split Squat when lower-body muscular development is requested and no external loading is available.

It is distinct from the Bulgarian Split Squat, whose rear foot is elevated. The Split Squat keeps both feet grounded, which lowers the balance demand and the skill requirement, and makes it the appropriate entry point.

# Coaching Cues

Set the stance long enough that the front shin stays near vertical.

Keep the pelvis square to the front.

Lower the rear knee toward the floor under control.

Drive through the whole front foot.

Keep the trunk tall.

---

# Common Errors

Stance too short, forcing the front knee forward.

Pelvis rotating toward the working leg.

Trunk collapsing forward.

Pushing off the rear foot.

Partial range of motion.

---

# Performance Indicators

Repetitions completed at a controlled tempo on both legs.

Symmetry between left and right.

Depth maintained under fatigue.

Balance retained to the final repetition.

---

# Scientific Evidence

Evidence Level

★★★★

Unilateral lower-body exercise is well supported for muscular development, correction of inter-limb asymmetry and lower-body preparation.

Evidence specific to the grounded Split Squat, as distinct from the rear-foot-elevated variation, is more limited; CAS treats the distinction as a skill and balance decision rather than a claim of different adaptation.

Transfer to combat performance is stated as expert consensus.

---

# Explainability

Example

The Split Squat is selected because the athlete requested lower-body muscular development and has no external loading available.

Training one leg at a time raises the relative load enough to produce a hypertrophy stimulus from body mass alone, and the grounded rear foot keeps the balance demand low enough that the working leg, not balance, is the limiting factor.

# Final Principle

The Split Squat trains the leg the way combat uses it:

one at a time,

from an asymmetric base,

with the trunk holding position,

and it does so without requiring a single piece of equipment.
