# Exercise Identity

Name: Rope Pull

Category: Grip

Primary Pattern: Horizontal or Vertical Pull

Secondary Pattern: Repeated Grip and Hand-Over-Hand Pulling

Equipment: Climbing Rope, Sled or Anchored Load

Complexity: Moderate

Unilateral or Bilateral: Alternating Bilateral

Closed or Open Chain: Open Chain

---

# Purpose

The Rope Pull develops grip-integrated pulling strength and endurance through repeated hand-over-hand force production.

Within CAS, it is used when the athlete must maintain a strong, deformable grip while repeatedly pulling an external load toward the body.

The exercise combines:

- open-hand support grip,
- forearm endurance,
- elbow-flexor strength,
- upper-back strength,
- scapular control,
- trunk stability,
- and repeated force production.

It is particularly relevant for combat athletes who need to sustain gripping and pulling under fatigue.

The Rope Pull must not be selected solely because it creates a strong conditioning effect.

Its use depends on whether the athlete needs grip-integrated pulling endurance and can maintain safe hand, elbow, shoulder and trunk mechanics.

# Movement Description

The athlete grips a rope connected to an external resistance or secured to an anchor point.

Using alternating hand-over-hand pulls, the athlete draws the rope and attached load toward the body.

The movement may be performed:

- standing,
- seated,
- half-kneeling,
- kneeling,
- or from an athletic stance.

The athlete continues until completing the prescribed distance, number of pulls or work interval.

The set ends before grip security, posture or pulling mechanics deteriorate.

---

# Starting Position

The athlete should begin with:

- the rope securely attached,
- sufficient clear space,
- both hands gripping the rope,
- the wrists controlled,
- the elbows slightly flexed,
- the shoulders stabilized,
- the ribs stacked,
- the trunk braced,
- and the feet or knees positioned according to the selected stance.

The rope should be aligned with the intended pulling direction.

---

# Execution

1. Verify the rope, anchor and external load.
2. Grip the rope securely with both hands.
3. Establish a stable stance.
4. Brace the trunk and keep the shoulders controlled.
5. Pull one hand toward the torso.
6. Regrip farther along the rope with the opposite hand.
7. Continue alternating hands.
8. Keep the rope close to the body.
9. Maintain consistent posture and rhythm.
10. Complete the prescribed distance, repetitions or duration.
11. Stop before slipping or major technical breakdown.
12. Reset or return the rope safely.

---

# Key Technical Cues

- Grip before you pull.
- Keep the wrists strong.
- Pull the elbows back.
- Keep the rope close.
- Stay tall and braced.
- Do not shrug.
- Regrip securely every time.
- Maintain a steady rhythm.
- Stop before the hands slip.

# Grip Demand

The Rope Pull creates a high open-hand grip demand.

The rope:

- deforms under pressure,
- limits complete hand closure,
- requires repeated regripping,
- increases thumb and finger contribution,
- and creates sustained friction-based loading.

Grip demand rises with:

- thicker rope,
- smoother rope material,
- heavier external load,
- longer pulling distance,
- faster pulling cadence,
- reduced lower-body support,
- and accumulated fatigue.

---

# Technical Complexity

Moderate

The movement is easier to learn than a Rope Climb because the athlete remains in contact with the floor.

However, the exercise still requires coordination between:

- grip,
- hand repositioning,
- pulling mechanics,
- trunk control,
- and stance stability.

CAS should not increase load or speed until the athlete can maintain secure regripping and consistent posture.

---

# Prerequisites

The athlete should demonstrate:

- pain-free rope gripping,
- competent horizontal or vertical pulling mechanics,
- stable wrist position,
- adequate shoulder control,
- sufficient elbow-tendon tolerance,
- trunk stability,
- and the ability to maintain balance under pulling force.

---

# Eligibility Criteria

The Rope Pull is eligible when:

- grip-integrated pulling endurance is a target,
- the athlete needs repeated hand-over-hand pulling,
- open-hand grip is a limiting factor,
- the equipment setup is safe,
- hand, elbow and shoulder tissues are healthy,
- and total grip volume remains recoverable.

---

# Ineligibility Criteria

The exercise is not eligible when the athlete presents:

- acute finger pain,
- thumb pain,
- wrist pain,
- elbow pain,
- shoulder pain,
- inability to regrip securely,
- poor trunk control,
- unsafe rope anchoring,
- unstable external loading,
- or severe residual grip fatigue.

---

# Relative Contraindications

Use caution when the athlete has:

- previous finger-pulley injury,
- chronic elbow tendinopathy,
- shoulder instability,
- reduced sensation in the hand,
- skin tears,
- rope-burn susceptibility,
- high weekly grappling volume,
- or low-back irritation under bracing.

The engine should reduce load, duration, rope thickness or total volume when these factors are present.

---

# Absolute Contraindications

Do not prescribe the exercise when there is:

- acute hand injury,
- acute wrist injury,
- acute elbow injury,
- acute shoulder injury,
- neurological loss of grip control,
- inability to maintain a stable stance,
- or an unsafe rope or load setup.

---

# Common Errors

## Insecure Regripping

The athlete releases one hand before the other hand is secure.

Risk:

- sudden loss of tension,
- rope slipping,
- abrupt finger loading.

Correction:

- secure the next grip before releasing the previous hand.

## Wrist Collapse

The wrist flexes or deviates excessively.

Risk:

- reduced force transfer,
- increased tendon stress.

Correction:

- reduce load and maintain neutral wrist alignment.

## Excessive Shoulder Elevation

The athlete shrugs during each pull.

Risk:

- unnecessary neck tension,
- reduced scapular efficiency.

Correction:

- keep the shoulders down and pull through the back.

## Trunk Rotation

The athlete twists excessively with each hand pull.

Risk:

- reduced stability,
- uneven loading,
- loss of control.

Correction:

- brace the trunk and keep the ribcage square.

## Rope Too Far From the Body

The athlete pulls with long inefficient lever arms.

Risk:

- greater shoulder stress,
- reduced control,
- faster grip fatigue.

Correction:

- keep the rope close to the torso.

## Pulling Too Fast

The athlete prioritizes speed over grip security.

Risk:

- incomplete regripping,
- technique breakdown,
- sudden slipping.

Correction:

- use a controlled and repeatable cadence.

## Continuing After Grip Breakdown

The athlete continues despite slipping or loss of wrist control.

Risk:

- dropped rope,
- hand injury,
- abrupt tendon stress.

Correction:

- terminate the set before grip failure.

# Coaching Priorities

1. Verify rope and load security.
2. Establish a stable stance.
3. Maintain a strong grip.
4. Keep the wrists controlled.
5. Regrip securely before pulling.
6. Pull through the elbows and upper back.
7. Keep the rope close.
8. Brace the trunk.
9. Maintain consistent rhythm.
10. Stop before technical failure.

---

# Prescription Variables

## Distance

Typical range:

- 5 to 15 metres for strength emphasis,
- 10 to 30 metres for strength endurance,
- longer distances only for advanced athletes.

## Duration

Typical range:

- 10 to 20 seconds for strength,
- 20 to 40 seconds for strength endurance,
- 30 to 60 seconds for work-capacity emphasis.

## Hand Pulls

Typical range:

- 6 to 20 hand-over-hand pulls per set.

## Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 5 sets.

## Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 4 minutes.

## Load

Load is determined by:

- sled weight,
- rope angle,
- friction,
- rope diameter,
- pulling position,
- distance,
- and intended adaptation.

---

# Strength Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 5 sets,
- heavy external resistance,
- 6 to 10 strong hand pulls,
- 2 to 4 minutes rest,
- complete grip and trunk control.

---

# Strength-Endurance Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 4 sets,
- moderate external resistance,
- 10 to 20 hand pulls,
- 90 to 180 seconds rest,
- consistent pace and posture.

---

# Work-Capacity Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 4 sets,
- 20 to 40 second intervals,
- moderate load,
- controlled cadence,
- no uncontrolled slipping,
- no excessive trunk compensation.

This prescription should not replace technical conditioning when the primary objective is sport-specific endurance.

# Standing Rope Pull

The athlete pulls from an athletic standing position.

Primary benefits:

- whole-body force transfer,
- trunk and lower-body stabilization,
- greater sport-transfer potential,
- scalable external loading.

---

# Seated Rope Pull

The athlete performs the movement seated.

Primary benefits:

- reduced lower-body contribution,
- greater upper-body emphasis,
- easier posture standardization,
- useful for precise pulling assessment.

---

# Kneeling Rope Pull

The athlete performs the movement from a tall-kneeling or half-kneeling position.

Primary benefits:

- greater trunk demand,
- reduced lower-body assistance,
- improved anti-rotation emphasis.

---

# Vertical Rope Pull

The rope is oriented upward, and the athlete pulls a load vertically or downward toward the body.

Primary benefits:

- vertical pulling emphasis,
- greater latissimus and scapular-depression demand,
- increased similarity to climbing mechanics.

This variation requires a safe pulley or anchor setup.

---

# Progressions

Progressions include:

1. longer pulling distance,
2. greater external load,
3. thicker rope,
4. smoother rope,
5. faster but controlled cadence,
6. reduced rest,
7. lower-support stance,
8. unilateral emphasis,
9. vertical pulling orientation,
10. repeated heavy efforts.

Only one or two variables should progress at a time.

---

# Regressions

Regressions include:

- lighter load,
- shorter distance,
- shorter work interval,
- seated position,
- thicker-friction rope,
- bilateral cable row with rope attachment,
- towel row,
- static rope hold,
- supported hand-over-hand pull.

---

# Recommended Progression Sequence

1. Static rope hold.
2. Light seated rope pull.
3. Standing rope pull.
4. Longer pulling distance.
5. Heavier load.
6. Reduced rest.
7. Kneeling or half-kneeling variation.
8. Thick-rope pull.
9. Vertical rope pull.
10. Heavy repeated hand-over-hand pulling.

---

# Fatigue Profile

## Systemic Fatigue

Moderate

Whole-body fatigue increases with standing variations, heavy sleds and long work intervals.

## Local Fatigue

High

Primary local fatigue occurs in:

- finger flexors,
- forearms,
- biceps,
- brachialis,
- brachioradialis,
- lats,
- and scapular retractors.

## Neurological Fatigue

Moderate

Heavy short sets may create substantial neural demand.

## Connective-Tissue Stress

Moderate to High

Stress may accumulate in:

- finger flexor tendons,
- thumb structures,
- elbow flexor tendons,
- wrist tissues,
- and shoulder structures.

---

# Recovery Cost

Recovery cost is moderate.

It increases with:

- heavy sled loads,
- long pulling distances,
- thick rope,
- repeated near-failure sets,
- fast cadence,
- and concurrent grappling volume.

Grip and elbow fatigue may persist even when general fatigue is low.

---

# Session Placement

The Rope Pull is usually placed:

- after explosive work,
- after primary strength lifts when those lifts are the priority,
- as a secondary grip-integrated pulling exercise,
- or in a general physical preparation block.

It should not normally precede:

- Olympic-lifting derivatives,
- heavy deadlifts,
- maximal pull-ups,
- intensive grappling,
- or technical sessions requiring fresh hands.

When Rope Pull performance is the primary objective, it may be placed earlier after warm-up.

---

# Weekly Placement

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 2 times per week.

A second exposure should usually use lower load or shorter duration.

High-frequency Rope Pull work is inappropriate when the athlete already has:

- high pulling volume,
- high grappling volume,
- persistent elbow irritation,
- or limited grip recovery.

---

# Interaction With Other Training

## Deadlifts

The Rope Pull can reduce grip readiness for heavy deadlifts.

Place it after deadlifts or on a separate day when deadlift performance is the priority.

## Rows and Pull-Ups

The exercise overlaps strongly with upper-back and elbow-flexor work.

Avoid redundant pulling volume.

## Loaded Carries

Carries and Rope Pulls both stress support grip.

Combined volume must remain recoverable.

## Grappling

Grappling already creates substantial gripping and pulling fatigue.

Gi-based disciplines create especially high overlap.

## Striking

Severe forearm fatigue may impair hand relaxation, wrist comfort and striking quality.

Avoid high-fatigue Rope Pull work before priority striking sessions.

---

# Combat Transfer

The Rope Pull supports combat performance by improving:

- sustained open-hand grip,
- repeated pulling force,
- hand-over-hand coordination,
- forearm endurance,
- scapular control,
- trunk stability,
- and force production under fatigue.

Its transfer is strongest when the athlete must repeatedly establish and maintain grip while drawing an opponent or object toward the body.

Technical pulling and grip-fighting skills remain sport-specific.

# Substitution Logic

## Substitute Rope Climb When

- bodyweight vertical pulling is the target,
- climbing skill is relevant,
- and the athlete has the required technical and safety capacity.

## Substitute Towel Pull-Up When

- the athlete needs grip-integrated pulling with simpler load control,
- vertical pulling is preferred,
- or space and equipment are limited.

## Substitute Cable Rope Row When

- precise loading is required,
- the athlete needs lower technical complexity,
- or hand-over-hand pulling is unnecessary.

## Substitute Farmer Carry When

- support grip is the main objective,
- pulling volume is already high,
- or locomotion under load is more relevant.

## Substitute Plate Pinch When

- pinch grip is the true limitation,
- thumb opposition is the priority,
- or open-hand support grip is not required.

# Progression Criteria

The athlete may progress when:

- every regrip is secure,
- the wrists remain controlled,
- the shoulders remain stable,
- the trunk stays braced,
- pulling rhythm remains consistent,
- no pain occurs,
- and recovery is complete before the next grip-intensive session.

---

# Regression Criteria

The exercise should be regressed when:

- the rope slips,
- the athlete cannot regrip securely,
- wrist alignment deteriorates,
- the shoulders shrug excessively,
- trunk rotation increases,
- pace becomes uncontrolled,
- pain appears,
- or local fatigue disrupts subsequent sport practice.

---

# Readiness Screen

Before prescription, assess:

- finger pain,
- thumb pain,
- wrist pain,
- elbow pain,
- shoulder pain,
- skin integrity,
- pulling competence,
- stance stability,
- previous grip-intensive training,
- and current grappling volume.

---

# Safety Rules

- Use a securely anchored rope.
- Inspect the rope before use.
- Verify sled or load attachment.
- Keep the pulling lane clear.
- Maintain a stable stance.
- Do not wrap the rope around the hand or wrist.
- Stop before grip failure.
- Avoid uncontrolled rope recoil.
- Protect the skin from excessive friction.
- Avoid maximal loading with irritated elbows or fingers.
- Terminate immediately if sharp pain, numbness or sudden weakness occurs.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine may select the Rope Pull when:

- grip-integrated pulling endurance is the intended adaptation,
- open-hand grip is a limiting factor,
- repeated hand-over-hand pulling is relevant,
- the athlete has adequate pulling and trunk control,
- equipment and setup are safe,
- and total grip volume remains recoverable.

The engine must avoid selecting it when:

- the athlete has active hand, wrist, elbow or shoulder pain,
- the athlete cannot regrip securely,
- pulling volume is already excessive,
- the setup is unsafe,
- or a simpler exercise better matches the objective.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When the Rope Pull is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the targeted grip and pulling functions,
- the reason hand-over-hand pulling is preferred,
- the selected stance,
- the external load,
- the prescribed distance, pulls or duration,
- the interaction with grappling and pulling volume,
- relevant hand, elbow, shoulder or trunk constraints,
- and the progression criterion.

Example:

> Rope Pull selected to develop repeated open-hand grip and upper-body pulling endurance. The athlete demonstrates pain-free horizontal pulling and secure rope handling. Three moderate-load standing pulls are prescribed after primary strength work to limit interference with the next grappling session.

---

# Summary

The Rope Pull is a moderate-complexity grip-integrated pulling exercise.

Its primary value lies in combining:

- open-hand support grip,
- repeated pulling strength,
- forearm endurance,
- scapular control,
- trunk stability,
- and hand-over-hand coordination.

Within CAS, it is appropriate when the athlete requires repeated grip and pulling capacity without the technical and safety demands of full rope climbing.

It must be programmed conservatively because local grip and elbow fatigue can accumulate rapidly even when systemic fatigue remains moderate.
