# Purpose

The Pinch Carry develops pinch-grip strength and endurance while requiring the athlete to maintain posture and control during locomotion.

Within CAS, it is used when the athlete must improve thumb contribution, finger opposition and the ability to preserve a secure grip on flat or narrow objects while moving.

The exercise combines local hand demand with:

- shoulder stabilization,
- trunk control,
- gait coordination,
- and whole-body tension.

It should not be selected merely as a more difficult version of a static plate pinch.

Its value depends on whether the athlete needs pinch strength under movement and can maintain safe control of the load.

# Movement Description

The athlete grips one or more weight plates, or a dedicated pinch block, between the thumb and fingers.

The load is lifted to the side of the body and maintained without hooking the fingers around an edge or allowing the implement to rest against the leg.

The athlete then walks for the prescribed distance or duration while maintaining:

- a strong pinch,
- a controlled wrist,
- upright posture,
- and stable load position.

The set ends before uncontrolled slipping or postural breakdown occurs.

---

# Starting Position

The athlete should begin with:

- the implement positioned safely beside the body,
- clean and dry gripping surfaces,
- the thumb on one side,
- the fingers on the opposite side,
- the wrist near neutral,
- the elbow extended or slightly flexed,
- the shoulder controlled,
- the trunk upright,
- and the feet in a stable stance.

For bilateral execution, the loads should be matched unless asymmetrical loading is intentional.

---

# Execution

1. Select the appropriate plate combination or pinch block.
2. Establish true pinch contact through the thumb and fingers.
3. Squeeze firmly before lifting.
4. Stand tall with the wrist controlled.
5. Keep the implement clear of the thigh.
6. Begin walking with short, controlled steps.
7. Maintain a level pelvis and quiet trunk.
8. Keep the load vertical and stable.
9. Complete the prescribed distance or duration.
10. Stop before the grip begins to fail.
11. Lower the implement under control.

---

# Key Technical Cues

- Pinch hard through the thumb.
- Keep the wrist neutral.
- Keep the plate vertical.
- Walk tall.
- Do not lean away from the load.
- Keep the load off the thigh.
- Take controlled steps.
- Stop before slipping.
- Lower under control.

# Grip Demand

The Pinch Carry primarily trains pinch grip.

Unlike support-grip carries, the athlete cannot wrap the fingers around a handle.

The movement therefore increases demand on:

- thumb opposition,
- fingertip pressure,
- finger adduction,
- hand-intrinsic musculature,
- and wrist stabilization.

Walking increases difficulty by introducing repeated movement and small positional disturbances.

---

# Technical Complexity

Low to Moderate

The grip task is simple, but locomotion increases technical demand.

The athlete must simultaneously control:

- the hand position,
- the wrist,
- the shoulder,
- the trunk,
- and the walking pattern.

The exercise should be introduced only after the athlete can perform a stable static plate pinch.

---

# Prerequisites

The athlete should demonstrate:

- pain-free static plate pinching,
- secure control of the selected implement,
- stable wrist position,
- adequate thumb and finger tolerance,
- controlled walking under load,
- and the ability to lower the load safely.

---

# Eligibility Criteria

The Pinch Carry is eligible when:

- pinch-grip endurance is a target,
- the athlete must maintain grip while moving,
- thumb contribution is underdeveloped,
- the athlete can control the load safely,
- trunk stability is adequate,
- and local hand fatigue will not compromise higher-priority work.

---

# Ineligibility Criteria

The exercise is not eligible when the athlete presents:

- acute thumb pain,
- finger-joint pain,
- recent hand injury,
- wrist pain,
- active pulley irritation,
- poor static pinch control,
- unsafe plate geometry,
- impaired balance,
- or inability to lower the load safely.

---

# Relative Contraindications

Use caution when the athlete has:

- chronic thumb-base irritation,
- previous finger-tendon injury,
- medial elbow pain,
- reduced sensation in the hand,
- skin tears,
- balance limitations,
- or high weekly gi-grappling volume.

The engine should reduce load, distance or frequency when these factors are present.

---

# Absolute Contraindications

Do not prescribe the exercise when there is:

- acute thumb injury,
- acute finger injury,
- acute wrist injury,
- neurological loss of grip control,
- inability to walk safely under load,
- or an unstable implement setup.

# Common Errors

## Hooking the Fingers

The athlete curls the fingers under the plate rim.

Risk:

- reduced pinch-grip stimulus,
- false performance assessment.

Correction:

- maintain flat finger contact against the plate surface.

## Wrist Collapse

The wrist flexes or deviates under load.

Risk:

- reduced force transfer,
- increased tendon stress.

Correction:

- reduce load and restore neutral alignment.

## Leaning Away From the Load

The athlete shifts the trunk excessively.

Risk:

- reduced postural quality,
- altered carry stimulus.

Correction:

- walk tall and maintain stacked posture.

## Resting the Load Against the Thigh

The athlete uses body contact to reduce hand demand.

Risk:

- reduced grip stimulus,
- inaccurate loading.

Correction:

- keep the implement clear of the leg.

## Excessive Walking Speed

The athlete rushes and loses control.

Risk:

- swinging,
- slipping,
- poor trunk stability.

Correction:

- use short, controlled steps.

## Continuing After Slipping Begins

The athlete attempts to complete the distance despite grip failure.

Risk:

- dropped plates,
- abrupt thumb loading,
- foot injury.

Correction:

- terminate the set immediately when security is lost.

## Load Rotation

The plate tilts or rotates during the carry.

Risk:

- uneven hand stress,
- sudden grip failure.

Correction:

- reduce load or distance and maintain vertical alignment.

# Coaching Priorities

1. Establish a secure true pinch.
2. Maintain a neutral wrist.
3. Keep the implement vertical.
4. Walk with controlled posture.
5. Prevent trunk leaning.
6. Keep the load clear of the body.
7. Use short, stable steps.
8. Stop before grip failure.
9. Lower the load under control.
10. Progress thumb stress gradually.

---

# Prescription Variables

## Distance

Typical range:

- 10 to 20 metres for strength emphasis,
- 20 to 40 metres for strength endurance,
- longer distances only for advanced athletes.

## Duration

Typical range:

- 10 to 20 seconds for strength,
- 20 to 40 seconds for strength endurance,
- 40 to 60 seconds for endurance.

## Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 5 sets.

## Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 3 minutes.

## Load

Load is determined by:

- plate weight,
- plate thickness,
- number of plates,
- implement width,
- surface friction,
- unilateral or bilateral execution,
- and prescribed distance or duration.

---

# Strength Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 5 sets,
- 10 to 20 metres,
- high but controlled load,
- 2 to 3 minutes rest,
- no uncontrolled slipping.

---

# Strength-Endurance Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 4 sets,
- 20 to 40 metres,
- moderate load,
- 90 to 180 seconds rest,
- consistent posture and wrist position.

---

# Endurance Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 4 sets,
- 40 to 60 seconds,
- conservative loading,
- controlled gait,
- termination before severe hand opening.

---

# Unilateral Variation

The athlete carries one implement on one side.

Primary benefits:

- independent hand assessment,
- asymmetry identification,
- greater anti-lateral-flexion demand,
- targeted side-to-side correction.

The athlete must not lean away from the load.

---

# Bilateral Variation

The athlete carries one implement in each hand.

Primary benefits:

- efficient bilateral training,
- reduced trunk asymmetry,
- greater total pinch volume,
- simpler gait control.

---

# Offset Variation

The athlete carries different loads or uses different grip widths between hands.

Primary benefits:

- asymmetrical trunk challenge,
- side-specific grip loading,
- advanced object-control demand.

This variation is reserved for athletes with established technical control.

---

# Progressions

Progressions include:

1. longer carry distance,
2. greater hold duration,
3. heavier load,
4. thicker plate combination,
5. smoother plate surfaces,
6. unilateral execution,
7. reduced rest,
8. slower controlled walking,
9. offset loading,
10. more demanding pinch blocks.

Only one or two variables should progress at a time.

---

# Regressions

Regressions include:

- lighter plates,
- shorter distances,
- shorter duration,
- bilateral execution,
- textured surfaces,
- static plate pinch,
- partial unloading with the opposite hand,
- pinch block with adjustable load.

---

# Recommended Progression Sequence

1. Static bilateral plate pinch.
2. Static unilateral plate pinch.
3. Short bilateral pinch carry.
4. Short unilateral pinch carry.
5. Longer-distance carry.
6. Heavier plate combination.
7. Smooth-surface carry.
8. Offset carry.
9. High-load short-distance carry.

# Fatigue Profile

## Systemic Fatigue

Low to Moderate

The exercise creates limited cardiovascular and whole-body fatigue at moderate loads.

## Local Fatigue

High

Primary fatigue occurs in:

- thumb musculature,
- finger flexors,
- hand intrinsics,
- forearm stabilizers,
- and shoulder-support musculature.

## Neurological Fatigue

Low to Moderate

Short heavy carries may create meaningful local neural demand.

## Connective-Tissue Stress

Moderate

Stress accumulates in:

- thumb tendons,
- finger flexor tendons,
- wrist stabilizers,
- and elbow flexor-pronator structures.

---

# Recovery Cost

Recovery cost is generally low systemically but moderate locally.

It increases with:

- maximal loading,
- long distances,
- high weekly frequency,
- smooth surfaces,
- unilateral carries,
- and concurrent grappling volume.

Local pinch strength may remain reduced after whole-body fatigue has resolved.

---

# Session Placement

The Pinch Carry is usually placed:

- after primary strength exercises,
- after technical pulling work,
- near the end of the session,
- or in a dedicated grip block.

It should not usually precede:

- heavy deadlifts,
- Olympic-lifting derivatives,
- maximal pull-ups,
- high-priority grappling,
- or technical sessions requiring fresh hands.

---

# Weekly Placement

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 2 times per week.

A third exposure may be appropriate only when:

- loading is moderate,
- total distance is low,
- the athlete has strong tissue tolerance,
- and sport practice does not already impose high pinch demand.

---

# Interaction With Other Training

## Deadlifts

The Pinch Carry may reduce hand readiness for heavy pulling.

Place it after deadlifts when deadlift performance is the priority.

## Pull-Ups and Rows

The exercise adds local hand and forearm fatigue.

Avoid redundant high-volume pulling.

## Farmer Carry

Farmer carries emphasize support grip, while pinch carries emphasize thumb and finger opposition.

The two may complement one another if total grip volume remains controlled.

## Grappling

Gi-based grappling already creates substantial pinch and finger stress.

The engine must include sport practice in total hand volume.

## Striking

Severe thumb and forearm fatigue may impair hand relaxation, glove comfort and wrist stability.

Avoid high-fatigue pinch carries before priority striking sessions.

---

# Combat Transfer

The Pinch Carry supports combat performance by improving:

- thumb contribution,
- finger opposition,
- grip maintenance under movement,
- control of flat or narrow surfaces,
- wrist stability,
- and hand strength under locomotor disturbance.

Transfer is strongest when the athlete must preserve grip while repositioning the body.

Technical gripping skill remains discipline-specific.

# Substitution Logic

## Substitute Static Plate Pinch When

- locomotion is unnecessary,
- the athlete lacks stable carry mechanics,
- loading must be isolated to the hand,
- or a lower-complexity option is required.

## Substitute Farmer Carry When

- support grip is the actual target,
- whole-body loading is a priority,
- or heavier external load is desired.

## Substitute Suitcase Carry When

- trunk anti-lateral-flexion capacity is the main objective,
- pinch grip is not the limiting factor,
- or handle-based grip is more appropriate.

## Substitute Pinch Block Hold When

- precise loading is required,
- plate geometry is unsuitable,
- or static strength is the primary goal.

## Substitute Towel Carry or Towel Hang When

- open-hand support grip is the target,
- sport transfer requires deformable gripping,
- or shoulder-integrated grip work is preferred.

# Progression Criteria

The athlete may progress when:

- the implement remains fully controlled,
- the wrist stays neutral,
- posture remains stable,
- gait remains controlled,
- no body contact occurs,
- no pain is present,
- and the set ends before uncontrolled slipping.

---

# Regression Criteria

The exercise should be regressed when:

- the implement slips early,
- the wrist collapses,
- trunk leaning increases,
- gait becomes unstable,
- the athlete hooks the plate rim,
- thumb or finger pain appears,
- or local fatigue compromises technical practice.

---

# Readiness Screen

Before prescription, assess:

- thumb pain,
- finger pain,
- wrist pain,
- elbow pain,
- skin condition,
- balance,
- recent grip-intensive training,
- current grappling volume,
- and ability to perform a static pinch hold safely.

---

# Safety Rules

- Use intact plates or secure pinch blocks.
- Keep the walking area clear.
- Wear suitable footwear.
- Keep the load close to the body but off the thigh.
- Do not carry plates directly over the feet.
- Stop before uncontrolled slipping.
- Do not use oily or damaged equipment.
- Turn only under full control.
- Lower the load safely.
- Terminate immediately if sharp pain, numbness or sudden weakness occurs.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine may select the Pinch Carry when:

- dynamic pinch-grip endurance is the intended adaptation,
- thumb contribution is a limiting factor,
- the athlete must maintain grip while moving,
- static pinch competency is established,
- hand and wrist readiness are adequate,
- and total grip volume remains recoverable.

The engine must avoid selecting it when:

- the athlete has active thumb, finger or wrist pain,
- the athlete cannot perform a secure static pinch,
- support grip is the actual target,
- locomotion creates unnecessary complexity,
- or local fatigue would compromise a higher-priority session.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When the Pinch Carry is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the pinch-grip limitation targeted,
- the reason a carry is preferred over a static hold,
- the selected implement and load,
- the prescribed distance or duration,
- unilateral or bilateral execution,
- the interaction with grappling and pulling volume,
- relevant thumb, finger, wrist or balance constraints,
- and the progression criterion.

Example:

> Pinch Carry selected to develop pinch-grip endurance under movement for gi control. The athlete demonstrates secure static plate pinches and stable loaded-carry mechanics. Three short bilateral carries are prescribed after primary strength work to limit interference with the next grappling session.

---

# Summary

The Pinch Carry is a grip-focused loaded carry that primarily develops pinch strength and endurance under movement.

Its main value lies in improving:

- thumb opposition,
- finger pressure,
- hand-intrinsic strength,
- wrist stability,
- postural control,
- and grip maintenance while walking.

Within CAS, it is appropriate when the athlete needs stronger dynamic pinch capacity and has already established safe static pinch control.

It must be progressed conservatively because local thumb and finger stress can accumulate rapidly despite relatively low systemic fatigue.
