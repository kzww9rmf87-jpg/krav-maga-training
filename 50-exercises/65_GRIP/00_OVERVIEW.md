# GRIP OVERVIEW

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Grip category defines exercises used to improve the athlete’s ability to hold, crush, support, control and transfer force through the hands and forearms.

Within CAS, grip is treated as a force-transmission capacity rather than an isolated forearm attribute.

A combat athlete must be able to maintain connection with an opponent, resist grip disruption, control implements, stabilize the wrist during striking and preserve hand function under fatigue.

Grip exercises are therefore selected according to the specific gripping action required, the joint positions involved, the expected duration of effort and the broader recovery cost.

They are not selected merely to create forearm fatigue.

Their value depends on matching the exercise to the required grip function and integrating it without compromising technical training, pulling strength or hand health.

---

# Philosophy

The hands are the final point of contact in many athletic actions.

Force generated by the legs, hips, trunk and upper body becomes useful only when the athlete can transmit it through a stable wrist and an effective grip.

In grappling, insufficient grip can end an otherwise dominant position.

In striking, poor wrist stability can reduce force transfer and increase injury risk.

In strength training, grip can become the limiting factor before the target musculature receives the intended stimulus.

CAS therefore treats grip as a multi-dimensional capability.

The governing principle is:

> Train the grip function required by the task while protecting hand health, technical quality and total pulling capacity.

Grip training must remain specific enough to create useful adaptation, but general enough to avoid unnecessary overuse.

# Core Principle

Grip is not one single physical quality.

An athlete may possess a strong support grip while having limited pinch strength.

Another athlete may demonstrate high crushing force but lose grip rapidly during prolonged grappling exchanges.

CAS therefore separates grip into distinct functions instead of treating all hand exercises as interchangeable.

The primary grip functions are:

- crushing,
- supporting,
- pinching,
- open-hand gripping,
- wrist stabilization,
- and grip endurance.

Exercise selection must begin with the required function.

---

# Grip Function Categories

## Crushing Grip

Crushing grip is the ability to close the fingers forcefully toward the palm.

Primary qualities:

- finger-flexor force,
- hand-closing strength,
- short-duration maximal gripping.

Typical applications:

- forceful hand control,
- squeezing handles,
- resisting grip separation,
- gripping small-diameter objects.

## Support Grip

Support grip is the ability to maintain a closed hand around an object while external load attempts to open the fingers.

Primary qualities:

- sustained finger-flexor force,
- load-bearing hand strength,
- isometric endurance.

Typical applications:

- loaded carries,
- deadlifts,
- pull-up variations,
- prolonged control of an opponent or implement.

## Pinch Grip

Pinch grip is the ability to hold an object between the thumb and fingers without wrapping the hand fully around it.

Primary qualities:

- thumb opposition,
- finger pressure,
- hand-intrinsic strength.

Typical applications:

- plate pinches,
- edge gripping,
- control of fabric or narrow surfaces,
- improved thumb contribution to total grip.

## Open-Hand Grip

Open-hand grip is the ability to generate and maintain force when the fingers cannot close completely around the object.

Primary qualities:

- strength at longer finger-flexor muscle lengths,
- thick-handle control,
- large-surface gripping.

Typical applications:

- gi and clothing grips,
- wrist control,
- thick handles,
- grappling with partially open fingers.

## Wrist Stabilization

Wrist stabilization is the ability to resist unwanted flexion, extension, radial deviation, ulnar deviation and rotation.

Primary qualities:

- force transfer through the hand,
- striking alignment,
- implement control,
- joint protection.

Typical applications:

- punches,
- weapon control,
- clinch fighting,
- loaded carries,
- pressing and pulling.

## Grip Endurance

Grip endurance is the ability to maintain useful gripping force over time or across repeated efforts.

Primary qualities:

- local muscular endurance,
- repeated isometric force,
- fatigue resistance.

Typical applications:

- extended grappling exchanges,
- repeated clinch efforts,
- rope work,
- high-volume pulling.

# Global Exercise Roles

## Gripper-Based Exercises

Primary role:

- crushing-strength development.

Primary characteristics:

- highly specific hand-closing action,
- simple loading progression,
- low whole-body fatigue,
- potentially high local tendon stress.

## Pinch Exercises

Primary role:

- thumb and finger opposition strength.

Primary characteristics:

- high thumb contribution,
- limited load tolerance compared with support grip,
- strong specificity to edge and plate control.

## Hang Variations

Primary role:

- support grip and shoulder-integrated hanging capacity.

Primary characteristics:

- bodyweight loading,
- simultaneous shoulder demand,
- scalable duration,
- significant local endurance component.

## Thick-Handle Holds

Primary role:

- open-hand support strength.

Primary characteristics:

- increased finger opening,
- reduced mechanical advantage,
- high transfer to large-object control.

## Wrist and Hand-Robustness Exercises

Primary role:

- support tissue capacity and joint resilience.

Primary characteristics:

- lower external load,
- controlled tempo,
- multi-directional wrist demand,
- preventive rather than maximal-strength emphasis.

# Physiological Profile

Grip training can target several distinct physiological outcomes.

## Maximum Grip Strength

Characterized by:

- high force,
- short effort duration,
- long recovery,
- low repetition count,
- high neural demand.

## Grip Endurance

Characterized by:

- moderate force,
- longer holds,
- repeated efforts,
- high local metabolic stress.

## Tendon and Tissue Capacity

Characterized by:

- controlled loading,
- moderate intensity,
- gradual progression,
- repeated exposure without failure.

## Forearm Hypertrophy

Characterized by:

- moderate to high local volume,
- repeated contractions,
- sufficient range or duration,
- accumulated local fatigue.

CAS must identify the intended adaptation before assigning volume or intensity.

---

# Neurological Profile

High-intensity grip work requires substantial motor-unit recruitment in the finger flexors and forearm musculature.

Maximal crushing and support efforts:

- maximal local force,
- high-threshold motor-unit recruitment,
- local muscular endurance,
- tendon adaptation,
- repeated-isometric capacity,
- and hypertrophy of the forearm musculature.

The physiological outcome depends on:

- resistance,
- duration,
- contraction type,
- handle diameter,
- friction,
- wrist position,
- rest interval,
- and proximity to local failure.

Short maximal efforts emphasize strength.

Long holds emphasize endurance.

High-repetition wrist work creates substantial local metabolic stress.

CAS must distinguish these objectives explicitly.

---

# Neurological Profile

Maximal grip training requires strong neural drive to relatively small muscle groups.

Key neurological qualities include:

- rapid motor-unit recruitment,
- synchronization,
- forceful voluntary contraction,
- hand-position awareness,
- and coordination between the fingers, thumb and wrist.

Grip performance is sensitive to pain, skin condition, prior pulling volume and accumulated local fatigue.

Loss of coordination or sharp discomfort is not an acceptable training objective.

Risk increases when volume rises too quickly or when the athlete has existing pulley, tendon or joint irritation.

## Thumb

Pinch and open-hand tasks place high demand on thumb opposition and stabilization.

Thumb pain must not be ignored.

## Wrist

The wrist should generally remain controlled and close to neutral unless the exercise intentionally trains deviation or flexion-extension.

Excessive uncontrolled wrist movement reduces force transfer and may aggravate tendons.

## Elbow

The wrist flexor and extensor tendons cross the elbow.

Excessive grip volume can contribute to medial or lateral elbow irritation.

## Shoulder

Hang-based grip exercises also load the shoulder complex.

Shoulder tolerance must be assessed separately from hand capacity.

---

# Technical Complexity Classification

## Low Complexity

Typical examples:

- simple gripper work,
- plate pinch holds,
- static bar holds,
- finger extension work.

These exercises are easy to understand but still require appropriate loading and wrist alignment.

## Moderate Complexity

Typical examples:

- towel hangs,
- thick-handle holds,
- wrist-roller variations,
- loaded holds integrated with other movements.

These require greater positional control and may involve additional shoulder or trunk demands.

## High Complexity

Grip tasks become high complexity when combined with:

- dynamic locomotion,
- unstable implements,
- rotational demands,
- technical grappling patterns,
- or multi-joint explosive actions.

CAS should not increase complexity unless the added demand serves a clear purpose.

---

# Intensity Determinants

Grip-exercise intensity may be determined by:

- external load,
- handle diameter,
- surface friction,
- hold duration,
- finger position,
- thumb involvement,
- implement shape,
- unilateral versus bilateral execution,
- bodyweight percentage,
- and proximity to grip failure.

Grip intensity is not represented accurately by external load alone.

A lighter thick handle may be more demanding than a heavier standard handle.

A smooth plate may be harder to pinch than a textured implement.

# General Prescription Ranges

## Maximum Grip Strength

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 5 repetitions or short maximal efforts,
- 3 to 6 work sets,
- 3 to 10 seconds per effort,
- 2 to 4 minutes recovery,
- no technical breakdown.

## Grip Strength Endurance

Typical prescription:

- 15 to 45 second holds,
- 2 to 5 sets,
- 60 to 120 seconds recovery,
- submaximal effort maintained with consistent position.

## Long-Duration Grip Endurance

Typical prescription:

- 30 to 90 second efforts,
- 2 to 4 sets,
- conservative loading,
- termination before severe hand opening or joint discomfort.

## Tissue Capacity

Typical prescription:

- 8 to 20 controlled repetitions,
- 2 to 4 sets,
- low to moderate loading,
- progressive exposure across multiple directions.

## Technical or Recovery Work

Typical prescription:

- low intensity,
- 1 to 3 sets,
- no grinding,
- no persistent forearm pump,
- emphasis on circulation and movement quality.

## Grip Repetition Strength

### Purpose

Grip-integrated pulling and pressing strength, where a complete repetition
of a whole movement is the unit of work and grip difficulty is the
dominant constraint on that movement.

This category exists because the ranges above cannot express it. Maximum
Grip Strength counts short maximal efforts, Grip Strength Endurance and
Long-Duration Grip Endurance count seconds of holding, and Tissue Capacity
counts controlled repetitions at low to moderate loading for progressive
exposure. None of them describes a near-maximal movement whose repetition
ceiling is set by the hands rather than by the prime movers.

### Admissible Exercises

Exercises whose prescribed unit is a **complete repetition of the whole
movement**, and whose limiting factor is grip security rather than the
prime movers.

Towel Pull-Up is the first documented example. The category is defined by
the unit and the limiting factor, not by that exercise: any future grip
exercise counted in complete repetitions belongs here.

### Excluded Exercises

This category must not absorb work counted in any other unit, in keeping
with the Volume Metrics rule below:

- rope ascents — a climb is a bout composed of many hand transitions, not
  a repetition of a movement;
- hand-over-hand pulls — a repetition of one hand's action, not of the
  whole movement;
- distance travelled — Loaded Carries cover that;
- timed holds and isometric variations — Grip Strength Endurance and
  Long-Duration Grip Endurance cover those;
- timed work intervals and conditioning intervals;
- gripper closes and finger contacts.

An exercise may contribute one of its prescriptions to this category
without every one of its variations belonging to it.

### Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 5 sets.

### Repetitions

Typical range:

- 2 to 8 complete repetitions,
- the lower half for strength emphasis,
- the upper half for strength-endurance emphasis.

### Intensity

Expressed as repetitions in reserve:

- 1 to 3 repetitions in reserve.

Repetitions in reserve is the honest metric here because grip work is not
represented accurately by external load alone, as the Intensity
Determinants above state. A repetition remains available only while the
grip remains secure, so the reserve is counted against grip security, not
against the prime movers.

### Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 4 minutes,
- the upper half for strength emphasis,
- the lower half for strength-endurance emphasis.

### Tempo

Controlled throughout, with a controlled descent.

The descent must never be uncontrolled: releasing tension abruptly under a
grip-limited hold is both a technical failure and a safety failure.
Exercise records may document a specific eccentric duration; that phase
timing belongs in the exercise's own instructions.

### Progression

- repetition count within the range,
- grip difficulty of the implement,
- reduced assistance,
- added external load only for well-adapted athletes,
- never by sacrificing controlled descent.

### Limits of Use

This category must not be used:

- when the prescribed unit is anything other than a complete repetition;
- when the limiting factor is the prime movers rather than the grip — that
  is ordinary strength work and belongs to the Strength module;
- to prescribe an isometric variation of the same exercise;
- to accumulate volume once grip security is lost, which terminates the
  set regardless of the repetitions remaining.

## Grip Climb Strength

### Purpose

Grip-integrated climbing strength, where a complete ascent is the unit of
work and grip security is what ends the effort.

An ascent is not a repetition. It is a bout composed of many hand
transitions, and the Volume Metrics rule below forbids counting it as one.
It needs its own category for that reason alone.

### Admissible Exercises

Exercises whose prescribed unit is a **complete ascent** of a climbable
implement, and whose limiting factor is grip security rather than the prime
movers.

Rope Climb is the first documented example. Any future climbing exercise
counted in complete ascents belongs here.

### Excluded Exercises

- work counted in hand-over-hand pulls — Grip Hand-Pull Work covers that;
- work counted in complete repetitions of a whole movement — Grip
  Repetition Strength covers that;
- timed holds, carried distance and timed intervals.

Climbed HEIGHT is a documented variable of the exercise, never a volume
dimension of this category: an ascent is counted, its height is described.

### Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 5 sets.

### Climbs

Typical range:

- 1 to 5 complete ascents per set.

### Intensity

Expressed as technical effort, at high quality.

No repetitions-in-reserve figure is available here and none is invented: an
ascent cannot be left partly in reserve, because a climb abandoned mid-rope
is a descent under compromised grip, which the safety rules of this category
forbid. What the records document instead is the technical standard the
effort must preserve, so that is what the category prescribes.

### Rest

Typical range:

- 2 to 5 minutes.

### Tempo

Controlled ascent and controlled descent.

The descent is never a drop: grip is at its weakest at the end of an ascent,
and that is precisely when an uncontrolled descent occurs.

### Progression

- number of ascents,
- climbed height,
- reduced assistance (foot lock, leg drive, partial height, seated start),
- rope characteristics,
- never by descending under exhausted grip.

### Limits of Use

This category must not be used:

- when the prescribed unit is anything other than a complete ascent;
- to prescribe partial climbs counted as hand transitions;
- to accumulate volume once grip security is lost, which terminates the set
  regardless of the ascents remaining.

## Grip Hand-Pull Work

### Purpose

Grip-integrated pulling where the athlete draws a resistance in
hand-over-hand fashion, and one pull of one hand is the unit of work.

A hand-over-hand pull is one hand's action, not one execution of a whole
movement. It is therefore neither a repetition nor an ascent, and the Volume
Metrics rule below forbids treating it as either.

### Admissible Exercises

Exercises whose prescribed unit is a **hand-over-hand pull** and whose
limiting factor is grip security.

Rope Pull is the first documented example. Any future hand-over-hand pulling
exercise counted in pulls belongs here.

### Excluded Exercises

- complete ascents — Grip Climb Strength covers those;
- complete repetitions of a whole movement — Grip Repetition Strength
  covers those;
- the same exercise prescribed by distance travelled — Loaded Carries cover
  distance;
- the same exercise prescribed as timed work intervals.

An exercise may contribute one of its prescriptions to this category without
its distance-based or interval-based prescriptions belonging to it.

### Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 5 sets.

### Hand Pulls

Typical range:

- 6 to 20 hand-over-hand pulls per set.

### Intensity

Expressed as technical effort, at high quality.

External resistance is a documented determinant here — sled weight, rope
angle, friction, rope diameter and pulling position all change the demand —
but the records qualify it in words rather than figures, and the Intensity
Determinants above already state that grip intensity is not represented
accurately by external load alone. A qualitative resistance level is
therefore not encoded as a number; what is prescribed is the technical
standard the pulls must preserve.

### Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 4 minutes.

### Tempo

Controlled throughout, at a consistent cadence.

No uncontrolled slipping, and no trunk compensation to replace grip.

### Progression

- number of hand pulls,
- external resistance,
- rope diameter and friction,
- pulling position,
- never by allowing the rope to slip through the hands.

### Limits of Use

This category must not be used:

- when the prescribed unit is anything other than a hand-over-hand pull;
- to prescribe the same exercise by distance or by timed interval;
- to accumulate volume once grip security is lost.

# Volume Metrics

Grip volume may be tracked through:

- total hold time,
- number of maximal closes,
- total loaded seconds,
- total repetitions,
- total distance under carry,
- number of finger contacts,
- or accumulated work sets.

CAS must avoid combining incompatible volume measures without context.

For example, ten maximal gripper closes and sixty seconds of towel hanging are not equivalent simply because both count as one set.

---

# Placement Within the Session

Grip work is usually placed:

- after primary technical and strength work,
- after exercises where grip must not be pre-fatigued,
- or in a separate low-volume micro-session.

Grip work may be placed earlier only when grip strength is the primary objective.

It should generally not precede:

- heavy deadlifts,
- weighted pull-ups,
- high-skill Olympic-lifting derivatives,
- intensive grappling,
- or striking sessions requiring fresh hands.

---

# Frequency

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 3 targeted grip exposures per week.

Higher frequency may be appropriate for low-intensity tissue work.

Maximal grip work should be used more conservatively.

Frequency must reflect:

- total pulling volume,
- grappling volume,
- hand and elbow health,
- occupational hand use,
- and recovery capacity.

---

# Readiness Requirements

Grip training should be reduced, modified or omitted when the athlete presents:

- finger-joint pain,
- thumb pain,
- wrist pain,
- medial or lateral elbow irritation,
- tendon soreness,
- reduced hand-closing ability,
- unexplained loss of grip strength,
- recent hand trauma,
- severe forearm fatigue,
- or upcoming high-priority grappling work.

Local tissue readiness is more important than motivation.

---

# Progression Logic

Grip progression may occur through:

1. improved technique and wrist alignment,
2. longer hold duration,
3. greater external load,
4. larger handle diameter,
5. reduced surface friction,
6. greater thumb demand,
7. unilateral loading,
8. more difficult hand position,
9. increased total volume,
10. reduced assistance.

Only one or two variables should usually progress at a time.

Progression must remain slow enough to allow tendon and connective-tissue adaptation.

---

# Regression Logic

Grip exercises should be regressed by:

- reducing load,
- shortening hold duration,
- using a smaller handle,
- improving friction,
- using bilateral instead of unilateral execution,
- adding assistance,
- reducing range,
- reducing weekly frequency,
- or replacing maximal work with submaximal isometrics.

Regression is appropriate when technique, tissue tolerance or recovery is insufficient.

---

# Exercise Selection Logic

## Select Crushing-Grip Work When

- maximal hand-closing force is a priority,
- the athlete lacks strength with standard-diameter objects,
- low whole-body fatigue is desired,
- and finger or elbow tissues tolerate concentrated loading.

## Select Support-Grip Work When

- deadlift, carry or hanging performance is limited by the hands,
- prolonged object control is required,
- or grappling exchanges demand sustained grip.

## Select Pinch-Grip Work When

- thumb contribution is underdeveloped,
- edge or plate control is relevant,
- or the athlete requires balanced hand strength beyond finger flexion.

## Select Open-Hand Work When

- thick handles, clothing grips or wrist control are relevant,
- the athlete is strong only in fully closed positions,
- or greater long-length finger-flexor strength is required.

## Select Wrist-Stability Work When

- striking alignment is inconsistent,
- the athlete has difficulty controlling implements,
- or wrist robustness is a limiting factor.

## Select Grip-Endurance Work When

- the athlete loses control during prolonged exchanges,
- repeated pulling efforts are limited by forearm fatigue,
- or sustained gripping is more important than peak force.

---

# Grip Versus Forearm Hypertrophy

Grip strength and forearm size overlap but are not identical.

Hypertrophy-oriented work may improve force potential, but larger forearms do not guarantee task-specific gripping ability.

Maximal grip strength requires:

- neural recruitment,
- specific hand position,
- appropriate tendon stiffness,
- and force coordination.

CAS therefore does not use forearm hypertrophy as a substitute for grip training when grip is the limiting capability.

---

# Grip Versus Pulling Strength

Pulling exercises often train grip, but their primary adaptation may occur elsewhere.

A row or pull-up can improve support grip indirectly, yet the grip may remain underloaded if straps are used or if the back fatigues first.

Conversely, direct grip fatigue can reduce pulling performance.

CAS must distinguish:

- grip as a primary target,
- grip as a secondary adaptation,
- and grip as a limiting factor.

---

# Grip Versus Carries

Carries train support grip while also loading the trunk, shoulders and lower body.

They are efficient when multiple adaptations are desired.

Direct grip exercises are preferable when:

- local hand strength is the main objective,
- whole-body fatigue must remain low,
- or a specific grip function requires isolation.

Carries and direct grip work should complement rather than automatically duplicate each other.

---

# Combat Transfer

Grip contributes to combat performance through several mechanisms:

- maintaining wrist and limb control,
- retaining clothing grips,
- resisting grip breaks,
- sustaining clinch connection,
- controlling weapons or training implements,
- stabilizing the wrist during strikes,
- and preserving hand function under fatigue.

Transfer depends on the similarity between the trained grip function and the sport demand.

General grip strength supports performance, but technical gripping skill remains sport-specific.

---

## Boxing

Primary transfer:

- wrist stability,
- force transfer through the fist,
- hand and forearm robustness.

Excessive grip fatigue before punching work is undesirable.

## Kickboxing and Muay Thai

Primary transfer:

- wrist stability,
- clinch hand control,
- forearm endurance,
- glove-based gripping capacity.

## MMA

Primary transfer:

- wrist control,
- clinch retention,
- grappling support grip,
- repeated gripping under fatigue.

## Wrestling

Primary transfer:

- wrist and hand fighting,
- underhook and tie control,
- prolonged isometric gripping,
- grip endurance.

## Judo

Primary transfer:

- gi gripping,
- open-hand strength,
- pinch and support endurance,
- resistance to grip breaks.

## Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

Primary transfer:

- gi or no-gi hand control,
- sleeve and collar retention,
- wrist control,

## Krav Maga

Primary transfer:

- wrist and limb control,
- weapon retention and disarming mechanics,
- striking alignment,
- rapid establishment of hand control.

---

# Fatigue Cost

Grip exercises generally create low systemic fatigue but can generate high local fatigue.

Local fatigue may impair:

- deadlift performance,
- pull-ups,
- grappling,
- weapon handling,
- punching comfort,
- fine motor control,
- and recovery of the elbow tendons.

Because the muscles are small, athletes often underestimate the accumulated stress.

CAS must account for local fatigue even when heart rate and whole-body exertion remain low.

---

# Recovery Interaction

Grip recovery is influenced by:

- grappling volume,
- heavy pulling,
- loaded carries,
- manual work,
- climbing,
- racket or stick sports,
- and repeated device or tool use.

Grip work may be reduced when the athlete has:

- multiple grappling sessions,
- high deadlift volume,
- extensive carry work,
- or persistent elbow and forearm tightness.

Recovery decisions must consider the hands, wrists and elbows as one connected system.

---

# Safety Constraints

Grip exercises must be stopped or modified when they produce:

- sharp finger pain,
- thumb instability,
- wrist pain,
- numbness or tingling,
- tendon pain at the elbow,
- sudden loss of hand strength,
- swelling,
- or abnormal joint clicking with pain.

Maximal grip work must not be prescribed through active hand injury.

Hang-based exercises require independent shoulder screening.

High-frequency finger work must progress conservatively.

---

# Common Errors Across the Category

Common errors include:

- training every set to failure,
- using excessive weekly frequency,
- ignoring wrist alignment,
- treating all grip types as equivalent,
- progressing load faster than tendon tolerance,
- adding grip work on top of high grappling volume,
- using pain as a sign of productive work,
- neglecting finger-extensor training,
- and pre-fatiguing the grip before priority pulling exercises.

---

# Coaching Priorities

Primary coaching priorities are:

1. identify the intended grip function,
2. maintain a controlled wrist position,
3. apply force through the whole hand,
4. avoid unnecessary joint distortion,
5. stop before uncontrolled hand opening,
6. preserve consistent effort quality,
7. progress connective-tissue stress gradually,
8. monitor hand and elbow symptoms.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine must:

- identify the required grip function before exercise selection,
- distinguish maximal strength from endurance,
- account for grappling and pulling volume,
- protect high-priority technical sessions,
- avoid redundant local fatigue,
- screen hand, wrist and elbow readiness,
- apply conservative progression,
- and provide a clear reason for selecting direct grip work.

The engine must not prescribe grip training solely because forearm work is absent from the session.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When a grip exercise is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the grip function targeted,
- the athlete limitation addressed,
- the reason the chosen exercise is eligible,
- the expected adaptation,
- the intended intensity or duration,
- the interaction with pulling and grappling volume,
- relevant pain or readiness constraints,
- and the progression or regression rule.

Example decision logic:

> Support-grip work selected because the athlete’s pulling performance is limited by premature hand opening. The exercise is placed after primary strength work and prescribed below failure to avoid compromising the next grappling session.

---

# Summary

The Grip category develops the athlete’s ability to create, maintain and transmit force through the hands and forearms.

CAS treats grip as a collection of distinct functions rather than a single quality.

Effective grip programming requires:

- function-specific exercise selection,
- appropriate intensity and duration,
- gradual tissue progression,
- protection of hand and elbow health,
- coordination with pulling and grappling volume,
- and preservation of priority technical work.

Grip training is successful when the athlete can maintain stronger, more stable and more durable contact without unnecessary local fatigue or injury risk.
