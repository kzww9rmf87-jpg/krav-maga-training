# HANGING LEG RAISE

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Hanging Leg Raise is a suspended dynamic trunk exercise used to develop anterior-core strength, pelvic control, hip-flexion strength and grip endurance.

Within CAS, it is primarily selected as an advanced bodyweight core-strength exercise.

It requires the athlete to raise the legs while maintaining control of the pelvis, ribcage and shoulder girdle and while minimizing uncontrolled swinging.

---

# Philosophy

Combat athletes must be able to generate force through the trunk while the body is unsupported and the limbs move through large ranges of motion.

The Hanging Leg Raise develops this capacity by combining active suspension, trunk flexion, posterior pelvic tilt and long-lever leg movement.

CAS does not treat the exercise as a simple hip-flexor drill or as a test of how high the feet can travel.

The objective is to produce controlled pelvic motion and trunk tension while preserving shoulder integrity, grip security and movement quality.

# Execution Standard

## Starting Position

Hang from a stable pull-up bar with a secure pronated or neutral grip.

The arms remain extended unless a specific supported variation is prescribed.

Establish an active shoulder position with the shoulder girdle controlled rather than passively collapsing into the joints.

Bring the ribs down and create light whole-body tension.

The legs begin still and aligned beneath the body.

## Raising Phase

Initiate the movement by controlling the pelvis rather than throwing the legs forward.

Raise the knees or straight legs according to the prescribed variation.

Continue the movement by producing posterior pelvic tilt and shortening the distance between the ribcage and pelvis.

Avoid using momentum from the shoulders, spine or legs.

## Top Position

Reach the prescribed height without losing shoulder position or creating uncontrolled spinal motion.

For a strict hanging leg raise, the thighs should reach at least horizontal while the pelvis visibly rotates posteriorly.

For advanced toe-to-bar variations, the feet may approach or contact the bar only if the movement remains controlled.

## Lowering Phase

Lower the legs under control.

Maintain abdominal tension as the legs move away from the torso.

Return to a stable dead-hang or active-hang position without swinging.

The next repetition begins only after the body is controlled.

# Technical Failure Criteria

The set is terminated when any of the following occurs:

The athlete relies on repeated swinging to raise the legs.

The pelvis no longer rotates posteriorly.

The movement becomes almost entirely hip flexion without trunk contribution.

The athlete loses grip security.

The shoulders collapse into an uncontrolled passive hang.

The elbows bend substantially without prescription.

The lumbar spine extends excessively at the bottom.

The eccentric phase cannot be controlled.

The athlete kicks or jerks to complete the repetition.

Pain occurs in the shoulder, elbow, wrist, lumbar spine or hip.

Potential applications include:

Kicking preparation

Knee-strike mechanics

Clinch posture

Guard and ground-fighting leg control

Body stabilization during hanging or pulling actions

CAS does not classify the exercise as a direct technical simulation of a strike, kick or grappling action.

Its transfer is structural and coordinative rather than movement-specific.

# CAS Selection Logic

CAS may select the Hanging Leg Raise when:

The target adaptation includes advanced core strength.

The athlete has adequate hanging and grip capacity.

The athlete can perform strict knee raises without swing.

A bodyweight long-lever trunk exercise is appropriate.

Grip and shoulder stability can be trained concurrently.

The session contains sufficient recovery and technical focus.

CAS should avoid selecting it when:

The athlete is a beginner without foundational trunk control.

The athlete reports shoulder, elbow, wrist, lumbar or anterior-hip pain.

Grip fatigue would interfere with higher-priority work.

The session already contains excessive hanging or pulling volume.

The athlete cannot control the eccentric phase.

The equipment is not demonstrably secure.

Selection Priority

Moderate

The Hanging Leg Raise is a valuable advanced core exercise but is not a universal default.

It is selected only when the athlete's prerequisites and session context justify its complexity and combined grip demand.

# Evidence Classification

Evidence Level

Level 2 — Expert Practice

Hanging leg-raise variations are widely used in gymnastics, calisthenics and physical preparation to develop anterior-core strength, hip-flexion strength and suspended body control.

CAS treats the exercise as an advanced practical strength movement rather than a direct measure of combat performance.

Its value depends strongly on strict execution because momentum can substantially reduce the intended trunk demand.

---

# Decision Summary

The Hanging Leg Raise is an advanced suspended core-strength exercise that develops anterior-trunk strength, pelvic control, hip-flexion strength, grip endurance and shoulder-girdle stability.

Its principal value lies in integrating trunk function with long-lever leg movement while the athlete supports the entire body from an overhead bar.

CAS selects it only when the athlete has adequate foundational trunk control, pain-free hanging capacity and the ability to eliminate uncontrolled swing.

It should be regressed whenever grip, shoulder stability, pelvic control or eccentric control limits technical quality.

Progression should prioritize stricter execution, greater range of motion and longer lever length before external load or ballistic variations are introduced.
