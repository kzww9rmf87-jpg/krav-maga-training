# CORE EXERCISES

Version 0.1

---

# Purpose

The Core Exercise Library defines the trunk-training exercises approved for use by the Combat Athlete System.

Its purpose is not to catalogue abdominal exercises.

Its purpose is to provide the CAS Engine with exercises that develop the athlete's ability to:

* organize the trunk;
* transmit force between the lower and upper body;
* maintain effective positions under load;
* resist unwanted movement;
* produce controlled movement;
* coordinate the trunk with the limbs;
* preserve posture during sport-relevant actions.

The Core Exercise Library implements the principles defined in:

* the Module Engine;
* the Exercise Knowledge Base;
* the Exercise Classification System;
* the Exercise Selection Rules;
* the Substitution Rules;
* the Scoring Model;
* the Validation Rules.

This document defines the common rules for all exercise records contained in `62_CORE`.

---

# Core Principle

> Core training develops trunk function, not abdominal fatigue.

An exercise does not qualify as a Core exercise merely because it creates:

* abdominal burning;
* local muscular fatigue;
* soreness;
* a visible abdominal contraction;
* or difficulty maintaining a position.

A valid Core exercise must improve at least one meaningful trunk function.

These functions include:

* force transmission;
* anti-extension;
* anti-flexion;
* anti-rotation;
* anti-lateral flexion;
* controlled rotation;
* postural organization;
* bracing;
* dynamic trunk control;
* coordination between the upper and lower body.

Abdominal fatigue may occur.

It is never the primary validation criterion.

---

# Module Classification

The Core module belongs primarily to the following Adaptation Domain:

* Movement

The Core module develops the athlete's ability to organize the trunk during human movement.

Robustness may occur as a secondary physiological consequence.

However, Robustness is not currently modeled as the primary selection objective of the Core module.

Core exercises must therefore be selected according to their contribution to:

* movement quality;
* postural control;
* force transmission;
* position maintenance;
* limb-to-trunk coordination;
* and sport-relevant trunk function.

They must not be selected primarily according to muscle isolation or hypertrophy potential.

---

# Definition of the Core

Within the Combat Athlete System, the Core is not treated as a single muscle group.

It is treated as a functional system involving coordinated control of:

* the pelvis;
* the lumbar spine;
* the thoracic spine;
* the rib cage;
* the abdominal wall;
* the diaphragm;
* the spinal extensors;
* the hips;
* the shoulder girdle;
* and the structures that connect the upper and lower body.

Core function depends on the ability to manage internal and external forces while preserving or deliberately changing trunk position.

The CAS therefore evaluates Core exercises according to function rather than anatomy alone.

---

# Primary Functions

## Force Transmission

Force transmission is the ability to transfer force efficiently between the lower and upper body.

This function is relevant to:

* punching;
* kicking;
* throwing;
* clinching;
* grappling;
* sprinting;
* jumping;
* carrying;
* changing direction;
* and resisting external contact.

The trunk must remain sufficiently organized to prevent force leakage while allowing the movement required by the action.

---

## Anti-Extension

Anti-extension is the ability to resist excessive extension of the lumbar spine.

Common demands include:

* maintaining rib cage and pelvis organization;
* preventing the lower back from arching excessively;
* controlling overhead positions;
* maintaining posture during push-up patterns;
* and transmitting force without losing trunk position.

Representative exercise families include:

* ab wheel variations;
* body-saw variations;
* plank variations;
* dead bug variations;
* hollow-body variations.

---

## Anti-Flexion

Anti-flexion is the ability to resist excessive forward bending of the trunk under load.

Common demands include:

* maintaining posture while carrying;
* preserving spinal organization during heavy pulling;
* resisting collapse during clinch or grappling exchanges;
* and supporting force production under axial or anterior loading.

Representative exercise families include:

* loaded carries;
* front-loaded carries;
* hinge-position isometrics;
* selected bracing exercises.

---

## Anti-Rotation

Anti-rotation is the ability to resist unwanted rotation of the trunk.

This function is relevant when:

* force is applied asymmetrically;
* one limb produces force;
* an opponent pulls or pushes from one side;
* the athlete changes direction;
* or the athlete must stabilize before producing intentional rotation.

Representative exercise families include:

* Pallof press variations;
* unilateral carries;
* plank variations with asymmetric loading;
* cable or band anti-rotation exercises.

---

## Anti-Lateral Flexion

Anti-lateral flexion is the ability to resist unwanted side bending of the trunk.

This function supports:

* unilateral load carriage;
* single-leg movement;
* stance control;
* striking posture;
* clinch stability;
* and resistance to asymmetric external forces.

Representative exercise families include:

* suitcase carries;
* offset carries;
* side plank variations;
* unilateral overhead carries.

---

## Controlled Rotation

Controlled rotation is the ability to produce, decelerate and stop trunk rotation while maintaining effective alignment.

Rotation must be:

* intentional;
* coordinated;
* appropriately sequenced;
* and compatible with the athlete's mobility and technical ability.

Controlled rotation differs from uncontrolled twisting.

Representative exercise families may include:

* controlled cable rotations;
* medicine-ball rotational drills;
* landmine rotations;
* rotational lifts and chops.

Rotational exercises belong in the Core module only when their primary purpose is trunk organization or controlled force transmission.

When maximal velocity or explosive output becomes the primary objective, the exercise may belong more appropriately to the Power module.

---

## Bracing

Bracing is the ability to create sufficient trunk stiffness for a specific task.

Bracing is not maximal tension at all times.

The required stiffness depends on:

* load;
* speed;
* direction of force;
* movement complexity;
* breathing demands;
* and external perturbation.

Effective bracing allows the athlete to maintain position without creating unnecessary tension that interferes with movement.

---

## Dynamic Trunk Control

Dynamic trunk control is the ability to maintain or recover effective trunk organization while the body is moving.

This includes:

* moving the limbs while controlling the pelvis and spine;
* transitioning between positions;
* responding to changing loads;
* absorbing perturbations;
* and coordinating movement across multiple joints.

Dynamic trunk control is particularly relevant to combat sports because trunk demands rarely occur in perfectly static positions.

# Core Exercise Families

The V0.1 Core Exercise Library contains the following primary families.

## Anti-Extension Exercises

Primary purpose:

* resist excessive lumbar extension;
* maintain rib cage and pelvis organization;
* coordinate breathing and trunk tension.

Initial V0.1 exercises:

* Ab Wheel;
* Dead Bug;
* Hollow Body Hold.

---

## Anti-Rotation Exercises

Primary purpose:

* resist unwanted rotational movement;
* maintain alignment under asymmetric force;
* improve force transfer through the trunk.

Initial V0.1 exercise:

* Pallof Press.

---

## Dynamic Trunk Flexion Exercises

Primary purpose:

* produce controlled trunk or pelvic movement;
* develop trunk strength through a defined range;
* maintain control without excessive momentum.

Initial V0.1 exercises:

* Hanging Leg Raise;
* Dragon Flag.

These exercises must not be selected solely because they create high abdominal fatigue.

Their technical demands and movement quality must remain compatible with the athlete's level.

---

## Loaded Carry Exercises

Primary purpose:

* integrate trunk control with locomotion;
* maintain posture under load;
* resist extension, rotation or lateral flexion;
* transmit force while walking.

Initial V0.1 exercises:

* Farmer Carry;
* Suitcase Carry;
* Overhead Carry.

Carries may also appear in the dedicated Carry Exercise Library.

Their assignment depends on the primary session objective.

When the main objective is trunk control, the exercise may implement the Core module.

When the main objective is locomotion under load, whole-body work capacity or carrying performance, it may implement the Carry module.

The same exercise must not be assigned to multiple modules within the same prescription without an explicit reason.

# Selection Priorities

Core exercises must be selected according to the following priorities.

## 1. Trunk-Function Match

The exercise must correspond to the trunk function required by the selected Core module.

Examples:

* anti-extension need → Ab Wheel, Dead Bug or Hollow Body Hold;
* anti-rotation need → Pallof Press;
* anti-lateral flexion need → Suitcase Carry;
* integrated bracing need → Farmer Carry;
* overhead postural control need → Overhead Carry.

A general abdominal exercise must not replace a specific trunk-function requirement without an explicit compromise.

---

## 2. Force-Transmission Relevance

The exercise should improve the athlete's ability to connect upper-body and lower-body force production.

Higher scores should be given to exercises that:

* require whole-body organization;
* coordinate the trunk with limb movement;
* preserve posture under external force;
* or resemble the force-management demands of the target activity.

Visual similarity to combat movement is not sufficient.

Transfer must result from shared physical demands.

---

## 3. Postural Control

The exercise must require the athlete to maintain or recover an effective position.

The position must be:

* clearly defined;
* observable;
* technically repeatable;
* and compatible with the intended adaptation.

Exercises that reward uncontrolled movement or compensation receive a lower score.

---

## 4. Movement Integration

Integrated exercises are preferred when they develop the required trunk function without unnecessary complexity or fatigue.

Integration may involve:

* locomotion;
* limb movement;
* asymmetric loading;
* breathing;
* balance;
* external resistance;
* or controlled perturbation.

Isolation may still be appropriate when:

* teaching a basic position;
* reducing complexity;
* returning from pain or interruption;
* or targeting a specific technical limitation.

---

## 5. Technical Repeatability

The athlete must be able to reproduce the intended position and action consistently.

An exercise receives a lower score when:

* technique deteriorates rapidly;
* the athlete cannot identify the target position;
* compensations dominate;
* setup is unreliable;
* or performance cannot be assessed clearly.

Advanced exercises must not be selected merely because they appear more difficult.

---

## 6. Low Unnecessary Fatigue

The exercise should produce sufficient stimulus without creating fatigue that interferes with higher-priority work.

The Engine must consider:

* local abdominal fatigue;
* grip fatigue;
* shoulder fatigue;
* hip-flexor fatigue;
* spinal loading;
* metabolic demand;
* and recovery cost.

A Core exercise should not compromise:

* technical combat practice;
* Power training;
* primary Strength work;
* or recovery from high-priority sessions.

---

# Exercise Selection Rules

A Core exercise may be selected only when:

* its primary function matches the module requirement;
* no Hard Constraint excludes it;
* the athlete possesses the necessary technical level;
* the required equipment is available;
* the exercise is compatible with pain and injury inputs;
* the exercise fits the available duration;
* the expected fatigue is acceptable;
* and an appropriate prescription can be generated.

The Engine must reject an exercise when:

* abdominal fatigue is its only clear benefit;
* the athlete cannot maintain the required trunk position;
* the exercise requires compensations that change its function;
* technical complexity exceeds athlete capability;
* the exercise aggravates declared pain;
* the exercise duplicates an already sufficient stimulus;
* or the exercise creates disproportionate fatigue relative to its value.

---

# Exercise Position Within a Session

Core work normally appears after higher-priority neural and technical work.

The default session order is:

1. Preparation;
2. Speed;
3. Power;
4. Primary Strength;
5. Secondary Strength;
6. Assistance;
7. Robustness or Grip;
8. Core;
9. Conditioning;
10. Recovery.

Core placement may change when:

* trunk activation is used in Preparation;
* a low-fatigue drill improves positioning for a later exercise;
* Core is the primary session objective;
* a carry is integrated into Strength or Conditioning;
* or the session architecture explicitly requires another order.

Low-fatigue preparation drills must not be confused with the main Core prescription.

---

# Volume Principles

Core volume must be sufficient to improve function without creating unnecessary fatigue.

Volume may be prescribed through:

* repetitions;
* duration;
* distance;
* sets;
* controlled breaths;
* or quality exposures.

The preferred unit depends on the exercise.

Examples:

* Ab Wheel → repetitions;
* Pallof Press → repetitions or duration;
* Dead Bug → repetitions per side or controlled breaths;
* Hollow Body Hold → duration;
* Hanging Leg Raise → repetitions;
* Dragon Flag → repetitions or controlled eccentrics;
* Farmer Carry → distance or duration;
* Suitcase Carry → distance or duration per side;
* Overhead Carry → distance or duration per side.

Volume must not be increased when technical quality is deteriorating.

---

# Intensity Principles

Core intensity may be increased through:

* external load;
* longer lever length;
* greater range of motion;
* reduced base of support;
* increased asymmetry;
* increased movement complexity;
* longer duration;
* slower tempo;
* stronger band or cable resistance;
* or greater perturbation.

Intensity must not be defined by discomfort alone.

The chosen intensity must allow the athlete to preserve the intended trunk function.

---

# Progression Principles

Core exercises progress according to technical mastery before difficulty.

The default progression hierarchy is:

1. understand the target position;
2. maintain the target position under low demand;
3. coordinate breathing with trunk control;
4. move the limbs without losing position;
5. increase range of motion;
6. increase duration or repetitions;
7. increase external load;
8. increase asymmetry;
9. increase movement complexity;
10. integrate the function into more dynamic actions.

The Engine must not progress several variables simultaneously unless the progression protocol explicitly permits it.

---

# Regression Principles

A Core exercise should be regressed when:

* the athlete loses spinal or pelvic position;
* breathing becomes uncontrolled;
* momentum replaces muscular control;
* range of motion exceeds available control;
* pain appears;
* the intended trunk function changes;
* fatigue produces repeated technical failure;
* or the athlete cannot meet the minimum quality threshold.

Regression options may include:

* reducing range of motion;
* shortening the lever;
* lowering external resistance;
* increasing the base of support;
* reducing asymmetry;
* using an isometric variation;
* reducing duration;
* reducing repetition count;
* slowing the movement;
* or selecting a simpler exercise from the same functional family.

Regression is not failure.

It preserves the intended adaptation at an appropriate level.

---

# Substitution Principles

Core substitutions must preserve the required trunk function whenever possible.

Examples:

* anti-extension must remain anti-extension;
* anti-rotation must remain anti-rotation;
* anti-lateral flexion must remain anti-lateral flexion;
* controlled rotation must not be replaced by uncontrolled flexion;
* an integrated carry should not automatically be replaced by an isolated abdominal exercise.

Substitution priority is:

1. same function and similar movement context;
2. same function with different equipment;
3. same function with reduced complexity;
4. same function with altered loading;
5. partial functional match;
6. last-resort alternative with an explicit compromise.

Every substitution must state why it was made.

---

# Fatigue Considerations

Core exercises may create fatigue outside the trunk.

Examples include:

* grip fatigue during carries or hanging exercises;
* shoulder fatigue during overhead carries or Ab Wheel variations;
* hip-flexor fatigue during Hanging Leg Raises;
* spinal extensor fatigue during loaded carries;
* metabolic fatigue during long-duration sets.

The Engine must distinguish the intended limitation from the actual limiting factor.

An exercise receives a lower score when a secondary limitation prevents the intended Core stimulus.

Examples:

* grip failure stops the carry before trunk control is challenged;
* shoulder fatigue stops the Ab Wheel before anti-extension capacity is trained;
* hip-flexor fatigue dominates the Hanging Leg Raise;
* cardiovascular fatigue disrupts posture during a loaded carry.

---

# Combat-Sport Relevance

Core function contributes to combat performance through shared physical demands.

Potential transfer includes:

* maintaining stance under contact;
* transmitting force during strikes;
* controlling rotation during punching and kicking;
* resisting unwanted rotation during grappling;
* maintaining posture in the clinch;
* stabilizing during unilateral actions;
* controlling the trunk during level changes;
* maintaining position during sprawls and ground transitions;
* and recovering from perturbations.

Core exercises must not be evaluated according to visual imitation of combat techniques.

The Engine must evaluate transfer according to:

* direction of force;
* force-transmission requirement;
* postural demand;
* contraction type;
* movement speed;
* coordination demand;
* fatigue profile;
* and compatibility with technical practice.

---

# Safety Principles

Core exercises must be performed within a controlled and pain-free range unless a qualified professional has defined another acceptable condition.

The Engine must apply additional caution when the athlete reports:

* acute low-back pain;
* radiating pain;
* neurological symptoms;
* abdominal or inguinal pain;
* recent abdominal surgery;
* recent spinal injury;
* uncontrolled shoulder pain;
* recent rib injury;
* dizziness;
* or unexplained symptoms during exertion.

The CAS must not diagnose the cause of symptoms.

When a declared condition exceeds the system's safe operating rules, the exercise must be excluded and the athlete directed toward appropriate professional assessment.

---

# Common Technical Failures

Common Core exercise failures include:

* excessive lumbar extension;
* uncontrolled lumbar flexion;
* rib flare;
* anterior pelvic tilt;
* loss of pelvic control;
* shoulder elevation;
* uncontrolled rotation;
* lateral trunk collapse;
* holding the breath without intent;
* using momentum;
* excessive speed;
* reducing range inconsistently;
* losing position under fatigue;
* and continuing after the target function has failed.

Each exercise record must define its own specific failure criteria.

---

# Quality Standard

A Core repetition or exposure is valid only when:

* the intended trunk function is visible;
* the athlete maintains the required position;
* breathing remains compatible with the prescription;
* movement is controlled;
* compensations remain below the exercise-specific threshold;
* and the athlete can repeat the action consistently.

A set must stop when the intended trunk function can no longer be maintained.

Completion of the planned number of repetitions does not override technical failure.

---

# Prescription Requirements

Every Core exercise prescription must specify:

* exercise name;
* exercise ID;
* primary trunk function;
* number of sets;
* repetitions, duration or distance;
* side allocation where applicable;
* tempo where relevant;
* load or resistance;
* rest interval;
* technical intent;
* stopping rule;
* and progression criterion.

Example:

```yaml
exerciseId: CORE-002
exerciseName: Pallof Press
primaryFunction: ANTI_ROTATION
sets: 3
repetitions: 10
sideAllocation: EACH_SIDE
tempo: CONTROLLED
restSeconds: 45
technicalIntent: Maintain pelvis and rib cage alignment while resisting cable rotation.
stoppingRule: Stop the set if the trunk rotates or posture cannot be restored immediately.
progressionCriterion: Complete all sets with stable alignment and no visible compensation.
```

---

# Validation Requirements

A Core exercise selection is valid only when:

* it implements the Core module correctly;
* its primary Adaptation Domain remains Movement;
* its purpose describes trunk control, force transmission or postural organization;
* it passes all mandatory exercise criteria;
* it respects all Hard Constraints;
* its prescription is complete;
* and the Decision Trace explains its selection.

A Core exercise selection is invalid when:

* abdominal fatigue alone is used as justification;
* the selected exercise does not match the required trunk function;
* the athlete cannot perform the exercise safely;
* the prescription does not define a stopping rule;
* or the exercise creates a conflict that the Engine does not resolve.

Relevant blocking validation code:

```text
EXERCISE_MODULE_MISMATCH
```

# V0.1 Scope

The V0.1 Core Exercise Library intentionally remains limited.

Its purpose is to validate:

* exercise classification;
* selection logic;
* progression logic;
* substitution logic;
* fatigue management;
* combat-transfer reasoning;
* and Decision Trace generation.

The V0.1 library should prioritize a small number of exercises that are:

* well understood;
* technically observable;
* broadly applicable;
* progressively loadable;
* substitutable;
* and compatible with the current Engine architecture.

Additional exercises may be introduced only when they provide a meaningful function not already covered or materially improve the available decision options.

The library must not expand merely to increase exercise variety.

---

# Current Limitations

The current V0.1 inventory does not yet provide complete coverage of:

* anti-flexion exercises;
* side plank progressions;
* crawling patterns;
* dynamic cable rotation;
* rotational deceleration;
* reactive trunk stability;
* perturbation-based exercises;
* medicine-ball trunk exercises;
* partner-resisted trunk work;
* ground-fighting-specific trunk patterns;
* or advanced sport-specific integration.

These gaps must be documented.

They must not be filled automatically without:

* a defined functional need;
* an approved exercise record;
* compatible progression rules;
* substitution relationships;
* risk information;
* and validation tests.

# Final Principle

> The best Core exercise is not the exercise that creates the greatest abdominal fatigue.

The best Core exercise is the exercise that develops the required trunk function:

* for the correct athlete;
* at the correct level;
* within the correct session;
* at an acceptable fatigue and risk cost;
* while preserving technical quality;
* and without interfering with higher-priority adaptations.

The Core Exercise Library exists to make that decision coherent, reproducible, safe and explainable.
