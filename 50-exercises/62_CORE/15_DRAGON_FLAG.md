# DRAGON FLAG

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Dragon Flag is an advanced bodyweight trunk-strength exercise used to develop high-level anterior-core strength, anti-extension capacity, posterior pelvic control and whole-body rigidity.

Within CAS, it is primarily selected as an advanced long-lever core-strength movement.

It requires the athlete to maintain a rigid body line while controlling the body through a large arc around a fixed upper-back contact point.

---

# Philosophy

Combat athletes must be able to preserve trunk stiffness while large external or internal forces attempt to extend, rotate or disorganize the body.

The Dragon Flag develops this capacity by forcing the athlete to connect the ribcage, pelvis and lower limbs into one rigid lever.

CAS does not treat the exercise as a demonstration skill or as a test of how close the feet can approach the floor.

The objective is to maintain posterior pelvic control, whole-body tension and a stable shoulder anchor throughout the prescribed range of motion.

# Execution Standard

## Starting Position

Lie supine on a stable bench or firm surface with a secure overhead anchor.

Grip the bench, post or handles behind the head using a position that allows strong shoulder anchoring without pain.

Keep the upper back and posterior shoulder region firmly connected to the support.

Raise the hips and legs until the body approaches a straight line from shoulders to feet.

Create posterior pelvic tilt, lock the knees and point or dorsiflex the feet consistently.

The neck remains neutral and does not bear the load.

## Lowering Phase

Begin lowering the rigid body as one unit.

Maintain the same relationship between ribcage, pelvis, hips and knees.

Do not allow the hips to fold before the trunk or the lumbar spine to arch.

Lower only through the range that can be controlled.

The upper back remains anchored while the rest of the body moves around that contact point.

## Bottom Position

Stop before the lower back loses control or the body line breaks.

The feet do not need to approach the floor for the repetition to be valid.

The prescribed range ends at the deepest position the athlete can maintain without lumbar extension, hip collapse or loss of shoulder stability.

## Raising Phase

Reverse the movement by maintaining whole-body rigidity and pulling through the upper-body anchor.

Raise the body as one connected lever.

Avoid initiating the return by sharply flexing the hips or swinging the legs.

Finish in the controlled elevated position without rolling onto the neck.

# Key Coaching Cues

Ribs down.

Curl the pelvis toward the ribs.

Move as one rigid piece.

Squeeze the glutes and thighs.

Pull hard against the anchor.

Keep the weight on the upper back, not the neck.

Lower only as far as you can own.

Do not let the hips fold.

# Technical Failure Criteria

The set is terminated when any of the following occurs:

The lumbar spine visibly extends.

The hips sag or fold independently of the trunk.

The knees bend beyond the prescribed variation.

The athlete loses posterior pelvic tilt.

The body no longer moves as one unit.

The upper-back anchor becomes unstable.

The athlete rolls onto the cervical spine or loads the neck.

The lowering phase accelerates uncontrollably.

The athlete uses a leg swing or rebound to return.

The grip or shoulder position becomes insecure.

Pain occurs in the neck, shoulder, elbow, lumbar spine or abdominal wall.

# Combat Transfer

The Dragon Flag contributes indirectly to combat performance by improving:

High-level anterior-core strength

Resistance to unwanted lumbar extension

Whole-body rigidity

Ribcage–pelvis control

Force transmission between upper and lower body

Trunk stiffness during powerful limb actions

Control under long-lever perturbation

Potential applications include:

Maintaining posture during striking

Stabilizing the trunk during kicks and knees

Resisting extension during grappling exchanges

Improving body-line control in ground positions

Supporting force transfer during explosive movement

CAS does not classify the Dragon Flag as a direct simulation of any combat technique.

Its transfer is structural and neuromuscular rather than movement-specific.

# CAS Selection Logic

CAS may select the Dragon Flag when:

The target adaptation includes advanced anti-extension or anterior-core strength.

The athlete has mastered foundational trunk-control exercises.

The athlete can maintain posterior pelvic tilt under long-lever load.

A safe and stable anchor is available.

The session allows low-repetition, high-quality technical work.

The athlete has sufficient shoulder tolerance and upper-body anchoring strength.

CAS should avoid selecting it when:

The athlete is a beginner.

The athlete cannot perform a strict Hollow Body Hold.

The athlete reports cervical, shoulder, lumbar or abdominal pain.

The session already contains excessive trunk, pulling or shoulder fatigue.

The exercise would be performed in a high-density fatigue circuit.

The athlete cannot control an eccentric regression.

Selection Priority

Low to Moderate

The Dragon Flag is a valuable but specialized advanced core-strength exercise.

It is selected only when the athlete's prerequisites, equipment and session objective justify its technical complexity and high local demand.

# Evidence Classification

Evidence Level

Level 3 — Established Practice

The Dragon Flag is widely used in calisthenics, gymnastics-inspired training and advanced physical preparation as a high-demand anterior-core and body-line strength exercise.

Direct research on the exercise itself is limited.

CAS therefore classifies it primarily through biomechanical analysis, established coaching practice and comparison with other long-lever anti-extension exercises.

Its usefulness depends strongly on appropriate regression and strict technical execution.

---

# Decision Summary

The Dragon Flag is a highly advanced bodyweight core-strength exercise that develops anterior-trunk strength, dynamic anti-extension capacity, posterior pelvic control and whole-body rigidity.

Its principal value lies in teaching the athlete to resist large extension torque while maintaining the body as one connected lever around a fixed upper-back anchor.

CAS selects it only when the athlete has mastered foundational trunk control, possesses adequate shoulder anchoring capacity and can perform strict regressions without lumbar extension or cervical loading.

It should be regressed whenever body-line integrity, posterior pelvic tilt, eccentric control or safe upper-back support cannot be maintained.

Progression should prioritize technical control, lever length and range of motion before external loading or additional repetition volume.
