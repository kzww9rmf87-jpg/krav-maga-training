# OVERHEAD CARRY

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Overhead Carry is a loaded locomotion exercise used to develop overhead shoulder stability, scapular control, trunk stiffness, rib-cage and pelvic alignment, grip endurance and whole-body force transmission while moving under load.

Within CAS, it is primarily selected as a shoulder-girdle robustness and integrated core-control movement.

It requires the athlete to walk while maintaining one or two implements in a stable overhead position without excessive lumbar extension, rib flare, shoulder elevation collapse or loss of gait control.

---

# Philosophy

Combat athletes must frequently maintain force and structure with the arms elevated, extended or displaced from the torso.

The Overhead Carry trains the ability to preserve shoulder centration, scapular upward rotation, trunk stiffness and locomotor control while the load creates a long lever above the body.

CAS does not use the exercise as a maximal overhead endurance test performed through deteriorating posture.

The objective is to maintain a stacked relationship between the hand, shoulder, rib cage and pelvis while walking with controlled breathing and stable mechanics.

# Execution Standard

1. Select an implement that can be pressed or placed overhead safely.
2. Establish a stable overhead position with the elbow extended but not aggressively locked.
3. Position the hand approximately above the shoulder and maintain a neutral wrist.
4. Allow the scapula to upwardly rotate while keeping the shoulder stable and controlled.
5. Keep the rib cage stacked over the pelvis.
6. Brace the trunk without holding the breath continuously.
7. Maintain a neutral head position.
8. Walk with controlled, even steps.
9. Prevent the load from drifting forward, backward or laterally.
10. Stop the set before shoulder position, trunk alignment or gait mechanics deteriorate.

# Technical Failure Criteria

The set is terminated when any of the following occurs:

The load drifts significantly away from the shoulder line.

The elbow repeatedly bends under fatigue.

The shoulder collapses into an unstable or painful position.

The athlete compensates with excessive lumbar extension.

The ribs flare and cannot be corrected.

The athlete leans laterally or rotates excessively.

Gait becomes irregular or uncontrolled.

The wrist loses neutral alignment.

Pain, numbness or neurological symptoms appear.

# Contraindications and Restrictions

Avoid or restrict when the athlete presents:

Acute shoulder pain

Recent shoulder dislocation or instability

Painful overhead range of motion

Significant limitation in shoulder flexion

Uncontrolled scapular winging

Acute cervical symptoms

Upper-limb neurological symptoms

Inability to maintain rib-cage and pelvic alignment overhead

Inadequate grip security

Use unilateral loading cautiously when asymmetrical spinal symptoms are present.

# Combat Transfer

The Overhead Carry contributes to combat performance by improving:

Shoulder stability during elevated-arm positions

Structural integrity when framing or posting

Force transmission between the upper and lower body

Trunk control during asymmetrical movement

Postural endurance under fatigue

Grip and forearm endurance

Resistance to extension and rotational collapse

Its transfer is indirect but meaningful for maintaining upper-body structure during clinch exchanges, defensive frames, grappling transitions and repeated striking activity.

# Substitution Logic

Substitute the Overhead Carry when:

The athlete lacks safe overhead range of motion.

Shoulder pain or instability is present.

The training environment does not permit safe overhead locomotion.

The athlete cannot control rib flare or lumbar extension.

Grip fatigue would interfere with priority training.

Preferred substitutions include:

Front-Rack Carry

Farmer Carry

Suitcase Carry

Landmine Carry

Static Overhead Hold

The substitute should preserve as much shoulder-girdle and trunk-stability demand as safely possible.

# Decision Summary

The Overhead Carry is a shoulder-girdle robustness and integrated core exercise that develops the ability to maintain a stable overhead position while walking under load.

CAS selects it when overhead stability, anti-extension control, postural endurance and whole-body force transmission are desired without excessive systemic fatigue.

It is most appropriate for athletes who already possess pain-free overhead mobility and adequate shoulder control.

The exercise must be stopped when the load drifts, the shoulder collapses, the ribs flare, lumbar extension increases or gait integrity deteriorates.

Technical quality always takes priority over load, distance or duration.
