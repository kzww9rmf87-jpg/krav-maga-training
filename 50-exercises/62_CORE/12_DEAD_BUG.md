# DEAD BUG

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Dead Bug is a supine trunk-control exercise used to develop anti-extension capacity, lumbopelvic control and coordinated movement of the limbs around a stable torso.

Within CAS, it is primarily selected as a low-cost motor-control and robustness exercise.

It teaches the athlete to preserve ribcage–pelvis alignment while the arms and legs create progressively longer lever arms.

---

# Philosophy

Combat athletes must be able to move the limbs rapidly without losing trunk position or leaking force through excessive lumbar extension.

The Dead Bug develops this foundational capacity with minimal joint stress, low metabolic demand and a high degree of movement control.

CAS does not select the Dead Bug as a maximal strength exercise.

It selects it when the athlete needs to learn, restore or reinforce efficient trunk stiffness before progressing toward more demanding anti-extension tasks.

# Execution Standard

Start supine with the hips and knees flexed to approximately 90 degrees and the arms positioned above the shoulders.

Set the ribcage over the pelvis without forcing the lower back aggressively into the floor.

Create gentle circumferential trunk tension and maintain normal controlled breathing.

Extend the prescribed arm, leg or opposite arm-and-leg combination without allowing the lumbar spine to arch, the ribs to flare or the pelvis to rotate.

Stop the limb movement at the furthest range that preserves trunk position.

Return under control and repeat on the opposite side when required.

---

# Key Coaching Cues

Ribs down, pelvis quiet.

Brace before the limbs move.

Reach long rather than reaching low.

Move only as far as the spine remains stable.

Exhale through the difficult portion without losing tension.

Keep the movement slow and symmetrical.

# Progression Model

Entry Level

The athlete can establish ribcage–pelvis alignment and breathe while maintaining light trunk tension.

Progression Strategy

Increase lever length before adding substantial external load.

Increase range of motion only while the lumbar position remains stable.

Progress from unilateral limb movement to contralateral arm-and-leg movement.

Add resistance only after the base movement is technically automatic.

Regression Strategy

Reduce lever length.

Move one limb at a time.

Decrease range of motion.

Support the feet or press the hands into a wall.

Performance Indicators

Stable lumbar position

No rib flare

No pelvic rotation

Controlled breathing

Symmetrical limb range

Consistent tempo

Exit Criteria

The athlete performs 3 sets of 8 controlled contralateral repetitions per side with full technical control and no symptom increase.

At that point, CAS may progress the Dead Bug or select a higher-demand anti-extension exercise.

---

# Programming Applications

Movement Preparation

Use 1–3 sets of 5–8 repetitions per side to establish trunk position before loaded training.

Robustness Module

Use 2–4 sets of 6–10 repetitions per side with slow tempo and controlled breathing.

Recovery Session

Use low-intensity variations for 2–3 sets without approaching technical failure.

Return-to-Training Progression

Use the Dead Bug to reintroduce contralateral limb movement around a stable trunk before advancing to loaded anti-extension work.

Technique Reinforcement

Use between demanding exercises only when it improves position without creating unnecessary fatigue or extending the session excessively.

---

# Combat Transfer

Primary Transfer

Maintaining trunk position while the limbs move independently

Improving proximal stability for distal force expression

Reinforcing contralateral coordination

Reducing unnecessary lumbar extension during striking and defensive movement

Secondary Transfer

Ground movement control

Bracing during clinch exchanges

Force transmission between upper and lower body

Postural control during fatigue

Transfer is indirect.

The Dead Bug builds a foundational capacity that supports combat actions but does not reproduce combat skill itself.

# Substitution Logic

Substitute the Dead Bug when:

The athlete cannot tolerate loaded anti-extension exercises.

The athlete lacks basic ribcage–pelvis control.

The session requires a very low-fatigue trunk exercise.

Equipment is unavailable.

A recovery or return-to-training context limits loading.

Do not substitute the Dead Bug for:

Maximal trunk strength work

High-force loaded carries

Ballistic rotation

Combat-specific striking practice

Advanced anti-extension work when the athlete already demonstrates full mastery and requires greater overload

---

# CAS Selection Logic

CAS favors the Dead Bug when:

The target adaptation is trunk motor control or low-level anti-extension capacity.

The athlete needs a beginner-friendly core exercise.

Readiness is reduced but controlled movement remains appropriate.

The session has limited equipment or space.

The desired stimulus must create minimal recovery cost.

The athlete is learning to coordinate breathing and bracing.

CAS deprioritizes the Dead Bug when:

The target adaptation is maximal strength.

The athlete requires high-force trunk loading.

The session objective is power or ballistic force production.

The athlete has already mastered the exercise and no appropriate progression is used.

Supine positioning or limb movement provokes symptoms.

# Evidence Classification

Evidence Level

Level 2 — Expert Practice

The Dead Bug is widely used in strength and rehabilitation practice as a low-load trunk-control exercise.

CAS treats its primary value as motor learning, anti-extension control and graded exposure rather than as a direct measure of maximal core strength or combat performance.

---

# Decision Summary

The Dead Bug is a low-risk, low-fatigue anti-extension exercise that develops trunk control, contralateral coordination and ribcage–pelvis stability.

Its greatest value lies in teaching the athlete to move the limbs around a stable torso.

CAS selects it primarily for movement preparation, robustness, recovery and return-to-training contexts.

It should be progressed or replaced when the athlete requires substantially greater force production, overload or sport-specific transfer.
