# BOX JUMP

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Box Jump is a bilateral lower-body plyometric exercise used to develop explosive force production, vertical projection, triple-extension coordination and controlled landing mechanics.

Within CAS, it is primarily selected as a lower-body power exercise.

The athlete jumps from the floor onto an elevated stable platform and lands under control.

The box reduces the landing distance relative to a maximal vertical jump, but it does not eliminate impact or technical demand.

---

# Philosophy

The Box Jump is not a test of how high the athlete can pull the knees toward the chest.

Its purpose is to produce a forceful take-off while preserving efficient posture, coordinated arm action and a stable landing.

CAS therefore evaluates the exercise according to take-off quality rather than box height alone.

A higher box is not automatically a better stimulus.

Excessive box height often converts the exercise into a mobility and landing-contortion task rather than a true power exercise.

The governing principle is:

> Jump high, land quietly and finish in control.

# Execution Standard

## Starting Position

Stand facing a stable box with the feet approximately hip-width apart.

Maintain enough distance from the box to allow a near-vertical jump without striking the front edge.

Keep the feet fully grounded and the trunk organized.

The athlete should be able to see and judge the landing surface clearly.

## Countermovement

Initiate a short, coordinated dip through the hips, knees and ankles.

Allow the arms to swing backward naturally.

Maintain whole-foot pressure and avoid collapsing the knees inward.

The countermovement should be fast but controlled.

## Take-Off

Drive forcefully through the floor.

Extend the hips, knees and ankles rapidly.

Use an aggressive arm swing to assist projection.

Leave the ground from both feet at approximately the same time.

The objective is maximal upward displacement, not excessive forward travel.

## Flight Phase

Bring the feet forward only as much as required to clear the box.

Do not exaggerate knee tucking to simulate greater jump height.

Maintain visual awareness of the landing surface.

## Landing

Land with both feet fully supported on the box.

Contact should occur through the midfoot or whole foot rather than the toes alone.

Absorb force through coordinated hip, knee and ankle flexion.

Keep the knees tracking in line with the feet.

The landing should be quiet, stable and balanced.

## Finish

Stand fully upright on the box before stepping down.

Step down one foot at a time under control.

Reset completely before the next repetition unless a continuous variation is deliberately programmed.

# Technical Failure Criteria

CAS terminates or regresses the set when any of the following occurs:

The athlete clips the front edge of the box.

The athlete lands with only part of the foot supported.

The knees collapse inward.

The athlete lands in an excessively deep squat caused by an overly high box.

The trunk collapses forward on landing.

The athlete loses balance or steps off immediately.

The landing becomes loud or rigid.

The athlete uses progressively greater knee tuck without maintaining true jump output.

Take-off becomes visibly slower.

Jump height declines meaningfully.

The athlete requires repeated hesitation before jumping.

The athlete jumps down from the box despite a step-down prescription.

Pain occurs during take-off, flight or landing.

# Contraindications and Restrictions

Avoid or modify when the athlete presents:

Acute ankle, knee, hip or foot injury

Active Achilles or patellar tendon pain

Recent lower-limb surgery

Poor bilateral landing control

Significant fear or hesitation around the box

Insufficient clearance of the box edge

Severe fatigue or impaired coordination

Unstable or inappropriate equipment

Medical restrictions on jumping or impact

Use caution with:

High body mass relative to landing capacity

Previous ankle sprain history

Anterior knee pain

Low bone-loading tolerance

Return-to-sport athletes

Athletes with poor depth-perception or visual limitations

# Progression Model

The athlete learns to absorb force with aligned hips, knees and ankles.

## Stage 2 — Floor-Based Vertical Jump

Countermovement Jump

Squat Jump

The athlete demonstrates symmetrical take-off and controlled floor landing.

## Stage 3 — Low Box Jump

Use a low platform that requires minimal knee tucking.

The athlete focuses on confidence, accurate foot placement and stable landing.

## Stage 4 — Standard Box Jump

Use a moderate box height while preserving a landing position close to the take-off squat depth.

## Stage 5 — Maximal-Intent Box Jump

Use full recovery and very low repetition sets.

Progress is evaluated through take-off quality and jump output.

## Stage 6 — Advanced Variation

# Combat Transfer

The Box Jump has moderate indirect transfer to combat performance.

Potential transfer qualities include:

Explosive leg drive

Rapid extension from flexed positions

Acceleration of the centre of mass

Improved lower-body rate of force development

More forceful level changes and recoveries

Support for kicking and striking power through lower-body propulsion

Improved ability to leave the ground or clear obstacles

Landing and deceleration control

The transfer is general rather than sport-specific.

The exercise does not reproduce combat stance, rotational striking mechanics or reactive decision-making.

Its main value is to improve the athlete’s physical power reserve.

# Substitution Logic

Replace the Box Jump when:

The athlete cannot land safely on the platform.

The athlete lacks confidence around the box.

No stable box is available.

The athlete presents acute lower-limb pain.

The box height forces excessive hip and spinal flexion.

Fatigue causes repeated technical failure.

The training objective requires horizontal rather than vertical force production.

# CAS Selection Logic

CAS may select the Box Jump when:

The primary objective is lower-body power.

The athlete has sufficient landing competency.

A stable box is available.

The athlete is neurologically fresh.

The session contains adequate recovery between sets.

Vertical projection is relevant to the selected module.

Impact tolerance is sufficient.

CAS should deprioritize or exclude the Box Jump when:

The athlete has active lower-limb pain.

Readiness is low.

Landing control is poor.

The available box is too high, unstable or too narrow.

The session already contains excessive plyometric contacts.

The athlete is completing high-volume lower-body combat practice.

The target adaptation is conditioning rather than power.

The athlete cannot step down safely.

---

# Decision Summary

The Box Jump is a bilateral vertical plyometric exercise with high value for developing lower-body power, explosive triple extension and controlled elevated landing.

CAS selects it when the athlete is sufficiently fresh, technically competent and able to use an appropriate stable box.

The exercise should be performed with low repetitions, high intent and complete technical control.

Its quality is determined by true take-off output and landing stability, not by box height alone.

CAS terminates the set when jump speed, confidence, foot clearance or landing mechanics deteriorate.
