# PLYOMETRICS OVERVIEW

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Plyometrics category defines explosive jumping, hopping and bounding exercises that use the stretch-shortening cycle to improve rapid force production, reactive strength, elastic energy utilization and lower-body coordination.

Within CAS, plyometric exercises are selected to improve the athlete’s ability to produce, absorb and redirect force quickly.

They are not selected to create fatigue for its own sake.

Their value depends on movement quality, intent, landing control and appropriate exposure to impact.

---

# Philosophy

Combat athletes must repeatedly accelerate, decelerate, change direction, strike, evade, level-change and recover position in very short time frames.

These actions depend on more than maximal strength.

They require the athlete to express force rapidly and to use elastic structures efficiently.

CAS therefore treats plyometrics as a neural and mechanical power method rather than as general conditioning.

The governing principle is:

> Every repetition must remain fast, coordinated and mechanically controlled.

When jump height, distance, stiffness, landing position or intent deteriorates, the useful plyometric stimulus has ended.

# Core Principle

Plyometric training consists of rapid eccentric loading followed by explosive concentric action.

The shorter and more efficient the transition between these phases, the greater the reactive demand.

CAS distinguishes between:

- jump training,
- landing training,
- low-intensity elastic contacts,
- maximal projection,
- reactive plyometrics,
- unilateral plyometrics,
- lateral plyometrics,
- and repeated elastic actions.

These methods are related but not interchangeable.

---

# Stretch-Shortening Cycle

The stretch-shortening cycle contains three phases.

## Eccentric Phase

The athlete absorbs force while muscles and tendons lengthen under tension.

## Amortization Phase

The transition between eccentric loading and concentric propulsion.

## Concentric Phase

Stored elastic energy and active muscular force produce acceleration.

A short amortization phase generally increases reactive transfer.

A longer transition shifts the exercise toward explosive strength rather than reactive strength.

---

# Plyometric Categories

## Concentric-Dominant Jumps

These begin from a static or deliberately paused position and reduce the contribution of the rapid stretch-shortening cycle.

Primary qualities:

- explosive strength,
- starting strength,
- concentric rate of force development.

CAS examples:

- Knee Jump
- paused jump variations

---

## Countermovement Jumps

These use a controlled eccentric countermovement before take-off.

Primary qualities:

- lower-body power,
- eccentric-to-concentric coordination,
- maximal projection.

CAS examples:

- Box Jump
- Broad Jump
- Split Squat Jump

---

## Reactive Jumps

These emphasize rapid ground contact and a short amortization phase.

Primary qualities:

- reactive strength,
- tendon stiffness,
- fast stretch-shortening cycle performance.

CAS examples:

- Depth Jump
- advanced repeated hopping variations

---

## Horizontal Plyometrics

These project the centre of mass forward.

Primary qualities:

- horizontal force production,
- acceleration transfer,
- hip extension power.

CAS examples:

- Broad Jump
- Single-Leg Hop

---

## Vertical Plyometrics

These project the centre of mass upward.

Primary qualities:

- vertical impulse,
- lower-body power,
- triple extension.

CAS examples:

- Box Jump
- Depth Jump
- Knee Jump
- Split Squat Jump

---

## Lateral Plyometrics

These project or redirect the body in the frontal plane.

Primary qualities:

- lateral force production,
- frontal-plane control,
- change-of-direction transfer.

CAS example:

- Lateral Bound

---

## Unilateral Plyometrics

These use one limb as the primary take-off or landing limb.

Primary qualities:

- unilateral power,
- pelvic control,
- single-leg stiffness,
- asymmetry management.

CAS examples:

- Single-Leg Hop
- Lateral Bound
- Split Squat Jump

# Global Exercise Roles

## Box Jump

Primary role:

Vertical power with reduced landing impact when the athlete lands on an elevated surface.

Main CAS use:

Explosive intent, jump confidence and controlled vertical projection.

---

## Depth Jump

Primary role:

High-intensity reactive strength after a rapid drop landing.

Main CAS use:

Advanced fast stretch-shortening cycle development.

---

## Broad Jump

Primary role:

Maximal horizontal projection.

Main CAS use:

Horizontal power, acceleration transfer and hip extension explosiveness.

---

## Knee Jump

Primary role:

Concentric-dominant hip extension from a kneeling start.

Main CAS use:

Starting strength and explosive hip extension with limited countermovement.

---

## Lateral Bound

Primary role:

Frontal-plane projection and unilateral landing control.

Main CAS use:

Lateral power, force redirection and combat footwork transfer.

---

## Single-Leg Hop

Primary role:

Unilateral horizontal projection and landing stability.

Main CAS use:

Single-leg power, asymmetry assessment and unilateral force production.

---

## Split Squat Jump

Primary role:

Unilateral vertical power from a split stance.

Main CAS use:

Explosive leg drive, stance-specific power and contralateral coordination.

# Impact Classification

## Low Impact

- submaximal landing drills,
- low-amplitude hops,
- assisted jumps,
- low-volume controlled Box Jumps.

## Moderate Impact

- maximal Box Jumps,
- Broad Jumps,
- Split Squat Jumps,
- controlled Lateral Bounds.

## High Impact

- maximal Single-Leg Hops,
- repeated bounds,
- high-amplitude unilateral jumps.

## Very High Impact

- Depth Jumps,
- high drop heights,
- repeated reactive contacts,
- advanced unilateral reactive work.

CAS does not infer intensity from repetition count alone.

Impact, speed, amplitude and landing demand must all be considered.

---

# Technical Standards

All plyometric exercises must satisfy the following standards.

## Take-Off

- explosive intent,
- coordinated arm and leg action where appropriate,
- stable foot pressure,
- no unnecessary preparatory movement,
- force directed according to the intended vector.

## Flight

- controlled body position,
- no excessive trunk collapse,
- no uncontrolled rotation,
- limbs prepared for the required landing.

## Landing

- quiet and controlled contact when the exercise includes a landing,
- foot, knee and hip aligned with the intended movement,
- no uncontrolled valgus collapse,
- no excessive trunk flexion or rotation,
- centre of mass controlled over the base of support,
- balance recovered before the next repetition unless reactive contacts are specifically prescribed.

---

# Landing Principle

Landing is not merely the end of the jump.

It is a trainable force-absorption skill.

CAS evaluates landing through:

- position,
- stiffness,
- joint alignment,
- balance,
- sound,
- control,
- and readiness for the next action.

An athlete who can produce force but cannot absorb it safely does not possess complete plyometric capacity.

---

# Intensity Determinants

Plyometric intensity increases with:

- greater jump height,
- greater horizontal distance,
- greater lateral displacement,
- shorter ground-contact time,
- higher drop height,
- unilateral execution,
- external load,
- repeated contacts,
- unstable landing requirements,
- and reduced recovery.

CAS should change one major intensity variable at a time whenever possible.

---

# Volume Metrics

Plyometric volume is primarily measured through ground contacts.

However, CAS also considers:

- exercise intensity,
- unilateral versus bilateral loading,
- landing height,
- reactive demand,
- maximal versus submaximal intent,
- and total weekly combat impact exposure.

One Depth Jump contact cannot be treated as equivalent to one low-amplitude hop.

# Placement Within the Session

Plyometrics are normally placed:

1. after the general and specific warm-up,
2. before heavy strength work,
3. before conditioning,
4. while the athlete is neurologically fresh.

They may also be paired with strength exercises in contrast or complex training when the athlete has sufficient training age and the programme specifically targets potentiation.

CAS should not place high-intensity plyometrics after exhaustive conditioning or high-volume lower-body fatigue unless the objective explicitly requires fatigue exposure and the athlete is prepared for it.

---

# Frequency

Typical exposure:

1–3 sessions per week

Frequency depends on:

- training age,
- sport schedule,
- lower-body strength workload,
- sprint exposure,
- combat practice impact,
- current soreness,
- tendon tolerance,
- and exercise intensity.

High-intensity reactive work generally requires more recovery than low-level landing or elastic drills.

---

# Readiness Requirements

Before high-intensity plyometric selection, CAS should verify:

- pain-free walking and basic jumping,
- adequate ankle, knee and hip control,
- competent bilateral landing,
- acceptable unilateral balance where relevant,
- sufficient lower-body strength for the selected demand,
- absence of unresolved tendon or joint symptoms,
- and adequate recovery from recent lower-body loading.

High-intensity plyometrics are not selected solely because the athlete is strong.

Strength must be combined with landing skill, tissue tolerance and movement control.

---

# Progression Logic

General progression:

Landing Control

↓

Low-Amplitude Bilateral Jumps

↓

Maximal Bilateral Jumps

↓

Horizontal and Lateral Projection

↓

Unilateral Jumps and Hops

↓

Repeated Elastic Contacts

↓

High-Intensity Reactive Plyometrics

Progression may occur through:

- increased amplitude,
- increased velocity,
- shorter ground-contact time,
- greater directional complexity,
- unilateral execution,
- higher drop height,
- repeated contacts,
- or external resistance.

CAS should not progress all variables simultaneously.

---

# Regression Logic

Plyometric exercises may be regressed through:

- reduced jump height,
- reduced distance,
- bilateral instead of unilateral execution,
- stick landings instead of repeated contacts,
- lower drop height,
- assisted execution,
- slower preparatory movement,
- fewer contacts,
- longer recovery,
- or replacement with landing drills and explosive strength exercises.

Regression is appropriate when the athlete cannot preserve speed, alignment or landing control.

---

# Exercise Selection Logic

CAS selects plyometric exercises according to the dominant requirement.

## Select Box Jump When

- vertical power is required,
- landing impact should be moderated,
- the athlete benefits from a clear external target,
- maximal reactive strength is not the primary objective.

## Select Depth Jump When

- advanced reactive strength is required,
- the athlete has excellent landing mechanics,
- tendon and joint tolerance are established,
- high neuromuscular cost is acceptable.

## Select Broad Jump When

- horizontal projection is required,
- acceleration transfer is important,
- maximal hip extension power is desired.

## Select Knee Jump When

- concentric starting strength is required,
- hip extension explosiveness is the priority,
- countermovement contribution should be reduced.

## Select Lateral Bound When

- lateral power is required,
- frontal-plane control is a priority,
- change-of-direction or combat footwork transfer is desired.

## Select Single-Leg Hop When

- unilateral horizontal power is required,
- asymmetry needs to be assessed or trained,
- single-leg landing capacity is adequate.

## Select Split Squat Jump When

- split-stance explosiveness is required,
- unilateral vertical force production is desired,
- stance-specific combat transfer is relevant.

---

# Combat Transfer

Plyometrics can support combat performance through:

- faster stance initiation,
- more explosive entries,
- improved evasive movement,
- stronger level changes,
- quicker repositioning,
- more powerful kicking mechanics,
- improved recovery after strikes,
- better force absorption,
- and more efficient change of direction.

Transfer is indirect.

Plyometrics improve physical qualities that support combat actions.

They do not replace technical striking, grappling, footwork or tactical practice.

---

# Sport Transfer

## Boxing

Primary relevance:

Footwork initiation, lateral movement, stance recovery and explosive lower-body contribution to punching.

## Kickboxing and Muay Thai

Primary relevance:

Kicking support-leg stiffness, rapid repositioning, stance recovery and lower-body explosiveness.

## MMA

Primary relevance:

Shot entries, sprawls, direction changes, cage movement and mixed-stance power.

## Wrestling

Primary relevance:

Level changes, penetration steps, mat returns and rapid force redirection.

## Judo

Primary relevance:

Explosive entries, re-acceleration, unilateral force production and landing robustness.

## Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

Primary relevance:

General lower-body power and rapid ground-to-standing transitions, with lower direct transfer than in striking or wrestling sports.

## Krav Maga

Primary relevance:

Explosive movement initiation, evasive displacement, rapid stance recovery, kicking support and whole-body reactivity.

---

# Fatigue Cost

Plyometric fatigue is often neurological and mechanical rather than metabolic.

The athlete may not feel exhausted even when jump quality has already deteriorated.

CAS therefore monitors:

- jump height,
- jump distance,
- ground-contact time,
- landing sound,
- positional control,
- perceived stiffness,
- asymmetry,
- and movement confidence.

Low subjective effort does not automatically mean low training cost.

---

# Recovery Interaction

Plyometric demand interacts strongly with:

- heavy squats,
- deadlifts,
- Olympic lift variations,
- sprinting,
- lower-body power training,
- kicking volume,
- sparring,
- wrestling shots,
- and repeated change-of-direction work.

CAS must account for cumulative lower-limb impact and neural exposure across the entire training week.

---

# Safety Constraints

CAS should avoid or regress plyometric work when:

- acute joint pain is present,
- tendon symptoms worsen with impact,
- landing mechanics are uncontrolled,
- the athlete shows significant lower-limb asymmetry,
- fatigue prevents stable landings,
- the training surface is unsafe,
- footwear is inappropriate,
- the box or landing target is unstable,
- recent lower-body injury has not been cleared,
- or the athlete lacks the required progression history.

Depth Jumps, maximal unilateral hops and repeated reactive contacts require the strictest eligibility threshold.

---

# Common Errors Across the Category

- treating jumps as conditioning,
- using excessive repetitions,
- continuing after power output declines,
- selecting box height according to ego rather than take-off quality,
- landing in excessive knee valgus,
- allowing uncontrolled trunk collapse,
- using high drop heights before reactive competency is established,
- confusing deep landings with good force absorption,
- introducing unilateral work before bilateral control,
- and ignoring total combat-related impact exposure.

---

# Coaching Priorities

1. Define the intended force vector.
2. Use maximal or clearly prescribed intent.
3. Preserve alignment during take-off and landing.
4. Keep repetitions low enough to protect output.
5. Recover fully between high-quality efforts.
6. Stop when speed or control declines.
7. Progress impact only after technical competency is established.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine should:

1. identify whether the target is explosive strength, maximal power, reactive strength, landing skill or directional power,
2. verify athlete readiness and impact tolerance,
3. select the lowest-complexity exercise that satisfies the objective,
4. prescribe volume using contacts and intensity rather than repetition count alone,
5. place high-intensity plyometrics early in the session,
6. protect recovery from competing lower-body stressors,
7. stop progression when landing or output quality deteriorates,
8. avoid reactive plyometrics when the athlete lacks sufficient technical or tissue readiness,
9. record directional, unilateral and impact characteristics in the Decision Trace,
10. provide a safe regression whenever the preferred exercise is ineligible.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When a plyometric exercise is selected, the Decision Trace should explain:

- the targeted power quality,
- the selected force vector,
- whether the task is bilateral or unilateral,
- whether the stretch-shortening cycle is slow or fast,
- the expected impact level,
- the athlete readiness conditions considered,
- the prescribed ground-contact volume,
- the recovery interval,
- the stop condition,
- and the reason this exercise was preferred over alternative plyometric options.

---

# Summary

Plyometrics develop the athlete’s ability to produce, absorb and redirect force rapidly.

Within CAS, they are a high-value but tightly controlled power method.

Their effectiveness depends on:

- maximal intent,
- low unnecessary fatigue,
- precise exercise selection,
- appropriate impact exposure,
- stable landing mechanics,
- and sufficient recovery.

The category progresses from controlled landing and bilateral projection toward unilateral, multidirectional and highly reactive actions.

The objective is not to accumulate jumps.

The objective is to produce faster, safer and more transferable force.
