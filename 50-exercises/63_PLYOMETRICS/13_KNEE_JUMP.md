# KNEE JUMP

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Knee Jump is a concentric-dominant lower-body power exercise performed from a tall-kneeling position.

Within CAS, it is primarily selected to develop rapid hip extension, whole-body projection and the ability to transition explosively from the knees to a stable athletic stance.

The athlete begins upright on both knees, loads the hips without collapsing the trunk, drives forcefully through hip extension and arm action, brings the feet underneath the body and lands under control.

The exercise reduces the contribution of the ankle during the initial propulsion phase and places greater emphasis on the hips, trunk and coordinated repositioning of the lower limbs.

---

# Philosophy

The Knee Jump is not a flexibility trick or a test of bravery.

Its value lies in producing a rapid, coordinated hip-driven projection from a constrained starting position and finishing in a balanced stance.

CAS does not reward a repetition that reaches the feet through excessive lumbar extension, uncontrolled foot placement or a deep collapsing landing.

The governing principle is:

> Drive from the hips, move the feet quickly and own the landing.

The exercise should only be used when the athlete can tolerate kneeling, generate sufficient projection and land quietly with stable lower-limb alignment.

# Execution Standard

1. Place a dense exercise mat or folded pad on a stable, non-slip surface.

2. Begin in a tall-kneeling position with both knees approximately hip-width apart.

3. Keep the hips extended, ribs organized and pelvis neutral.

4. Position the toes according to comfort, either pointed behind or lightly tucked, without using them to push from the floor.

5. Swing the arms backward while allowing a controlled hip hinge.

6. Avoid sitting fully onto the heels unless the programmed variation explicitly allows it.

7. Reverse the movement aggressively by driving the hips forward and swinging the arms upward.

8. Create sufficient upward projection before attempting to bring the feet underneath the body.

9. Rapidly flex the hips and knees to place both feet on the floor at approximately shoulder width.

10. Land on the midfoot with the knees tracking in line with the feet.

11. Absorb the landing through the hips, knees and ankles.

12. Finish in a stable athletic stance without stepping, collapsing or rotating.

13. Reset deliberately to the tall-kneeling position before the next repetition.

Each repetition must begin from a controlled static position.

# Technical Failure Criteria

A repetition is invalid when any of the following occurs:

The athlete pushes significantly from the toes during take-off.

The hips remain flexed and propulsion comes primarily from lumbar extension.

The athlete throws the head backward to create momentum.

The lower back hyperextends excessively.

One foot lands clearly before the other without an intentional split-stance variation.

The feet land excessively narrow, crossed or asymmetrical.

The knees collapse inward.

The athlete lands on the toes without controlled heel contact.

The landing is excessively loud or rigid.

The athlete falls forward, backward or sideways.

An additional step is required to recover balance.

The athlete lands in an uncontrolled deep squat.

The knees strike the floor during an unsuccessful attempt.

Pain occurs during kneeling, projection or landing.

Repetition height or speed declines substantially.

Fear or hesitation alters the movement strategy.

# Safety Profile

Overall Risk

Moderate

Primary Risks

Direct Knee Discomfort From the Starting Surface

Failed Foot Recovery

Falling Forward or Backward

Knee Valgus During Landing

Excessive Lumbar Hyperextension

Hard or Asymmetrical Landing

Risk Reduction Measures

Use adequate knee padding.

Teach the movement onto a slightly raised soft landing surface first when needed.

Maintain clear space around the athlete.

Use low repetitions.

Stop immediately after a failed or painful attempt.

Do not add external load during normal CAS programming.

Do not use the exercise under meaningful fatigue.

A failed Knee Jump can result in the athlete returning abruptly to the knees or catching the feet poorly.

For this reason, progression must be conservative and technically supervised when possible.

Transfer Limitations

The Knee Jump does not replicate a normal striking, kicking or grappling stance.

It does not train complete ground-based triple extension because the feet are absent from the initial propulsion phase.

It should therefore be treated as a supplementary power exercise rather than a central combat-specific movement.

CAS does not infer direct technical fighting skill from successful Knee Jump performance.

# Substitution Logic

Substitute the Knee Jump when the programmed objective is explosive hip extension or concentric lower-body power and the athlete cannot safely or effectively perform the original exercise.

Preferred Substitutions by Constraint

Knee Discomfort in Tall Kneeling

Use Squat Jump, Box Jump or Medicine-Ball Scoop Throw

Insufficient Foot Recovery Speed

Use Knee Jump to Raised Mat or Seated Box Jump

Poor Landing Control

Use Snap-Down, Low Box Jump or Countermovement Jump With Landing Emphasis

Low Confidence

Use Raised-Surface Regression or Remove the Exercise

Limited Overhead Space

Use Reduced Arm Swing Only if technique remains effective, otherwise select a medicine-ball or horizontal power alternative

Need for Lower Technical Complexity

Use Squat Jump or Low Box Jump

Need for More Complete Triple Extension

Use Countermovement Jump, Broad Jump or Sprint Acceleration

CAS does not preserve the Knee Jump merely for novelty when a safer equivalent meets the same adaptation target.

# CAS Selection Logic

Select When

The primary objective is lower-body power.

Explosive hip extension is a relevant secondary target.

The athlete has demonstrated safe bilateral landing mechanics.

The athlete can kneel without pain.

The athlete has sufficient coordination and confidence.

The session allows low-volume, high-quality execution.

The exercise provides useful variation without displacing more specific priority work.

Do Not Select When

A hard safety constraint is present.

The athlete lacks landing competency.

Kneeling is uncomfortable or contraindicated.

The athlete is fatigued or technically unstable.

The session already contains excessive lower-body plyometric volume.

A simpler exercise can produce the same target adaptation more safely.

Selection Priority

Moderate

The Knee Jump is a useful supplementary exercise but not a foundational CAS plyometric.

It should rank below basic jumps, sprinting and validated medicine-ball power exercises when those options better match the objective.

# Evidence Classification

Exercise Evidence Level

Moderate for the general principles of ballistic power training and explosive hip extension

Limited for unique superiority of the Knee Jump over other plyometric exercises

Evidence Interpretation

The Knee Jump is biomechanically plausible as a hip-dominant explosive exercise and is widely used in athletic preparation.

However, direct comparative evidence establishing it as superior to conventional jumps, sprinting or loaded power exercises is limited.

CAS therefore classifies it as an optional supplementary tool rather than a mandatory exercise.

Programming decisions should be driven by athlete suitability, movement quality, adaptation need and risk-to-benefit ratio.

---

# Decision Summary

The Knee Jump is a concentric-dominant explosive exercise that develops rapid hip extension, whole-body projection and fast repositioning of the feet from a tall-kneeling start.

It is best used with intermediate or advanced athletes who can kneel comfortably, land safely and perform the movement without lumbar compensation.

Its principal strengths are minimal equipment, strong hip-extension emphasis and useful coordination demand.

Its principal limitations are reduced ankle contribution, moderate technical complexity, direct knee-contact requirements and the possibility of an uncontrolled failed repetition.

Within CAS, the Knee Jump should be selected as a low-volume supplementary power exercise, never as a fatigue drill and never when a simpler or safer exercise better serves the same objective.
