# SINGLE LEG HOP

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Single Leg Hop is a unilateral plyometric exercise used to develop force production, elastic reactivity, landing control and repeated propulsion on one leg.

Within CAS, it is primarily selected when the athlete needs to improve the ability to push explosively from one foot, travel through space and regain control on the same limb.

The exercise may be performed as a single maximal hop with a stabilized landing or as a controlled sequence of repeated hops when reactive capacity and stiffness are the primary objectives.

For combat athletes, the Single Leg Hop supports unilateral propulsion, rapid stance recovery, angle creation, evasive movement and the ability to accept force on one leg during dynamic footwork.

---

# Philosophy

The Single Leg Hop is not simply a balance drill and it is not a contest to cover maximum distance at any cost.

Its value comes from producing force rapidly while maintaining alignment of the foot, knee, hip and pelvis throughout take-off and landing.

CAS does not reward excessive forward reach, uncontrolled trunk rotation, knee collapse, repeated balance corrections or a landing that cannot be stabilized immediately.

The governing principle is:

> Produce force from one leg, land on the same leg and remain ready for the next action.

Distance, height and reactive speed are increased only after the athlete demonstrates repeatable control.

# Execution Standard

Starting Position

Stand on one leg with the foot facing forward.

Maintain a stable tripod foot with pressure distributed through the heel, base of the first metatarsal and base of the fifth metatarsal.

Keep the pelvis level, the trunk tall and the support knee softly flexed.

Allow the opposite leg and arms to prepare for a coordinated countermovement.

Take-Off

Perform a controlled countermovement through the hip, knee and ankle.

Drive the support leg forcefully into the ground.

Extend the hip, knee and ankle explosively.

Use the arms and free leg to assist projection without rotating the trunk excessively.

Flight

Maintain orientation toward the intended landing direction.

Keep the pelvis and trunk controlled.

Prepare the landing leg before ground contact rather than reaching excessively at the last moment.

Landing

Land on the same leg from which the athlete took off.

Contact the ground with the foot under or only slightly ahead of the centre of mass.

Allow the ankle, knee and hip to flex together to absorb force.

Maintain knee alignment over the foot.

Keep the pelvis level and prevent excessive trunk collapse or rotation.

Stabilization

Hold the landing for approximately one to two seconds during single-hop variations.

The athlete should remain balanced without touching the opposite foot down, taking a recovery step or using excessive arm movement.

For repeated hops, each ground contact should remain aligned, rhythmic and purposeful.

Completion Standard

A repetition is complete only when the athlete demonstrates controlled force acceptance and is physically prepared for the next movement.

# Technical Failure Criteria

The repetition fails when any of the following occur:

The opposite foot touches the ground during the landing.

The athlete takes a recovery step.

The knee collapses medially.

The foot rolls excessively inward or outward.

The pelvis drops or rotates uncontrollably.

The trunk collapses, twists or leans excessively.

The athlete lands with a rigid knee and minimal force absorption.

The landing foot reaches excessively far ahead of the centre of mass.

The heel, forefoot or entire foot strikes in an uncontrolled manner.

The athlete cannot stabilize within approximately two seconds during a single-hop variation.

Repeated hops show progressively longer contacts, shorter distances or deteriorating alignment.

Pain occurs during take-off, flight preparation or landing.

CAS should terminate or regress the exercise when technical failure becomes repetitive rather than isolated.

# Combat Transfer

The Single Leg Hop does not replicate a strike or defensive technique directly.

Its transfer is indirect but meaningful because combat movement repeatedly requires the athlete to produce and absorb force from asymmetric positions.

Potential Transfer Areas

Explosive push-off from a staggered stance

Rapid advance or retreat from one leg

Angle creation

Recovery after kicking

Repositioning after a missed strike

Single-leg control during evasive movement

Stability after contact or partial loss of balance

Preparation for rapid direction changes

The exercise is most transferable when combined with combat footwork and technical practice rather than treated as a substitute for them.

# Substitution Logic

Substitute With a Regression When

The athlete cannot stabilize the landing.

The knee collapses repeatedly.

Pain or apprehension appears.

The athlete lacks basic unilateral strength.

The planned intensity exceeds current readiness.

Substitute With a Bilateral Jump When

The objective is general horizontal power and unilateral control is not essential.

Substitute With a Lateral Bound When

The primary objective is frontal-plane propulsion and contralateral landing.

Substitute With a Single-Leg Pogo When

The primary objective is ankle stiffness and short ground contact rather than horizontal displacement.

Substitute With Sprinting or Acceleration Drills When

The primary objective is locomotor acceleration rather than isolated hop performance.

CAS should preserve the intended force vector, unilateral demand and landing objective whenever possible.

---

# CAS Selection Logic

CAS Selects the Single Leg Hop When

The athlete requires unilateral lower-body power.

The session objective includes single-leg landing control.

The athlete demonstrates adequate bilateral and unilateral prerequisites.

Horizontal force production is relevant.

Low equipment availability favours bodyweight power exercises.

The athlete needs a progression toward multidirectional or reactive plyometrics.

The movement complements combat footwork or change-of-direction training.

CAS Avoids or Regresses the Single Leg Hop When

The athlete reports lower-limb pain.

Single-leg landing control is inadequate.

Readiness is low.

Lower-body fatigue is high.

The surface is unsafe.

The athlete has not demonstrated basic bilateral landing competence.

The session already contains excessive high-impact contacts.

Selection Priority

High for unilateral power and landing development

Moderate to High for change-of-direction and combat footwork preparation

Moderate for general lower-body power

Low for beginners without unilateral landing prerequisites

# Decision Summary

The Single Leg Hop is a high-value unilateral plyometric exercise for developing same-leg propulsion, horizontal power, elastic function and single-leg landing control.

CAS selects it when the athlete needs to produce force explosively from one limb and regain a stable athletic position on that same limb.

It should be performed with low volume, high intent and strict alignment standards.

Distance, height and reactive speed must never be increased at the expense of foot control, knee position, pelvic stability or landing quality.

The exercise is especially relevant to combat athletes who must move, strike, evade and recover from asymmetric positions while remaining prepared for the next action.
