# Purpose

The Sandbag Carry develops loaded locomotion with an unstable and deformable object.

Within CAS, it is used when the athlete must improve the ability to move while controlling a load that does not remain perfectly balanced or rigid.

The exercise combines:

- object control,
- trunk stiffness,
- upper-back endurance,
- breathing under compression,
- gait integrity,
- shoulder stability,
- and whole-body bracing.

It is particularly relevant for combat athletes because the load behaves less predictably than a barbell, dumbbell or kettlebell.

The Sandbag Carry must not be selected merely because it appears more functional.

Its use depends on whether irregular-object control and front-loaded locomotion match the intended adaptation.

# Movement Description

The athlete lifts a sandbag and secures it in the prescribed carry position.

Common positions include:

- bear hug,
- front carry,
- shouldered carry,
- front rack,
- or offset carry.

The athlete then walks for the prescribed distance or duration while maintaining:

- control of the sandbag,
- stable posture,
- efficient breathing,
- deliberate gait,
- and consistent load position.

The set ends before the sandbag slips, posture collapses or breathing becomes uncontrolled.

---

# Starting Position

The athlete should begin with:

- the sandbag positioned safely on the floor or platform,
- feet in a stable stance,
- the trunk braced,
- the hips and knees prepared to lift,
- both arms positioned according to the carry style,
- and the walking lane clear.

The athlete must establish secure object control before moving.

---

# Execution

1. Approach the sandbag with a stable stance.
2. Grip or wrap the arms around the bag.
3. Brace the trunk.
4. Lift the sandbag using a controlled squat, hinge or lap-to-chest sequence.
5. Secure the bag in the selected carry position.
6. Stand tall without excessive extension.
7. Begin walking with short, deliberate steps.
8. Keep the load close to the torso.
9. Maintain posture and controlled breathing.
10. Complete the prescribed distance or duration.
11. Stop before the bag slips or posture deteriorates.
12. Lower the sandbag safely.

---

# Key Technical Cues

- Keep the bag close.
- Wrap and squeeze.
- Stay tall.
- Stack the ribs over the pelvis.
- Walk with controlled steps.
- Do not lean backward.
- Keep the upper back active.
- Breathe behind the brace.
- Stop before the bag shifts.
- Lower under control.

# Object-Control Demand

The sandbag differs from rigid implements because it:

- shifts under movement,
- changes shape,
- compresses against the body,
- may sag or rotate,
- and requires continuous adjustment.

This creates a high demand on:

- arm position,
- shoulder stability,
- trunk control,
- and grip or squeezing endurance.

The bag must remain controlled rather than simply supported.

---

# Trunk Demand

The trunk must resist:

- forward collapse,
- excessive lumbar extension,
- rotation,
- lateral shift,
- and rib flare.

Front-loaded positions create strong anti-flexion and anti-extension demands.

Offset and shouldered positions increase anti-rotation and anti-lateral-flexion demand.

---

# Breathing Demand

Sandbag Carries often compress the chest and abdomen.

The athlete must be able to:

- maintain brace,
- breathe under load,
- avoid panic or breath-holding,
- and preserve posture despite compression.

Breathing difficulty is expected but must remain controlled.

---

# Grip Demand

Grip demand varies by carry position.

## Bear-Hug Position

Grip demand is moderate.

The arms and torso create friction and compression around the bag.

## Front Carry

Grip demand is moderate to high depending on handle access.

## Shouldered Carry

Grip demand may be lower, while trunk asymmetry is higher.

## Handle Carry

Grip demand may become high and resemble a Farmer Carry.

CAS must identify whether grip or object control is intended to be the limiting factor.

---

# Technical Complexity

Moderate

The walking pattern is simple, but the sandbag introduces irregular load behavior.

The athlete must coordinate:

- pickup,
- load positioning,
- object control,
- posture,
- breathing,
- and gait.

Complexity increases with:

- heavier bags,
- looser fill,
- shouldered loading,
- offset positions,
- or repeated pickups.

---

# Prerequisites

The athlete should demonstrate:

- pain-free walking,
- safe squat or hinge mechanics,
- adequate trunk bracing,
- stable shoulder control,
- ability to secure the sandbag,
- and the ability to lower it safely.

No advanced carry experience is required, but the athlete should first demonstrate competence with lighter front-loaded carries.

---

# Eligibility Criteria

The Sandbag Carry is eligible when:

- irregular-object control is a target,
- front-loaded locomotion is appropriate,
- trunk and upper-back endurance are required,
- the athlete can lift and secure the bag safely,
- gait remains controlled,
- and total trunk and shoulder fatigue remains recoverable.

---

# Ineligibility Criteria

The exercise is not eligible when the athlete presents:

- acute low-back pain,
- shoulder pain,
- elbow pain,
- wrist pain,
- impaired balance,
- unsafe gait,
- inability to secure the bag,
- inability to breathe safely under compression,
- or inability to lift and lower the load safely.

---

# Relative Contraindications

Use caution when the athlete has:

- thoracic stiffness,
- cervical discomfort,
- breathing limitations,
- recent heavy front squatting,
- upper-back fatigue,
- rib irritation,
- or skin sensitivity from bag friction.

The engine should reduce load, distance, bag size or compression when necessary.

---

# Absolute Contraindications

Do not prescribe the exercise when there is:

- acute spinal injury,
- acute shoulder injury,
- acute upper-limb injury,
- uncontrolled respiratory symptoms,
- inability to walk safely,
- or an unstable or damaged sandbag.

---

# Common Errors

## Bag Too Far From the Body

The athlete holds the sandbag away from the torso.

Risk:

- greater spinal loading,
- faster arm fatigue,
- reduced control.

Correction:

- pull the bag close and wrap the arms around it.

## Excessive Lumbar Extension

The athlete leans backward to counterbalance the load.

Risk:

- increased lumbar stress,
- reduced trunk control.

Correction:

- stack the ribs over the pelvis and reduce load.

## Forward Trunk Collapse

The athlete folds over the bag.

Risk:

- reduced gait quality,
- increased spinal stress,
- breathing restriction.

Correction:

- keep the upper back active and shorten the carry.

## Loose Arm Position

The athlete allows the elbows or hands to separate from the bag.

Risk:

- shifting load,
- sudden loss of control.

Correction:

- squeeze the bag and maintain consistent arm pressure.

## Rushed Walking

The athlete moves too quickly.

Risk:

- excessive bag movement,
- poor foot placement,
- unstable posture.

Correction:

- use short, controlled steps.

## Prolonged Breath-Holding

The athlete holds one breath for the entire set.

Risk:

- dizziness,
- rapid fatigue,
- postural collapse.

Correction:

- breathe behind the brace.

## Unsafe Pickup

The athlete rounds aggressively or twists while lifting the bag.

Risk:

- spinal stress,
- poor load control.

Correction:

- use a controlled lap-to-chest, squat or hinge strategy.

## Dropping the Bag Without Control

The athlete releases the bag abruptly.

Risk:

- foot injury,
- equipment damage,
- loss of training control.

Correction:

- lower the sandbag deliberately.

# Coaching Priorities

1. Establish a safe pickup.
2. Secure the bag before walking.
3. Keep the load close.
4. Maintain stacked posture.
5. Keep the upper back active.
6. Walk with controlled steps.
7. Breathe without losing brace.
8. Prevent load shifting.
9. Stop before control is lost.
10. Lower the bag safely.

# Prescription Variables

## Distance

Typical range:

- 10 to 20 metres for strength,
- 20 to 40 metres for strength endurance,
- 30 to 60 metres for work capacity.

## Duration

Typical range:

- 10 to 20 seconds for strength,
- 20 to 40 seconds for strength endurance,
- 30 to 60 seconds for work capacity.

## Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 5 sets.

## Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 4 minutes.

## Load

Load is determined by:

- bag weight,
- bag size,
- fill density,
- carry position,
- distance,
- breathing tolerance,
- and athlete experience.

---

# Strength Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 5 sets,
- 10 to 20 metres,
- heavy but controlled bag,
- 2 to 4 minutes rest,
- strict posture,
- secure object control.

---

# Strength-Endurance Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 5 sets,
- 20 to 40 metres,
- moderate load,
- 90 to 180 seconds rest,
- stable breathing,
- consistent load position.

---

# Work-Capacity Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 5 sets,
- 30 to 60 seconds,
- moderate bag,
- controlled pace,
- incomplete but sufficient recovery,
- no technical collapse.

# Bear-Hug Carry

The athlete wraps both arms around the bag in front of the torso.

Primary benefits:

- object control,
- upper-back endurance,
- anti-flexion strength,
- breathing under compression,
- clinch-like posture.

This is the default Sandbag Carry variation for many combat athletes.

---

# Front Carry

The bag is held higher against the chest or upper torso.

Primary benefits:

- greater anterior trunk demand,
- stronger upper-back loading,
- higher breathing restriction.

---

# Shouldered Carry

The bag is supported on one shoulder.

Primary benefits:

- anti-lateral-flexion,
- anti-rotation,
- asymmetrical object control,
- side-to-side challenge.

The athlete must switch sides to balance total volume.

---

# Front Rack Sandbag Carry

The sandbag is supported high across both forearms or shoulders.

Primary benefits:

- front-loaded posture,
- upper-back endurance,
- reduced squeezing demand,
- stronger rack-position control.

---

# Progressions

Progressions include:

1. improved pickup technique,
2. greater carry distance,
3. longer duration,
4. increased bag weight,
5. looser or more unstable fill,
6. higher carry position,
7. shouldered loading,
8. reduced rest,
9. repeated pickups,
10. asymmetrical carry patterns.

Only one or two variables should progress at a time.

---

# Regressions

Regressions include:

- lighter bag,
- shorter distance,
- shorter duration,
- tighter or more stable fill,
- lower carry position,
- bear-hug position,
- static sandbag hold,
- elevated starting position,
- fewer sets.

---

# Recommended Progression Sequence

1. Static bear-hug hold.
2. Light short-distance Bear-Hug Carry.
3. Moderate-distance carry.
4. Increased load.
5. Longer duration.
6. Higher front-loaded position.
7. Shouldered carry.
8. Offset or asymmetrical carry.
9. Repeated pickup and carry combinations.

---

# Fatigue Profile

## Systemic Fatigue

Moderate to High

Systemic fatigue rises with:

- heavy bags,
- long carries,
- repeated pickups,
- breathing compression,
- and short rest.

## Local Fatigue

High

Primary local fatigue occurs in:

- upper back,
- shoulders,
- arms,
- trunk,
- hips,
- and lower-body stabilizers.

## Neurological Fatigue

Moderate

The exercise requires continuous correction of an unstable load.

## Connective-Tissue Stress

Moderate

Stress may accumulate in:

- spine,
- shoulders,
- elbows,
- wrists,
- hips,
- and knees.

---

# Recovery Cost

Recovery cost is moderate to high.

It increases with:

- heavy bags,
- shouldered loading,
- repeated pickups,
- long-duration carries,
- and concurrent wrestling, grappling or front-squat volume.

Upper-back and trunk fatigue may persist after the session.

---

# Session Placement

The Sandbag Carry is usually placed:

- after primary power work,
- after major strength lifts,
- before low-priority accessory work,
- or in a dedicated general physical preparation block.

It should not normally precede:

- Olympic-lifting derivatives,
- heavy front squats,
- high-priority grappling,
- or technical striking requiring fresh trunk and shoulders.

When Sandbag Carry skill is the main objective, it may be placed earlier after warm-up.

---

# Weekly Placement

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 2 times per week.

A second exposure should usually use:

- lower load,
- shorter distance,
- or a different carry position.

---

# Interaction With Other Training

## Front Squats

The exercise overlaps with:

- upper-back fatigue,
- anterior trunk loading,
- breathing under compression.

## Deadlifts

Repeated sandbag pickups add hinge and trunk fatigue.

## Grappling

The Sandbag Carry has high overlap with:

- clinch posture,
- object control,
- upper-back endurance,
- trunk stiffness.

Total weekly loading must remain recoverable.

## Striking

Severe arm and trunk fatigue may reduce hand relaxation and rotational quality.

Avoid high-fatigue Sandbag Carries before priority striking sessions.

## Conditioning

The exercise can create a strong cardiovascular response.

It should not replace sport-specific conditioning without a clear reason.

# Combat Transfer

The Sandbag Carry supports combat performance by improving:

- object control,
- clinch-like posture,

- trunk stiffness,

Its transfer is especially relevant when the athlete must control a resisting or shifting opponent.

# Substitution Logic

## Substitute Front Rack Carry When

- the athlete needs a more stable front-loaded position,
- precise loading is preferred,
- or sandbag control is unnecessary.

## Substitute Bear-Hug Carry With a Medicine Ball When

- a lighter or more accessible object is required,
- the athlete is learning front-loaded locomotion,
- or sandbag weight is excessive.

## Substitute Farmer Carry When

- support grip is the primary target,
- heavy symmetrical loading is required,
- or breathing compression should be reduced.

## Substitute Suitcase Carry When

- unilateral trunk control is the main objective,
- or asymmetry assessment is required.

## Substitute Static Sandbag Hold When

- locomotion is temporarily inappropriate,
- object control is the main target,
- or gait must be removed from the task.

# Progression Criteria

The athlete may progress when:

- the sandbag remains secure,
- the trunk stays stacked,
- breathing remains controlled,
- gait remains consistent,
- the shoulders and arms maintain position,
- no pain occurs,
- and recovery is complete before the next front-loaded session.

---

# Regression Criteria

The exercise should be regressed when:

- the bag slips,
- the athlete leans backward,
- the trunk folds forward,
- breathing becomes uncontrolled,
- gait deteriorates,
- arm position fails,
- pain appears,
- or fatigue compromises subsequent training.

---

# Readiness Screen

Before prescription, assess:

- low-back pain,
- shoulder pain,
- elbow pain,
- wrist pain,
- breathing tolerance,
- gait quality,
- trunk control,
- recent front-loaded strength work,
- current grappling volume,
- and ability to lift and lower the bag safely.

---

# Safety Rules

- Inspect the sandbag before use.
- Keep the carry lane clear.
- Use a bag with secure closures.
- Lift with controlled technique.
- Keep the load close.
- Avoid uncontrolled twisting during pickup.
- Turn under control.
- Stop before the bag slips.
- Do not drop the bag near the feet.
- Lower the load safely.
- Terminate immediately if sharp pain, dizziness, numbness or sudden weakness occurs.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine may select the Sandbag Carry when:

- irregular-object locomotion is the intended adaptation,
- front-loaded or bear-hug control is relevant,
- trunk and upper-back endurance are required,
- the athlete can secure the bag safely,
- gait and breathing remain controlled,
- and total front-loaded volume remains recoverable.

The engine must avoid selecting it when:

- the athlete has active spinal, shoulder or upper-limb pain,
- the athlete cannot control the bag,
- breathing restriction is unsafe,
- or a more stable carry better matches the objective.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When the Sandbag Carry is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the intended irregular-object adaptation,
- the selected carry position,
- the reason a sandbag is preferred over a rigid implement,
- the prescribed load,
- the distance or duration,
- the expected limiting factor,
- the interaction with grappling, front-loaded strength and conditioning,
- relevant spinal, shoulder, breathing or gait constraints,
- and the progression criterion.

Example:

> Sandbag Bear-Hug Carry selected to develop irregular-object control, upper-back endurance and trunk stiffness relevant to clinch demands. Moderate-distance carries are prescribed after primary strength work. Load is limited to preserve breathing and prevent forward trunk collapse.

---

# Summary

The Sandbag Carry is a moderate-complexity irregular-object loaded locomotion exercise.

Its primary value lies in developing:

- object control,
- upper-back endurance,
- trunk stiffness,
- breathing under compression,
- gait under load,
- and whole-body bracing.

Within CAS, it is appropriate when the athlete needs to move while controlling a shifting or deformable load.

It must be programmed according to carry position, breathing tolerance, pickup skill and total front-loaded fatigue.
