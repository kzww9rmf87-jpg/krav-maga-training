Name: Farmer Carry

Category: Carries

Secondary Pattern: Isometric Grip and Whole-Body Bracing

Equipment: Dumbbells, Kettlebells, Farmer Handles, Trap Bar or Similar Implements

Complexity: Low

Unilateral or Bilateral: Bilateral

Closed or Open Chain: Open Chain

---

# Purpose

The Farmer Carry develops bilateral support-grip strength, whole-body bracing, postural endurance and gait under load.

Within CAS, it is used when the athlete must improve the ability to move while maintaining:

- secure grip,
- upright posture,
- shoulder stability,
- trunk stiffness,
- and controlled locomotion.

It is one of the simplest and most scalable loaded-carry variations.

The Farmer Carry must not be selected merely as a conditioning finisher.

Its value depends on whether bilateral loaded locomotion, support grip or general whole-body bracing is the intended adaptation.

# Movement Description

The athlete holds one load in each hand beside the body and walks for the prescribed distance or duration.

The athlete maintains:

- a secure grip,
- stable shoulders,
- stacked ribs and pelvis,
- controlled steps,
- and upright posture.

The loads should remain close to the body without striking the thighs or swinging excessively.

The set ends before grip failure, postural collapse or gait breakdown occurs.

---

# Starting Position

The athlete should begin with:

- one implement positioned on each side,
- equal loading unless asymmetry is intentional,
- feet in a stable stance,
- the spine neutral,
- the ribs stacked over the pelvis,
- the shoulders controlled,
- the wrists neutral,
- and the hands centered on the handles.

The athlete should lift the implements using a safe hinge or squat pattern.

---

# Execution

1. Position the implements evenly beside the body.
2. Establish a secure grip.
3. Brace the trunk.
4. Lift the loads safely.
5. Stand tall without leaning backward.
6. Begin walking with short, controlled steps.
7. Keep the shoulders stable and the arms long.
8. Maintain the loads close to the body.
9. Preserve even stride length and foot placement.
10. Complete the prescribed distance or duration.
11. Stop before grip or posture breaks down.
12. Set the loads down under control.

---

# Key Technical Cues

- Stand tall.
- Crush the handles.
- Keep the ribs stacked.
- Keep the shoulders down and stable.
- Walk with short, controlled steps.
- Do not let the loads swing.
- Keep the wrists neutral.
- Breathe behind the brace.
- Stop before grip failure.
- Set the weights down safely.

# Grip Demand

The Farmer Carry primarily trains bilateral support grip.

Grip demand is influenced by:

- total load,
- handle diameter,
- handle texture,
- implement balance,
- carry duration,
- walking distance,
- and accumulated fatigue.

Thick handles increase open-hand demand.

Farmer handles usually permit heavier loading than dumbbells or kettlebells.

The selected grip demand should match the intended adaptation.

---

# Trunk Demand

The Farmer Carry creates a general whole-body bracing demand.

Because the load is symmetrical, anti-lateral-flexion demand is lower than in the Suitcase Carry.

The athlete must still resist:

- spinal flexion,
- spinal extension,
- trunk rotation,
- and excessive rib flare.

The trunk should remain stable without becoming rigid enough to prevent effective breathing.

---

# Shoulder Demand

The shoulders must remain controlled against the downward pull of the loads.

The athlete should avoid:

- excessive shrugging,
- passive shoulder depression,
- forward shoulder collapse,
- and internal rotation caused by fatigue.

The shoulder blades should remain stable without forced retraction.

---

# Gait Demand

The athlete must preserve:

- even step length,
- controlled foot placement,
- stable pelvis,
- appropriate cadence,
- and smooth direction changes.

Excessively long steps often reduce trunk control.

Rushed walking reduces technical quality.

---

# Technical Complexity

Low

The Farmer Carry is easy to teach and requires minimal movement complexity.

However, heavy loading can still create technical breakdown through:

- grip failure,
- excessive trunk extension,
- shoulder elevation,
- and rushed gait.

Low complexity does not justify careless loading.

---

# Prerequisites

The athlete should demonstrate:

- pain-free walking,
- safe lifting from the floor,
- stable grip,
- controlled posture,
- basic trunk bracing,
- and the ability to set loads down safely.

No advanced carry experience is required.

---

# Eligibility Criteria

The Farmer Carry is eligible when:

- bilateral support grip is a target,
- loaded locomotion is appropriate,
- whole-body bracing is required,
- the athlete can walk safely under load,
- the equipment is suitable,
- and total grip fatigue remains recoverable.

---

# Ineligibility Criteria

The exercise is not eligible when the athlete presents:

- acute hand pain,
- wrist pain,
- elbow pain,
- shoulder pain,
- acute low-back pain,
- impaired balance,
- unsafe gait,
- inability to grip the implements securely,
- or inability to lift and lower the loads safely.

---

# Relative Contraindications

Use caution when the athlete has:

- chronic elbow irritation,
- hand or finger tendon sensitivity,
- shoulder instability,
- low-back fatigue,
- recent heavy deadlifting,
- high grappling volume,
- or significant gait asymmetry.

The engine should reduce load, distance or frequency when these factors are present.

---

# Absolute Contraindications

Do not prescribe the exercise when there is:

- acute neurological weakness,
- acute hand injury,
- acute shoulder injury,
- acute spinal injury,
- inability to walk safely,
- or an unsafe carry environment.

# Common Errors

## Excessive Trunk Extension

The athlete leans backward and flares the ribs.

Risk:

- reduced trunk control,
- increased lumbar stress.

Correction:

- stack the ribs over the pelvis and reduce load.

## Excessive Shoulder Shrugging

The athlete elevates the shoulders aggressively.

Risk:

- unnecessary neck tension,
- inefficient load support.

Correction:

- keep the shoulders controlled and the arms long.

## Passive Shoulder Collapse

The athlete allows the shoulders to be pulled forward and downward.

Risk:

- reduced upper-body stability,
- shoulder discomfort.

Correction:

- maintain active shoulder-girdle control.

## Wrist Flexion

The wrists collapse under the handle.

Risk:

- reduced grip efficiency,
- greater forearm stress.

Correction:

- keep the wrists neutral and reduce load if necessary.

## Excessive Load Swing

The implements move forward and backward with each step.

Risk:

- reduced control,
- altered gait,
- higher grip demand.

Correction:

- shorten the stride and slow the pace.

## Rushed Walking

The athlete moves too quickly.

Risk:

- poor foot placement,
- reduced posture,
- unsafe turns.

Correction:

- use controlled steps and deliberate cadence.

## Uneven Load Position

One load hangs farther forward or backward.

Risk:

- asymmetrical trunk stress,
- poor gait mechanics.

Correction:

- center both hands and align the loads.

## Continuing After Grip Failure Begins

The athlete allows the fingers to open while continuing.

Risk:

- dropped implements,
- hand injury,
- technical collapse.

Correction:

- terminate the set at the first clear loss of grip security.

# Prescription Variables

## Distance

Typical range:

- 10 to 20 metres for strength,
- 20 to 40 metres for strength endurance,
- 40 to 80 metres for work capacity.

## Duration

Typical range:

- 10 to 20 seconds for strength,
- 20 to 40 seconds for strength endurance,
- 30 to 60 seconds for work capacity.

## Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 5 sets.

## Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 4 minutes.

## Load

Load is determined by:

- athlete body mass,
- implement type,
- handle diameter,
- carry distance,
- intended adaptation,
- and grip capacity.

---

# Strength Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 5 sets,
- 10 to 20 metres,
- heavy bilateral load,
- 2 to 4 minutes rest,
- strict posture,
- no grip failure.

---

# Strength-Endurance Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 3 to 5 sets,
- 20 to 40 metres,
- moderate to heavy load,
- 90 to 180 seconds rest,
- controlled gait,
- consistent shoulder position.

---

# Work-Capacity Prescription

Typical prescription:

- 2 to 5 sets,
- 30 to 60 seconds,
- moderate load,
- incomplete but sufficient recovery,
- no technical collapse.

The athlete should not be encouraged to sacrifice posture for speed.

---

# Grip-Endurance Prescription

Typical prescription:

- moderate load,
- 20 to 45 second carries,
- 3 to 4 sets,
- controlled pace,
- termination before uncontrolled hand opening.

---

# Heavy Short-Distance Variation

Primary objective:

- maximal loaded locomotor strength.

Typical characteristics:

- heavy load,
- short distance,
- long rest,
- low repetition count,
- high grip and trunk demand.

This variation is appropriate only when the athlete can lift, carry and lower the load safely.

---

# Moderate-Distance Variation

Primary objective:

- strength endurance.

Typical characteristics:

- moderate to heavy load,
- controlled walking,
- repeated sets,
- manageable local fatigue.

This is the default variation for many athletes.

---

# Long-Duration Variation

Primary objective:

- work capacity or postural endurance.

Typical characteristics:

- moderate load,
- longer duration,
- increased cardiovascular demand,
- greater grip fatigue.

This variation must remain technically controlled.

---

# Implement Comparison

## Dumbbells

Advantages:

- widely available,
- simple setup,
- easy load progression.

Limitations:

- may strike the thighs,
- shorter handle space,
- limited maximal loading.

## Kettlebells

Advantages:

- compact,
- stable handle,
- useful for moderate loading.

Limitations:

- load may swing,
- handle diameter varies,
- weight jumps may be large.

## Farmer Handles

Advantages:

- high loading potential,
- stable load position,
- strong specificity to heavy carries.

Limitations:

- specialized equipment,
- setup may require more space.

## Trap Bar

Advantages:

- symmetrical load,
- high loading potential,
- simple pickup.

Limitations:

- less independent arm movement,
- reduced natural gait freedom,
- implement surrounds the athlete.

---

# Progressions

Progressions include:

1. improved gait and posture,
2. greater carry distance,
3. longer carry duration,
4. increased external load,
5. thicker handles,
6. reduced rest,
7. more demanding terrain,
8. slower controlled tempo,
9. repeated heavy carries,
10. transition to more complex carry variations.

The athlete should master posture before progressing load.

---

# Regressions

Regressions include:

- lighter loads,
- shorter distance,
- shorter duration,
- static bilateral hold,
- trap-bar hold,
- supported pickup,
- slower walking pace,
- more secure handle,
- fewer sets.

---

# Recommended Progression Sequence

1. Static bilateral hold.
2. Light short-distance carry.
3. Moderate-distance carry.
4. Increased load.
5. Longer duration.
6. Reduced rest.
7. Thick-handle Farmer Carry.
8. Heavy short-distance Farmer Carry.
9. Transition to asymmetrical carry variations if required.

---

# Fatigue Profile

## Systemic Fatigue

Moderate

Systemic fatigue increases with:

- heavier loading,
- longer distance,
- shorter rest,
- and repeated sets.

## Local Fatigue

High

Primary local fatigue occurs in:

- finger flexors,
- forearms,
- upper trapezius,
- shoulders,
- trunk,
- and lower-body stabilizers.

## Neurological Fatigue

Moderate

Heavy carries require high isometric force and continuous postural correction.

## Connective-Tissue Stress

Moderate

Stress may accumulate in:

- hands,
- wrists,
- elbows,
- shoulders,
- spine,
- and lower-body support tissues.

---

# Recovery Cost

Recovery cost is moderate.

It increases with:

- very heavy loads,
- thick handles,
- long carries,
- high total distance,
- repeated near-failure sets,
- and concurrent deadlift or grappling volume.

Grip fatigue may persist after general fatigue has resolved.

---

# Session Placement

The Farmer Carry is usually placed:

- after primary power work,
- after major strength lifts,
- before low-priority accessory work,
- or in a dedicated general physical preparation block.

It should not normally precede:

- heavy deadlifts when grip is limiting,
- Olympic-lifting derivatives,
- high-priority grappling,
- or technical sessions requiring fresh hands and shoulders.

When carry strength is the primary objective, it may be placed earlier after warm-up.

---

# Weekly Placement

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 2 times per week.

A third exposure may be appropriate when:

- loading is moderate,
- volume is low,
- grip recovery is strong,
- and total pulling volume is controlled.

---

# Interaction With Other Training

## Deadlifts

The Farmer Carry strongly overlaps with support-grip demand.

Place it after deadlifts when deadlift performance is the priority.

## Pull-Ups and Rows

The exercise adds hand, forearm and upper-back fatigue.

Avoid unnecessary overlap with high-volume pulling.

## Grappling

Grappling already creates grip and postural fatigue.

The engine must include combat practice in total weekly load.

## Striking

Severe shoulder and forearm fatigue may impair hand relaxation and striking quality.

Avoid high-fatigue carries before priority striking sessions.

## Lower-Body Strength

Heavy carries add axial and locomotor loading.

Total lower-body and trunk fatigue must remain recoverable.

# Substitution Logic

## Substitute Suitcase Carry When

- unilateral trunk control is the priority,
- side-to-side asymmetry must be assessed,
- or anti-lateral-flexion demand is desired.

## Substitute Front Rack Carry When

- anterior trunk loading is required,
- upper-back posture is the limitation,
- or grip fatigue should be reduced.

## Substitute Bear-Hug Carry When

- object control is the priority,
- clinch-like posture is desired,
- or handle-based grip should be minimized.

## Substitute Static Farmer Hold When

- locomotion is temporarily inappropriate,
- grip strength is the main objective,
- or gait must be removed from the task.

## Substitute Trap-Bar Carry When

- symmetrical heavy loading is required,
- pickup simplicity is important,
- or farmer handles are unavailable.

---

# Progression Criteria

The athlete may progress when:

- the grip remains secure,
- the wrists stay neutral,
- the shoulders remain stable,
- posture stays stacked,
- gait remains controlled,
- no pain occurs,
- and recovery is complete before the next grip-intensive session.

---

# Regression Criteria

The exercise should be regressed when:

- the loads swing excessively,
- the athlete leans backward,
- shoulder position deteriorates,
- grip begins to fail early,
- gait becomes rushed or uneven,
- pain appears,
- or carry fatigue compromises subsequent training.

---

# Readiness Screen

Before prescription, assess:

- hand pain,
- wrist pain,
- elbow pain,
- shoulder pain,
- low-back pain,
- balance,
- gait quality,
- recent deadlift or pulling volume,
- current grappling volume,
- and ability to lift and lower the load safely.

---

# Safety Rules

- Use secure, intact implements.
- Keep the walking lane clear.
- Wear suitable footwear.
- Lift the loads with controlled technique.
- Do not allow the weights to strike the legs.
- Turn only under control.
- Stop before grip failure.
- Do not rush.
- Maintain awareness of surrounding athletes.
- Set the loads down safely.
- Terminate immediately if sharp pain, numbness or sudden weakness occurs.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine may select the Farmer Carry when:

- bilateral loaded locomotion is the intended adaptation,
- support grip is a target,
- whole-body bracing is required,
- the athlete can walk safely under load,
- hand, shoulder, trunk and gait readiness are adequate,
- and total grip and pulling volume remains recoverable.

The engine must avoid selecting it when:

- unilateral trunk control is the actual priority,
- grip fatigue would compromise higher-priority work,
- the athlete has active hand, shoulder or low-back pain,
- or a lower-complexity static hold better matches the objective.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When the Farmer Carry is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the intended loaded-carry adaptation,
- the reason bilateral low loading is preferred,
- the selected implement,
- the prescribed load,
- the distance or duration,
- the expected limiting factor,
- the interaction with deadlifting, pulling and combat practice,
- relevant hand, shoulder, trunk or gait constraints,
- and the progression criterion.

Example:

> Farmer Carry selected to develop bilateral support grip and whole-body bracing. Moderate-distance carries are prescribed after primary strength work. Load is limited to preserve posture and avoid excessive grip interference with the next grappling session.

---

# Summary

The Farmer Carry is a low-complexity bilateral loaded locomotion exercise.

Its primary value lies in developing:

- support grip,
- grip endurance,
- whole-body bracing,
- shoulder stability,
- postural endurance,
- and gait under symmetrical load.

Within CAS, it is appropriate when the athlete needs simple, scalable loaded locomotion and bilateral grip development.

It must be programmed according to the intended adaptation rather than used automatically as a conditioning finisher.
