# CARRIES OVERVIEW

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

The Carries category defines loaded locomotion exercises used to develop grip, trunk stability, postural control, gait integrity and whole-body force transmission.

Within CAS, carries are not treated as generic finishers.

They are selected according to:

- load position,
- direction of resistance,
- grip demand,
- trunk-control requirement,
- shoulder demand,
- locomotor pattern,
- intended adaptation,
- and recovery cost.

A carry is valuable only when the load position and execution match the athlete’s needs.

---

# Philosophy

Loaded carries require the athlete to move while resisting external forces that attempt to disrupt posture, alignment and grip.

They integrate:

- force production,
- force transmission,
- gait,
- breathing,
- trunk stiffness,
- shoulder stability,
- and grip endurance.

The governing principle is:

> Carry the load without allowing the load to reorganize the athlete.

The objective is not simply to move heavy weight.

The objective is to preserve efficient posture and locomotion under meaningful resistance.

# Core Principle

Carries are classified primarily by load position.

The load position determines:

- the direction of the destabilizing force,
- the grip requirement,
- the trunk strategy,
- the shoulder demand,
- and the athlete’s tolerance.

Carries must not be selected only by implement availability.

---

# Primary Carry Families

## Bilateral Low Carries

Typical example:

- Farmer Carry.

Primary qualities:

- support grip,
- bilateral load tolerance,
- upright posture,
- whole-body bracing,
- gait under symmetrical load.

## Unilateral Low Carries

Typical example:

- Suitcase Carry.

Primary qualities:

- anti-lateral flexion,
- unilateral grip,
- pelvic control,
- trunk stability,
- asymmetry detection.

## Front-Loaded Carries

Typical examples:

- Front Rack Carry,
- Bear-Hug Carry,
- Zercher Carry.

Primary qualities:

- anterior trunk loading,
- anti-flexion,
- upper-back strength,
- breathing under compression,
- posture under forward load.

## Overhead Carries

Typical examples:

- Overhead Carry,
- Waiter Carry.

Primary qualities:

- shoulder stability,
- scapular upward rotation,
- overhead control,
- trunk alignment,
- reduced tolerance for technical error.

## Mixed-Position Carries

Typical examples:

- Offset Carry,
- Front Rack plus Suitcase Carry,
- Overhead plus Suitcase Carry.

Primary qualities:

- anti-rotation,
- asymmetrical force management,
- cross-body stabilization,
- advanced coordination.

## Grip-Specific Carries

Typical examples:

- Pinch Carry,
- Thick-Handle Carry,
- Towel Carry.

Primary qualities:

- grip-function specificity,
- thumb or open-hand demand,
- local forearm endurance.

# Exercise Roles

## Farmer Carry

Primary role:

- bilateral support grip and whole-body bracing.

Best use:

- general strength,
- grip endurance,
- posture under heavy symmetrical load.

## Suitcase Carry

Primary role:

- unilateral support grip and anti-lateral-flexion strength.

Best use:

- trunk asymmetry,
- unilateral grip,
- pelvic control.

## Front Rack Carry

Primary role:

- anterior trunk loading and upper-back posture.

Best use:

- anti-flexion,
- bracing,
- front-rack tolerance,
- combat-posture robustness.

## Overhead Carry

Primary role:

- shoulder stability and overhead force transmission.

Best use:

- scapular control,
- trunk alignment,
- shoulder robustness.

## Bear-Hug Carry

Primary role:

- front-loaded object control.

Best use:

- clinch-like posture,
- upper-back strength,
- breathing under compression,
- irregular-object handling.

## Offset Carry

Primary role:

- asymmetrical trunk control.

Best use:

- anti-rotation,
- contralateral force transfer,
- advanced postural challenge.

# Carry Selection Dimensions

The CAS Engine should classify carries through the following dimensions:

- load position,
- bilateral or unilateral loading,
- external load,
- implement type,
- grip type,
- carry distance,
- carry duration,
- walking speed,
- surface stability,
- shoulder position,
- trunk-control demand,
- and technical complexity.

Each dimension changes the adaptation.

---

# Load Position

## Low Position

The load is held beside the body.

Primary effects:

- high grip demand,
- relatively low breathing restriction,
- strong anti-lateral-flexion demand when unilateral,
- efficient heavy loading.

## Front Position

The load is held in front of the torso or at the shoulders.

Primary effects:

- upper-back demand,
- anti-flexion,
- breathing under compression,
- greater anterior trunk stress.

## Overhead Position

The load is held above the head.

Primary effects:

- shoulder stability,
- scapular control,
- anti-extension,
- high technical requirement.

## Mixed Position

Different loads are held in different positions.

Primary effects:

- anti-rotation,
- asymmetrical stabilization,
- greater coordination demand,
- reduced load tolerance.

---

# Bilateral Versus Unilateral Loading

## Bilateral Carries

Primary advantages:

- greater total external load,
- symmetrical posture,
- high support-grip demand,
- simple progression.

Primary limitations:

- reduced asymmetry detection,
- less anti-lateral-flexion demand.

## Unilateral Carries

Primary advantages:

- side-to-side comparison,
- anti-lateral-flexion training,
- unilateral grip development,
- greater pelvic-control demand.

Primary limitations:

- lower total external load,
- greater tendency to lean,
- greater technical sensitivity.

---

# Grip Demand

Carries may train:

- standard support grip,
- thick-handle support grip,
- pinch grip,
- open-hand grip,
- front-rack support,
- bear-hug object control,
- or overhead implement stabilization.

Grip demand must be classified separately from trunk demand.

A carry may be limited by:

- the hand,
- the shoulder,
- breathing,
- trunk control,
- gait,
- or total load tolerance.

The limiting factor should match the intended adaptation.

---

# Trunk Demand

Carries may emphasize:

- anti-lateral flexion,
- anti-rotation,
- anti-extension,
- anti-flexion,
- or general circumferential bracing.

## Anti-Lateral Flexion

Most strongly trained by:

- Suitcase Carry,
- unilateral low carries,
- asymmetrical carries.

## Anti-Rotation

Most strongly trained by:

- Offset Carry,
- mixed-position carries,
- asymmetrical front-rack carries.

## Anti-Extension

Most strongly trained by:

- Overhead Carry,
- front-loaded carries,
- carries performed with poor rib control if not corrected.

## Anti-Flexion

Most strongly trained by:

- Bear-Hug Carry,
- Zercher Carry,
- Front Rack Carry.

---

# Shoulder Demand

## Low Shoulder Complexity

Typical examples:

- Farmer Carry,
- Suitcase Carry.

The shoulder mainly stabilizes the load beside the body.

## Moderate Shoulder Complexity

Typical examples:

- Front Rack Carry,
- Bear-Hug Carry.

The shoulder girdle and upper back must support the load in front of the body.

## High Shoulder Complexity

Typical examples:

- Overhead Carry,
- Waiter Carry.

These require:

- pain-free overhead range,
- scapular upward rotation,
- rotator-cuff control,
- thoracic positioning,
- and trunk alignment.

---

# Gait Demand

Carries train locomotion under external load.

The athlete must preserve:

- stable foot placement,
- pelvic control,
- consistent stride length,
- appropriate walking speed,
- and alignment through the trunk.

A carry should not become a sequence of rushed steps performed only to complete distance.

The gait must remain controlled.

---

# Breathing Demand

Carries challenge breathing because the athlete must maintain trunk stiffness while continuing to ventilate.

Front-loaded carries generally create the greatest breathing restriction.

The athlete should be able to:

- maintain brace,
- avoid breath-holding for excessive periods,
- preserve rib position,
- and breathe without losing posture.

Breathing breakdown is a valid termination criterion.

---

# Physiological Profile

Carries can develop several distinct outcomes.

## Maximum Loaded Carry Strength

Characterized by:

- heavy load,
- short distance,
- long rest,
- high grip and trunk demand,
- low movement velocity.

## Strength Endurance

Characterized by:

- moderate to heavy load,
- moderate distance,
- repeated sets,
- sustained posture.

## Work Capacity

Characterized by:

- moderate load,
- longer duration,
- shorter rest,
- greater cardiovascular contribution.

## Postural Endurance

Characterized by:

- submaximal load,
- longer duration,
- strict alignment,
- low tolerance for compensation.

## Grip Endurance

Characterized by:

- sustained load,
- local forearm fatigue,
- termination before uncontrolled hand opening.

CAS must identify which outcome is intended before prescribing distance or duration.

---

# Neurological Profile

Heavy carries require:

- high motor-unit recruitment,
- strong isometric force,
- continuous postural correction,
- coordinated gait,
- and rapid stabilization against small load shifts.

Asymmetrical and overhead variations increase coordination demand.

Carries should not be progressed by instability unless instability serves a clear adaptation.

---

# Technical Complexity Classification

## Low Complexity

Typical examples:

- Farmer Carry,
- bilateral low carry.

Requirements:

- basic gait,
- stable grip,
- upright posture.

## Moderate Complexity

Typical examples:

- Suitcase Carry,
- Front Rack Carry,
- Bear-Hug Carry,
- Pinch Carry.

Requirements:

- greater trunk control,
- specific load position,
- asymmetry management,
- or specialized grip.

## High Complexity

Typical examples:

- Overhead Carry,
- mixed-position carry,
- unstable-object carry,
- highly asymmetrical carry.

Requirements:

- advanced shoulder control,
- precise trunk alignment,
- greater coordination,
- established carry competence.

---

# Intensity Determinants

Carry intensity is influenced by:

- external load,
- distance,
- duration,
- carry position,
- implement shape,
- handle thickness,
- unilateral or bilateral loading,
- walking speed,
- terrain,
- and rest interval.

External load alone does not define intensity.

A lighter overhead carry may be more demanding than a heavier Farmer Carry.

---

# General Prescription Ranges

## Maximum Strength

Typical prescription:

- 10 to 20 metres,
- 3 to 5 sets,
- heavy load,
- 2 to 4 minutes rest,
- strict posture.

## Strength Endurance

Typical prescription:

- 20 to 40 metres,
- 3 to 5 sets,
- moderate to heavy load,
- 90 to 180 seconds rest.

## Postural Endurance

Typical prescription:

- 20 to 60 seconds,
- moderate load,
- 2 to 4 sets,
- controlled gait and breathing.

## Work Capacity

Typical prescription:

- 30 to 90 seconds,
- moderate load,
- 2 to 5 sets,
- incomplete but sufficient recovery.

Work-capacity prescriptions must not cause technical collapse.

---

# Volume Metrics

Carry volume may be tracked through:

- total distance,
- total time,
- load-distance product,
- number of work sets,
- unilateral distance per side,
- or total loaded steps.

CAS must keep the selected metric consistent.

For unilateral carries, left and right volume should be tracked separately.

---

# Placement Within the Session

Carries are usually placed:

- after primary power work,
- after primary strength work,
- before low-priority accessory work,
- or in a dedicated general physical preparation block.

Carries may be placed earlier when:

- carry strength is the main objective,
- posture under load is a primary training target,
- or technical carry skill requires freshness.

Carries should not usually precede:

- high-skill Olympic-lifting derivatives,
- heavy deadlifts when grip is limiting,
- high-priority grappling,
- or technical striking requiring fresh shoulders and hands.

---

# Weekly Frequency

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 3 carry exposures per week.

Higher frequency is appropriate only when:

- loading is moderate,
- volume is low,
- variation is controlled,
- and local tissues recover well.

Heavy Farmer Carries, Overhead Carries and high-volume grip carries should be used more conservatively.

---

# Progression Logic

Carry progression may occur through:

1. improved posture,
2. improved gait control,
3. greater distance,
4. longer duration,
5. greater external load,
6. thicker handles,
7. unilateral loading,
8. more demanding load position,
9. reduced rest,
10. mixed-position loading.

The athlete should master position before load.

Only one or two variables should progress at a time.

---

# Regression Logic

Carries may be regressed by:

- reducing load,
- shortening distance,
- shortening duration,
- using bilateral loading,
- lowering the load position,
- improving handle security,
- reducing asymmetry,
- slowing the walking pace,
- using a stable object,
- or replacing walking with a static hold.

---

# Readiness Requirements

Carries should be reduced, modified or omitted when the athlete presents:

- acute hand pain,
- wrist pain,
- elbow pain,
- shoulder pain,
- low-back pain,
- impaired balance,
- severe grip fatigue,
- significant gait disruption,
- or inability to brace without symptoms.

Overhead carries require separate shoulder and thoracic readiness.

Front-loaded carries require adequate breathing tolerance.

---

# Safety Constraints

Carries must be stopped when the athlete demonstrates:

- uncontrolled leaning,
- repeated foot crossing,
- loss of grip security,
- inability to control the load,
- excessive spinal flexion or extension,
- shoulder collapse,
- breathing distress beyond the intended demand,
- sharp pain,
- numbness,
- or sudden weakness.

The walking lane must remain clear.

The athlete must be able to set the load down safely.

---

# Common Errors Across the Category

Common errors include:

- selecting load before movement quality,
- walking too quickly,
- excessive trunk leaning,
- allowing the load to strike the body,
- holding the breath for the entire set,
- using distances that exceed postural capacity,
- progressing asymmetry too early,
- treating carries only as conditioning,
- ignoring hand and elbow fatigue,
- and failing to control turns.

---

# Coaching Priorities

Primary coaching priorities are:

1. establish the correct load position,
2. create full-body tension,
3. maintain stacked ribs and pelvis,
4. preserve a controlled gait,
5. keep the shoulders stable,
6. maintain secure grip,
7. breathe without losing posture,
8. turn under control,
9. terminate before technical breakdown,
10. lower the load safely.

---

# Combat Transfer

Carries support combat performance through:

- grip persistence,
- clinch posture,
- trunk stability,
- resistance to external displacement,
- object and opponent control,
- gait under load,
- shoulder robustness,
- and force transmission between the ground and upper body.

Front-loaded and bear-hug carries may have particular relevance to clinch and body-control demands.

Unilateral carries support resistance to asymmetrical pulling and pushing.

Overhead carries provide more general shoulder-stability transfer than direct combat specificity.

# Fatigue Cost

Carries can create:

- low to high local grip fatigue,
- moderate systemic fatigue,
- moderate cardiovascular demand,
- high trunk fatigue,
- and substantial shoulder fatigue depending on load position.

Fatigue cost rises with:

- heavier loads,
- longer duration,
- overhead position,
- front-loaded compression,
- asymmetry,
- thick handles,
- and reduced rest.

CAS must account for both systemic and local recovery.

---

# Recovery Interaction

Carries interact strongly with:

- deadlifts,
- pull-ups,
- rows,
- grappling,
- overhead pressing,
- direct grip work,
- and high-volume conditioning.

Heavy carries should not be added automatically to sessions already containing large amounts of grip or trunk loading.

---

# Selection Logic

## Select Farmer Carry When

- bilateral support grip is a priority,
- heavy whole-body loading is appropriate,
- simple progression is desired,
- and symmetrical posture is preferred.

## Select Suitcase Carry When

- anti-lateral-flexion capacity is a priority,
- unilateral grip is relevant,
- or side-to-side asymmetry must be assessed.

## Select Front Rack Carry When

- anterior trunk loading is desired,
- upper-back posture is a limitation,
- or front-rack tolerance is relevant.

## Select Bear-Hug Carry When

- object control is important,
- clinch-like posture is relevant,
- or grip limitation should be reduced.

## Select Overhead Carry When

- shoulder stability is the main objective,
- overhead range is pain-free,
- and the athlete can maintain rib and pelvic alignment.

## Select Offset Carry When

- anti-rotation and asymmetrical control are priorities,
- basic carry competency is established,
- and advanced coordination is justified.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine must:

- identify the intended carry adaptation,
- select the appropriate load position,
- distinguish grip limitation from trunk limitation,
- select the lowest sufficient complexity,
- account for pulling and grappling volume,
- assess hand, shoulder, trunk and gait readiness,
- protect higher-priority technical work,
- and prescribe recoverable distance, duration and load.

The engine must not select carries simply as metabolic finishers without a defined adaptation.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When a carry is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the carry family,
- the targeted adaptation,
- the reason for the load position,
- unilateral or bilateral execution,
- the selected load,
- the prescribed distance or duration,
- the expected limiting factor,
- the interaction with grip, pulling and combat practice,
- relevant pain or readiness constraints,
- and the progression or regression rule.

Example:

> Suitcase Carry selected to develop unilateral support grip and anti-lateral-flexion strength. The athlete demonstrates stable gait and no lumbar symptoms. Moderate-distance carries are prescribed after primary strength work to avoid compromising deadlift grip.

---

# Summary

The Carries category develops the athlete’s ability to move under load while preserving grip, posture, gait and force transmission.

CAS classifies carries primarily by:

- load position,
- symmetry,
- grip demand,
- trunk demand,
- shoulder demand,
- and locomotor complexity.

Carries are effective when they are selected for a specific adaptation and performed without allowing the load to distort the athlete’s movement.

The best carry is not the heaviest or most complex variation.

It is the variation that creates the required adaptation safely, efficiently and with acceptable recovery cost.
