Name: Front Rack Carry

Category: Carries

Closed or Open Chain: Open Chain

---

# Purpose

The Front Rack Carry develops loaded locomotion under an anterior load while demanding upper-back strength, trunk stiffness, shoulder-girdle control and breathing under compression.

Within CAS, it is used when the athlete must improve the ability to move while maintaining:

- a stable front-loaded position,
- upright posture,
- thoracic control,
- rib and pelvic alignment,
- and efficient gait.

The exercise is particularly useful when grip should not be the primary limiting factor.

It must not be selected merely as a variation of the Farmer Carry.

Its value depends on whether anterior trunk loading and front-rack stability match the intended adaptation.

# Movement Description

The athlete holds one or two implements in a front-rack position and walks for the prescribed distance or duration.

The load is supported close to the shoulders and upper torso.

The athlete maintains:

- stacked ribs and pelvis,
- controlled thoracic posture,
- stable elbows,
- quiet trunk,
- and deliberate gait.

The set ends before the rack position collapses, breathing becomes uncontrolled or gait quality deteriorates.

---

# Starting Position

The athlete should begin with:

- the implements secured in the selected front-rack position,
- the elbows directed according to the implement,
- the wrists controlled,
- the shoulders stable,
- the ribs stacked over the pelvis,
- the trunk braced,
- and the feet in a stable stance.

The load should rest on or near the shoulders rather than being held entirely by the arms.

---

# Execution

1. Clean or position the implement safely into the front rack.
2. Establish secure wrist, elbow and shoulder positions.
3. Brace the trunk.
4. Stand tall without excessive lumbar extension.
5. Begin walking with short, controlled steps.
6. Keep the load close to the torso.
7. Maintain stable elbows and shoulders.
8. Breathe without losing rib position.
9. Preserve even stride length.
10. Complete the prescribed distance or duration.
11. Stop before rack collapse or postural breakdown.
12. Lower the load safely.

---

# Key Technical Cues

- Keep the load close.
- Stack the ribs over the pelvis.
- Keep the elbows stable.
- Stay tall.
- Brace and breathe.
- Do not lean backward.
- Walk with controlled steps.
- Keep the upper back active.
- Stop before the rack position collapses.

# Load-Position Demand

The Front Rack Carry places the load anterior to the body’s center of mass.

This increases demand on:

- the upper back,
- anterior trunk,
- thoracic posture,
- and breathing control.

Compared with a Farmer Carry, the exercise usually creates:

- less direct support-grip demand,
- greater postural compression,
- more upper-back fatigue,
- and a stronger tendency toward trunk extension or flexion.

---

# Trunk Demand

The athlete must resist:

- forward trunk collapse,
- excessive lumbar extension,
- rib flare,
- trunk rotation,
- and loss of pelvic position.

The carry should produce strong bracing without forcing the athlete into prolonged breath-holding.

---

# Shoulder Demand

Shoulder demand depends on the implement.

## Kettlebells

The load rests partly on the forearms and shoulders.

Demand includes:

- rack stability,
- scapular control,
- wrist tolerance,
- upper-back endurance.

## Dumbbells

The athlete must control the load more actively.

Demand includes:

- greater arm contribution,
- wrist control,
- front-delt endurance.

## Barbell

The exercise requires:

- front-rack mobility,
- thoracic extension,
- shoulder and wrist tolerance,
- stable elbow position.

## Sandbag

The load may create:

- broader torso contact,
- greater breathing compression,
- reduced wrist demand,
- more irregular load movement.

---

# Breathing Demand

The front-rack position may restrict rib expansion.

The athlete should be able to:

- breathe behind the brace,
- avoid excessive shoulder elevation,
- preserve trunk alignment,
- and maintain controlled cadence.

Breathing breakdown is a valid reason to terminate the set.

---

# Technical Complexity

Moderate

The walking pattern is simple, but the rack position requires greater technical control than a low carry.

The athlete must coordinate:

- rack security,
- shoulder position,
- trunk stiffness,
- breathing,
- and gait.

Complexity rises with:

- unilateral loading,
- barbell front rack,
- asymmetrical loading,
- or unstable implements.

---

# Prerequisites

The athlete should demonstrate:

- pain-free walking,
- safe implement positioning,
- adequate front-rack tolerance,
- controlled wrist and shoulder position,
- stable trunk bracing,
- and the ability to lower the load safely.

Barbell Front Rack Carries require sufficient wrist, shoulder and thoracic mobility.

---

# Eligibility Criteria

The Front Rack Carry is eligible when:

- anterior trunk loading is a target,
- upper-back posture is a limitation,
- grip should not be the main limiting factor,
- the athlete can maintain a stable rack position,
- gait is safe,
- and total trunk and shoulder fatigue remains recoverable.

---

# Ineligibility Criteria

The exercise is not eligible when the athlete presents:

- acute wrist pain,
- elbow pain,
- shoulder pain,
- front-rack intolerance,
- inability to breathe under the load,
- acute low-back pain,
- impaired balance,
- unsafe gait,
- or inability to position and lower the load safely.

---

# Relative Contraindications

Use caution when the athlete has:

- limited wrist extension,
- shoulder mobility restrictions,
- thoracic stiffness,
- cervical discomfort,
- breathing limitations,
- recent heavy front squatting,
- or high upper-back fatigue.

The engine should modify the implement, load, distance or rack position when necessary.

---

# Absolute Contraindications

Do not prescribe the exercise when there is:

- acute shoulder injury,
- acute wrist injury,
- acute elbow injury,
- acute spinal injury,
- inability to maintain a safe rack,
- or inability to walk safely under load.

# Common Errors

## Excessive Lumbar Extension

The athlete leans backward to support the load.

Risk:

- increased lumbar stress,
- reduced trunk control.

Correction:

- reduce load,
- stack the ribs over the pelvis,
- shorten the carry.

## Elbows Dropping

The elbows collapse as fatigue increases.

Risk:

- loss of rack security,
- greater arm fatigue,
- forward trunk collapse.

Correction:

- reduce load or duration,
- restore upper-back tension.

## Wrist Collapse

The wrists extend or flex excessively.

Risk:

- discomfort,
- poor load transfer,
- reduced rack stability.

Correction:

- adjust implement or grip,
- reduce load,
- improve rack setup.

## Shoulder Elevation

The athlete shrugs continuously.

Risk:

- neck tension,
- inefficient breathing,
- early fatigue.

Correction:

- maintain stable shoulders without forcing them downward.

## Excessive Forward Lean

The athlete folds over the load.

Risk:

- loss of posture,
- increased spinal stress,
- impaired gait.

Correction:

- reduce load and restore stacked alignment.

## Breath-Holding Throughout the Set

The athlete maintains one prolonged breath hold.

Risk:

- rapid fatigue,
- dizziness,
- postural breakdown.

Correction:

- use controlled breathing behind the brace.

## Rushed Walking

The athlete moves too quickly.

Risk:

- rack instability,
- poor foot placement,
- excessive load movement.

Correction:

- use short, deliberate steps.

# Prescription Variables

## Distance

Typical range:

- 10 to 20 metres for strength,
- 20 to 40 metres for strength endurance,
- 30 to 60 metres for postural endurance.

## Duration

Typical range:

- 10 to 20 seconds for strength,
- 20 to 40 seconds for strength endurance,
- 30 to 60 seconds for postural endurance.

## Sets

Typical range:

- 3 to 5 sets.

## Rest

Typical range:

- 90 seconds to 4 minutes.

## Load

Load is determined by:

- implement type,
- bilateral or unilateral execution,
- rack tolerance,
- distance,
- breathing capacity,
- and intended adaptation.

# Bilateral Variation

The athlete holds one implement in each hand or a single barbell across both shoulders.

Primary benefits:

- symmetrical anterior loading,
- greater total load,
- strong upper-back demand,
- reduced rotational challenge.

---

# Unilateral Variation

The athlete holds one implement in a single front-rack position.

Primary benefits:

- anti-rotation demand,
- side-to-side assessment,
- greater trunk asymmetry,
- unilateral shoulder stabilization.

The athlete must resist rotation and lateral shift.

---

# Double Kettlebell Front Rack Carry

Primary characteristics:

- strong upper-back and trunk demand,
- moderate grip demand,
- significant breathing compression,
- high practical value.

---

# Barbell Front Rack Carry

Primary characteristics:

- high front-rack specificity,
- substantial thoracic and upper-back demand,
- reduced hand-support demand,
- greater mobility requirement.

---

# Sandbag Front Carry

Primary characteristics:

- broad torso contact,
- irregular object control,
- lower wrist demand,
- greater breathing restriction.

---

# Progressions

Progressions include:

1. improved rack position,
2. greater distance,
3. longer duration,
4. increased load,
5. unilateral loading,
6. asymmetrical loading,
7. reduced rest,
8. more demanding implement,
9. slower controlled cadence,
10. mixed-position carry.

The rack position must remain stable before load increases.

---

# Regressions

Regressions include:

- lighter load,
- shorter distance,
- shorter duration,
- bilateral execution,
- goblet carry,
- bear-hug carry,
- static front-rack hold,
- more comfortable implement,
- supported setup.

---

# Recommended Progression Sequence

1. Static front-rack hold.
2. Light bilateral carry.
3. Moderate-distance bilateral carry.
4. Increased load.
5. Longer duration.
6. Unilateral Front Rack Carry.
7. Reduced rest.
8. Barbell or heavier double-kettlebell variation.
9. Offset or mixed-position carry.

# Fatigue Profile

## Systemic Fatigue

Moderate

Systemic fatigue rises with:

- heavier loads,
- longer duration,
- breathing restriction,
- and repeated sets.

## Local Fatigue

Moderate to High

Primary local fatigue occurs in:

- upper back,
- shoulders,
- trunk,
- elbow flexors,
- and lower-body stabilizers.

## Neurological Fatigue

Moderate

The exercise requires sustained isometric control and continuous postural correction.

## Connective-Tissue Stress

Moderate

Stress may accumulate in:

- wrists,
- elbows,
- shoulders,
- upper back,
- and spine.

---

# Recovery Cost

Recovery cost is moderate.

It increases with:

- heavy double-kettlebell carries,
- barbell front-rack loading,
- long-duration sets,
- unilateral loading,
- and concurrent front-squat or pressing volume.

Upper-back and trunk fatigue may persist even when grip remains fresh.

---

# Session Placement

The Front Rack Carry is usually placed:

- after primary power work,
- after major strength lifts,
- before low-priority accessory work,
- or in a dedicated general physical preparation block.

It should not normally precede:

- front squats,
- Olympic-lifting derivatives,
- high-priority overhead pressing,
- intensive grappling,
- or technical sessions requiring fresh shoulders and trunk.

When front-loaded carry strength is the primary objective, it may be placed earlier after warm-up.

---

# Weekly Placement

Typical frequency:

- 1 to 2 times per week.

A second exposure should usually use:

- lower load,
- shorter distance,
- or a different implement.

---

# Interaction With Other Training

## Front Squats

The exercise overlaps strongly with:

- upper-back fatigue,
- front-rack tolerance,
- trunk bracing.

Place it after front squats when front-squat performance is the priority.

## Overhead Pressing

Front-rack loading may fatigue the shoulders and upper back.

Avoid excessive overlap.

## Grappling

Front-loaded carries may support clinch posture but can also add upper-back and breathing fatigue.

## Striking

Excessive front-rack fatigue may reduce shoulder relaxation and trunk rotation quality.

Avoid high-fatigue carries before priority striking sessions.

## Deadlifts

Grip overlap is lower than in Farmer Carries, but trunk fatigue may still affect heavy pulling.

# Substitution Logic

## Substitute Farmer Carry When

- bilateral support grip is the priority,
- heavier loading is desired,
- or front-rack tolerance is limited.

## Substitute Bear-Hug Carry When

- object control is more relevant,
- wrist demand should be reduced,
- or broader torso contact is preferred.

## Substitute Goblet Carry When

- a simpler front-loaded position is required,
- bilateral rack skill is insufficient,
- or lower technical complexity is desired.

## Substitute Suitcase Carry When

- unilateral anti-lateral-flexion strength is the main objective,
- or trunk asymmetry must be assessed.

## Substitute Static Front-Rack Hold When

- locomotion is temporarily inappropriate,
- rack tolerance is the primary target,
- or gait must be removed from the task.

# Progression Criteria

The athlete may progress when:

- the rack remains secure,
- the elbows remain stable,
- the wrists remain controlled,
- the ribs stay stacked,
- breathing remains manageable,
- gait remains consistent,
- no pain occurs,
- and recovery is complete before the next front-loaded session.

---

# Regression Criteria

The exercise should be regressed when:

- the elbows drop,
- the wrists collapse,
- the athlete leans backward,
- the trunk folds forward,
- breathing becomes uncontrolled,
- gait deteriorates,
- pain appears,
- or fatigue compromises subsequent training.

- front-rack tolerance,

- low-back symptoms,

# Safety Rules

- Use a secure rack position.
- Keep the walking lane clear.
- Wear suitable footwear.
- Do not force painful wrist extension.
- Maintain control during turns.
- Stop before rack collapse.
- Avoid prolonged breath-holding.
- Keep the load close to the body.
- Lower the implement safely.
- Terminate immediately if sharp pain, dizziness, numbness or sudden weakness occurs.

---

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine may select the Front Rack Carry when:

- front-loaded locomotion is the intended adaptation,
- upper-back posture is a limitation,
- anterior trunk stability is required,
- grip should not be the main limiting factor,
- the athlete can tolerate the selected rack position,
- and total shoulder and trunk volume remains recoverable.

The engine must avoid selecting it when:

- the athlete has active wrist, elbow or shoulder pain,
- front-rack mobility is insufficient,
- breathing restriction is unsafe,
- or a simpler carry better matches the objective.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

When the Front Rack Carry is selected, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the intended front-loaded adaptation,
- the reason the front-rack position is preferred,
- the selected implement,
- bilateral or unilateral execution,
- the prescribed load,
- the distance or duration,
- the expected limiting factor,
- the interaction with front squats, pressing and combat practice,
- relevant wrist, shoulder, trunk or breathing constraints,
- and the progression criterion.

Example:

> Front Rack Carry selected to develop upper-back endurance and anterior trunk stability without making grip the primary limiter. Double-kettlebell carries are prescribed after primary strength work with moderate distance to preserve front-rack quality and breathing control.

---

# Summary

The Front Rack Carry is a moderate-complexity front-loaded locomotion exercise.

Its primary value lies in developing:

- upper-back endurance,
- anterior trunk stability,
- postural control,
- shoulder-girdle stability,
- breathing under compression,
- and gait under load.

Within CAS, it is appropriate when the athlete needs stronger posture under anterior loading and can maintain a safe, stable rack position.

It must be programmed according to rack tolerance, breathing capacity and total shoulder and trunk fatigue.
