# CARRIES EXERCISE COMPARISON

Version 1.0

---

# Purpose

This document compares the exercises currently included in the CAS Carries category.

Its purpose is to help the CAS Engine distinguish between carry variations that may all involve loaded walking but create different demands through:

- load position,
- grip requirement,
- trunk strategy,
- shoulder involvement,
- breathing restriction,
- technical complexity,
- and recovery cost.

The exercises compared are:

- Farmer Carry
- Front Rack Carry
- Sandbag Carry
- Zercher Carry

The comparison must not be used to identify one universally superior carry.

Each exercise is appropriate only when it matches:

- the intended adaptation,
- the athlete’s current capability,
- the desired load position,
- the available equipment,
- tissue readiness,
- and the surrounding training load.

---

# Core Principle

> Select the carry position that creates the required adaptation with the lowest unnecessary complexity, fatigue and risk.

Carries are not interchangeable.

The same walking distance can create very different effects depending on whether the load is:

- held beside the body,
- supported at the shoulders,
- compressed against the torso,
- or cradled in the elbows.

The CAS Engine must identify the required loading strategy before selecting the exercise.

# Primary Adaptation Comparison

## Farmer Carry

Primary adaptation:

- bilateral loaded locomotor strength with support-grip emphasis.

Best suited for:

- bilateral grip development,
- whole-body bracing,
- heavy symmetrical loading,
- simple progression,
- general physical preparation.

Not ideal when:

- grip should not be the main limiter,
- anti-flexion strength is the primary goal,
- irregular-object control is required,
- or shoulder-level front loading is desired.

---

## Front Rack Carry

Primary adaptation:

- front-loaded locomotor strength with upper-back and trunk stabilization.

Best suited for:

- front-rack tolerance,
- anterior trunk control,
- upper-back endurance,
- posture under shoulder-level loading,
- breathing under compression.

Not ideal when:

- wrist or shoulder mobility is limited,
- the athlete cannot maintain a stable rack,
- grip development is the main objective,
- or irregular-object control is required.

---

## Sandbag Carry

Primary adaptation:

- irregular-object loaded locomotion.

Best suited for:

- object control,
- clinch-like posture,
- breathing under compression,
- upper-back endurance,
- movement with a shifting load.

Not ideal when:

- precise loading is required,
- the athlete cannot secure the bag,
- equipment standardization is important,
- or a simpler stable carry is sufficient.

---

## Zercher Carry

Primary adaptation:

- front-loaded anti-flexion strength with elbow-supported loading.

Best suited for:

- upper-back endurance,
- anterior trunk stiffness,
- body-lock posture,
- reducing grip limitation,
- heavy front-loaded walking.

Not ideal when:

- elbow pressure is poorly tolerated,
- distal biceps or elbow tissues are irritated,
- the athlete cannot maintain upright posture,
- or a more comfortable carry can provide the same adaptation.

---

# Load-Position Comparison

## Bilateral Low Position

Primary exercise:

- Farmer Carry.

Advantages:

- high loading potential,
- simple setup,
- strong support-grip stimulus,
- relatively low breathing restriction,
- easy progression.

Limitations:

- grip may fail before the trunk,
- lower anti-flexion demand,
- less front-loaded combat transfer.

---

## Shoulder-Level Front Position

Primary exercise:

- Front Rack Carry.

Advantages:

- high upper-back demand,
- strong front-loaded posture stimulus,
- less direct hand-grip limitation,
- good transfer to front-rack strength.

Limitations:

- wrist and shoulder mobility requirements,
- greater breathing restriction,
- possible elbow-position collapse.

---

## Irregular Front Position

Primary exercise:

- Sandbag Carry.

Advantages:

- object-control demand,
- unstable load behavior,
- broad torso contact,
- high combat relevance,
- multiple carry positions.

Limitations:

- lower loading precision,
- greater setup variation,
- more technical object management,
- higher difficulty standardizing progression.

---

## Elbow-Cradled Front Position

Primary exercise:

- Zercher Carry.

Advantages:

- high anti-flexion demand,
- reduced hand-grip limitation,
- strong upper-back stimulus,
- useful body-lock posture,
- heavy front-loaded capacity.

Limitations:

- elbow-crease discomfort,
- distal biceps stress,
- breathing restriction,
- greater front-loaded recovery cost.

---

# Grip Demand Comparison

## Highest Grip Demand

Farmer Carry

Primary grip function:

- bilateral support grip.

Best when:

- grip strength or grip endurance is part of the objective.

---

## Moderate Grip Demand

Sandbag Carry

Grip demand varies by:

- bag shape,
- carry position,
- handle access,
- and arm wrap.

The athlete may rely more on:

- squeezing,
- friction,
- forearm pressure,
- and object compression.

---

## Low to Moderate Grip Demand

Front Rack Carry

The load is supported largely by:

- shoulders,
- forearms,
- upper back,
- and rack position.

Grip should not normally be the primary limiter.

---

## Lowest Direct Grip Demand

Zercher Carry

The load is supported mainly through:

- elbow creases,
- forearms,
- and upper arms.

This makes the exercise useful when front-loaded strength is desired without high hand fatigue.

---

# Trunk-Demand Comparison

## General Bracing

Best represented by:

- Farmer Carry.

Primary demands:

- anti-extension,
- anti-flexion,
- postural endurance,
- symmetrical gait control.

---

## Anterior Trunk Stability

Best represented by:

- Front Rack Carry.

Primary demands:

- anti-extension,
- upper-back posture,
- rib-pelvis control,
- breathing behind the brace.

---

## Anti-Flexion and Irregular-Load Control

Best represented by:

- Sandbag Carry.

Primary demands:

- resistance to forward collapse,
- response to shifting load,
- trunk stiffness,
- object stabilization.

---

## Highest Anti-Flexion Demand

Best represented by:

- Zercher Carry.

Primary demands:

- resistance to forward trunk flexion,
- upper-back endurance,
- anterior-load posture,
- body-lock bracing.

---

# Shoulder and Upper-Back Demand Comparison

## Farmer Carry

Shoulder role:

- resist downward traction,
- maintain neutral arm position,
- stabilize the scapulae.

Upper-back demand:

- moderate.

---

## Front Rack Carry

Shoulder role:

- maintain front-rack position,
- stabilize the load near the shoulders,
- control the elbows.

Upper-back demand:

- high.

---

## Sandbag Carry

Shoulder role:

- maintain arm pressure,
- stabilize an irregular load,
- adapt to bag movement.

Upper-back demand:

- high.

---

## Zercher Carry

Shoulder role:

- maintain load close to the torso,
- stabilize the upper arms.

Upper-back demand:

- high to very high.

---

# Breathing-Demand Comparison

## Lowest Breathing Restriction

Farmer Carry

The torso remains relatively open.

Breathing is challenged mainly by:

- total load,
- bracing,
- and exercise duration.

---

## Moderate to High Breathing Restriction

Front Rack Carry

The rack position may limit rib expansion.

---

## High Breathing Restriction

Sandbag Carry

Compression depends on:

- bag size,
- carry height,
- and how tightly the bag is hugged.

---

## High Breathing Restriction

Zercher Carry

The load compresses the anterior torso and requires strong bracing.

---

# Technical Complexity Comparison

## Low Complexity

Farmer Carry

Appropriate for:

- beginners,
- simple heavy loading,
- straightforward bilateral progression.

---

## Moderate Complexity

Front Rack Carry

Requires:

- secure rack position,
- wrist and shoulder tolerance,
- upper-back control,
- breathing management.

Sandbag Carry

Requires:

- safe pickup,
- object control,
- adaptation to load movement,
- secure carry position.

Zercher Carry

Requires:

- safe elbow placement,
- front-loaded posture,
- elbow tolerance,
- controlled breathing.

---

Farmer Carry

---

---

Sandbag Carry

Zercher Carry

# Selection by Primary Objective

## Objective: Develop Bilateral Support Grip

Select:

1. Farmer Carry

Avoid selecting a front-loaded carry when grip is the primary target unless there is a specific reason.

---

## Objective: Develop Upper-Back Endurance Under Front Load

Select:

1. Front Rack Carry
2. Zercher Carry
3. Sandbag Carry

Default choice:

- Front Rack Carry when rack position is tolerated.
- Zercher Carry when grip limitation should be minimized.
- Sandbag Carry when object control also matters.

---

## Objective: Develop Irregular-Object Control

Select:

1. Sandbag Carry

---

## Objective: Develop Anti-Flexion Strength

Select:

1. Zercher Carry
2. Sandbag Carry
3. Front Rack Carry

---

## Objective: Develop Breathing Under Compression

Select:

1. Sandbag Carry
2. Zercher Carry
3. Front Rack Carry

---

## Objective: Minimize Grip Fatigue

Select:

1. Zercher Carry
2. Front Rack Carry
3. Sandbag Carry

Avoid:

- heavy Farmer Carry.

---

## Objective: Maximize Loading Simplicity

Select:

1. Farmer Carry
2. Front Rack Carry

---

## Objective: Maximize Combat-Like Object Control

Select:

1. Sandbag Carry
2. Zercher Carry

# Selection by Athlete Level

## Beginner

Preferred exercises:

- Farmer Carry
- light Front Rack Carry
- light Bear-Hug Sandbag Carry
- static Zercher hold before full carry.

## Intermediate

Preferred exercises:

- Farmer Carry
- Front Rack Carry
- Sandbag Carry
- Zercher Carry.

## Advanced

Eligible exercises:

- all listed carries,
- heavier loading,
- longer distances,
- asymmetrical positions,
- repeated pickups,
- more unstable implements.

Advanced status does not remove tissue-readiness requirements.

# Selection by Limiting Factor

## Weak Grip

Select:

- Farmer Carry.

## Poor Front-Rack Posture

Select:

- Front Rack Carry.

## Poor Object Control

Select:

- Sandbag Carry.

## Poor Anti-Flexion Strength

Select:

- Zercher Carry.

## Poor Breathing Under Load

Select progressively:

- Front Rack Carry,
- Sandbag Carry,
- Zercher Carry.

## Elbow Sensitivity

Avoid:

- Zercher Carry.

Prefer:

- Farmer Carry,
- Front Rack Carry,
- Sandbag Bear-Hug Carry when comfortable.

## Wrist Mobility Limitation

Avoid or modify:

- barbell Front Rack Carry.

Prefer:

- Zercher Carry,
- Sandbag Carry,
- Farmer Carry.

## High Grappling Grip Volume

Prefer:

- Front Rack Carry,
- Zercher Carry,
- or Sandbag Carry with reduced hand demand.

Avoid unnecessary heavy Farmer Carries.

# Default CAS Selection Hierarchy

When multiple carry exercises are eligible, CAS should generally use the following hierarchy:

1. identify the intended adaptation,
2. determine the required load position,
3. determine whether grip should be a limiter,
4. determine whether irregular-object control is required,
5. assess trunk, shoulder, elbow and breathing readiness,
6. select the lowest sufficient complexity,
7. account for interference with strength and combat practice,
8. prescribe recoverable load, distance and duration,
9. define progression and regression rules.

---

# Default Selection Rules

## Choose Farmer Carry When

- support grip is a major target,
- symmetrical heavy loading is appropriate,
- simple progression is preferred,
- and front-loaded compression is unnecessary.

## Choose Front Rack Carry When

- upper-back endurance and rack posture are priorities,
- the athlete tolerates shoulder-level loading,
- grip should not be the main limiter,
- and a stable implement is preferred.

## Choose Sandbag Carry When

- irregular-object control is required,
- clinch-like loading is relevant,
- breathing under compression is part of the objective,
- and the athlete can manage the pickup safely.

## Choose Zercher Carry When

- anti-flexion strength is a priority,
- body-lock posture is relevant,
- grip limitation should be minimized,
- and the athlete tolerates elbow-supported loading.

# CAS Engine Rules

The CAS Engine must not select a carry solely because it is heavier, more difficult or more sport-like.

The engine must:

- identify the primary carry adaptation,
- distinguish low-position from front-loaded demand,
- distinguish grip limitation from trunk limitation,
- determine whether object instability is useful,
- select the lowest sufficient complexity,
- assess local tissue readiness,
- account for breathing restriction,
- account for all grip, squat, deadlift and combat exposure,
- and justify the selection in the Decision Trace.

The engine should prefer the simpler carry when two options provide the same required adaptation.

---

# Decision Trace Requirements

For every Carry exercise selection, the Decision Trace should identify:

- the primary adaptation,
- the selected load position,
- the reason the exercise is preferred over alternatives,
- whether grip is intended to limit the set,
- the selected implement,
- the load,
- the distance or duration,
- the expected fatigue cost,
- the interaction with strength and combat practice,
- relevant hand, wrist, elbow, shoulder, trunk or breathing constraints,
- and the progression or regression rule.

Example:

> Zercher Carry selected instead of Farmer Carry because the objective is anti-flexion strength and body-lock posture rather than support-grip development. The athlete demonstrates adequate elbow tolerance and front-loaded bracing. Moderate-distance carries are prescribed after primary strength work to limit interference with grappling recovery.

# Summary

The Carries chapter contains exercises that differ primarily through load position and limiting factor.

Farmer Carry is the primary option for bilateral support grip and simple whole-body bracing.

Front Rack Carry emphasizes upper-back posture, anterior trunk stability and breathing under shoulder-level loading.

Sandbag Carry develops irregular-object control, clinch-like posture and movement with a shifting load.

Zercher Carry emphasizes anti-flexion strength and anterior-load locomotion while reducing direct hand-grip limitation.

The correct CAS selection depends on:

- the intended adaptation,
- the required load position,
- the desired limiting factor,
- the athlete’s technical competence,
- tissue readiness,
- acceptable fatigue cost,
- and interaction with surrounding training.

No carry is universally superior.

The best exercise is the carry that produces the required adaptation safely, efficiently and without unnecessary interference.
