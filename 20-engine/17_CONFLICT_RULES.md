# CONFLICT RULES

Version 0.1

---

# Purpose

The Conflict Rules define how the Combat Athlete System detects, evaluates and resolves incompatible training demands.

A conflict exists when two or more valid training elements cannot be combined without reducing safety, adaptation quality, recovery or long-term progression.

Conflicts may occur between:

* exercises,
* Capability Modules,
* session objectives,
* training methods,
* weekly sessions,
* physical qualities,
* combat practice,
* recovery needs,
* athlete constraints,
* and competition demands.

The fundamental principle is:

> When two valid elements compete, the system must protect the highest-priority adaptation and reduce the lowest-value cost.

The system must not attempt to maximize every quality at the same time.

It must organize priorities.

---

# Core Principle

A training element may be individually appropriate and still be inappropriate in combination with another element.

Examples:

* heavy lower-body strength may be valid,
* hard kicking practice may be valid,
* but placing both at high volume within insufficient recovery may create a conflict.

The engine must therefore evaluate:

1. the quality of each element,
2. the interaction between elements,
3. the timing of those elements,
4. the total recovery cost,
5. and the athlete’s current priorities.

---

# Conflict Resolution Hierarchy

When a conflict is detected, the system resolves it in this order:

1. Protect safety
2. Protect medical and rehabilitation constraints
3. Protect competition readiness
4. Protect the primary sport objective
5. Protect the primary session adaptation
6. Protect technical quality
7. Protect recovery capacity
8. Protect long-term development
9. Protect athlete adherence
10. Preserve optional volume only if compatible

Lower-priority elements must never override higher-priority constraints.

---

# Scope

Conflict detection applies at several levels:

## Exercise Level

Conflict between individual exercises in the same session.

## Module Level

Conflict between Capability Modules.

## Session Level

Conflict between session components or objectives.

## Microcycle Level

Conflict between sessions within the same week.

## Mesocycle Level

Conflict between training phases or adaptation priorities.

## Athlete Level

Conflict between the program and the athlete’s current state.

## Combat Integration Level

Conflict between physical preparation and technical combat practice.

## Competition Level

Conflict between training load and performance readiness.

---

# Conflict Detection Pipeline

Every planned session or training week must pass through the following process:

1. Identify the primary objective.
2. Identify all secondary objectives.
3. Identify mandatory athlete constraints.
4. Estimate the demand of each planned element.
5. Identify overlapping tissues, patterns and energy systems.
6. Identify timing and recovery requirements.
7. detect potential conflicts.
8. classify conflict severity.
9. resolve the conflict.
10. recalculate the session or week.
11. explain any material change.
12. record the decision.

A session must not be finalized while a severe unresolved conflict remains.

---

# Rule 1 — One Primary Objective

Every session must have one clearly defined primary objective.

Examples:

* maximum strength,
* explosive power,
* aerobic development,
* repeated-sprint ability,
* striking power,
* technical striking,
* hypertrophy,
* robustness,
* recovery.

Secondary objectives may be included only if they do not compromise the primary objective.

A session with several equally dominant objectives is considered structurally conflicted.

---

# Rule 2 — Priority Must Be Explicit

When multiple qualities are trained, the system must define their order of importance.

Example:

```text
Primary Objective:
Lower-Body Power

Secondary Objective:
Maximum Strength

Tertiary Objective:
Hamstring Robustness
```

The engine must preserve this order when:

* time is reduced,
* readiness is low,
* fatigue is excessive,
* or conflict resolution is required.

---

# Rule 3 — Safety Overrides Adaptation

Any conflict involving safety must be resolved immediately.

Examples:

* high-impact plyometrics with poor landing control,
* maximal loading under severe fatigue,
* sprinting on an unsafe surface,
* striking through significant joint pain,
* unsupervised maximal bench press without safeties,
* ballistic lifting beyond technical competence.

The unsafe element must be:

* removed,
* regressed,
* replaced,
* or postponed.

Safety conflicts are not solved through score compensation.

---

# Rule 4 — Pain Overrides Performance

Pain-related constraints take priority over performance targets.

The system must distinguish between:

* mild muscular discomfort,
* tolerable training sensation,
* joint pain,
* sharp pain,
* radiating pain,
* neurological symptoms,
* instability,
* and worsening symptoms.

When pain changes movement quality, force output or confidence, the planned exercise becomes conflicted with safe training.

The system does not diagnose injuries.

Persistent, significant or neurological symptoms require assessment by an appropriate health professional.

---

# Rule 5 — Medical Instructions Override Program Logic

Medical, rehabilitation or return-to-sport restrictions override:

* exercise preference,
* performance goals,
* module priority,
* scoring output,
* and progression plans.

The engine must not reinterpret or bypass a medical restriction.

When medical instructions are unclear, the system should choose the safer valid option.

---

# Rule 6 — Combat Practice Priority

For a combat athlete, technical and tactical practice generally has higher priority than general physical preparation.

Physical preparation must support:

* skill acquisition,
* sparring quality,
* movement precision,
* striking quality,
* grappling quality,
* and competition readiness.

Physical preparation becomes conflicted when it repeatedly reduces the quality of combat practice.

Examples:

* grip fatigue before grappling,
* shoulder fatigue before striking,
* leg soreness before kicking,
* neck fatigue before wrestling,
* exhaustive conditioning before technical learning.

---

# Rule 7 — Competition Priority

As competition approaches, readiness becomes increasingly important.

Near competition, the system should prioritize:

* performance quality,
* speed,
* confidence,
* technical precision,
* low soreness,
* low injury risk,
* and recovery.

The following may conflict with competition readiness:

* unfamiliar exercises,
* high-eccentric-volume work,
* maximal attempts,
* excessive hypertrophy volume,
* high-impact plyometrics,
* exhaustive conditioning,
* and heavy fatigue accumulation.

Competition proximity increases the severity of fatigue conflicts.

---

# Rule 8 — Adaptation Interference

Some adaptations may interfere when trained with excessive volume, poor timing or insufficient recovery.

Common interference categories include:

* strength and endurance,
* power and fatigue,
* hypertrophy and technical speed,
* maximal strength and high-volume combat practice,
* plyometrics and impact-heavy combat sessions,
* recovery work and high-intensity conditioning.

Interference is not always absolute.

It depends on:

* volume,
* intensity,
* training order,
* timing,
* athlete level,
* nutrition,
* sleep,
* and total weekly load.

---

# Rule 9 — Strength and Endurance Conflict

Strength and endurance may coexist, but conflict increases when:

* both are high volume,
* both target the same muscles,
* recovery time is short,
* endurance work is highly fatiguing,
* the athlete is under-recovered,
* or maximum strength is the primary goal.

When strength is the priority:

* strength should usually be performed before endurance,
* endurance volume should be controlled,
* low-impact modalities may be preferred,
* and high-intensity conditioning should be separated when possible.

When endurance is the priority:

* heavy strength volume may be reduced,
* maintenance strength may replace development volume,
* and muscle damage should be minimized.

---

# Rule 10 — Power and Fatigue Conflict

Power requires:

* high movement velocity,
* high neural quality,
* precise technique,
* and low fatigue.

Power work conflicts with:

* muscular failure,
* exhaustive conditioning,
* high soreness,
* poor sleep,
* technical breakdown,
* and excessive prior volume.

Power exercises must generally be placed before high-fatigue work.

If speed or height clearly decreases, the power block must be:

* stopped,
* reduced,
* or replaced.

---

# Rule 11 — Speed and Resistance Conflict

Speed development requires low enough resistance to preserve maximal movement velocity.

Conflict occurs when:

* load changes movement mechanics,
* resistance slows the movement excessively,
* technique becomes rigid,
* the athlete cannot relax,
* or velocity intent is no longer expressed.

Examples:

* heavily resisted punching,
* excessively loaded sprinting,
* slow jump squats presented as speed work,
* high-fatigue bag combinations labeled as speed training.

The engine must protect true velocity when speed is the objective.

---

# Rule 12 — Hypertrophy and Skill Conflict

Hypertrophy work may support combat performance, but excessive volume can conflict with:

* movement speed,
* technical practice,
* recovery,
* mobility,
* weight-class management,
* and combat-specific work.

Hypertrophy volume must be reduced when it creates:

* persistent soreness,
* joint irritation,
* excessive local fatigue,
* reduced technical quality,
* or unwanted body-mass gain.

Functional hypertrophy must remain subordinate to sport performance.

---

# Rule 13 — Strength and Skill Conflict

High-strength training volumes may reduce technical quality through:

* neural fatigue,
* local muscular fatigue,
* grip fatigue,
* postural fatigue,
* soreness,
* and reduced coordination.

Strength work should not compromise the athlete’s most important technical sessions.

When necessary, the engine should:

* reduce volume,
* reduce eccentric stress,
* change exercise selection,
* change training day,
* or maintain strength rather than develop it.

---

# Rule 14 — Robustness and Performance Conflict

Robustness work supports long-term performance but can become conflicting when:

* volume is excessive,
* tissue loading is too novel,
* it creates soreness before competition,
* it duplicates primary loading,
* or it becomes a second hidden strength session.

Robustness work should usually have:

* controlled fatigue,
* clear tissue targets,
* gradual progression,
* and limited interference.

---

# Rule 15 — Plyometric and Impact Conflict

Plyometric work creates impact and stretch-shortening-cycle stress.

It may conflict with:

* kicking volume,
* running volume,
* hard footwork,
* takedown practice,
* sparring,
* or lower-limb pain.

The system must monitor total impact exposure rather than only plyometric contacts.

High-impact plyometrics should be reduced when the weekly combat load already contains substantial impact.

---

# Rule 16 — Sprint and Lower-Body Conflict

Sprint work may conflict with:

* heavy squats,
* deadlifts,
* high-volume lunges,
* plyometrics,
* hard kicking,
* and repeated takedown practice.

The conflict increases when these occur:

* in the same session,
* on consecutive days,
* at high intensity,
* or during poor recovery.

When sprint quality is the priority, sprinting should generally occur before heavy strength or on a separate day.

---

# Rule 17 — Striking and Upper-Body Conflict

Striking quality may be reduced by:

* high pressing volume,
* shoulder fatigue,
* triceps fatigue,
* grip fatigue,
* chest soreness,
* and excessive trunk fatigue.

Pressing and striking may coexist, but the system should avoid:

* muscular failure before striking,
* high-volume pressing before technical sessions,
* maximal shoulder loading before high-speed punching,
* and excessive eccentric work close to hard bag or pad work.

---

# Rule 18 — Grappling and Pulling Conflict

Grappling relies heavily on:

* grip,
* pulling strength,
* elbow flexors,
* trunk control,
* neck strength,
* and isometric endurance.

High pulling volume may conflict with grappling when it creates:

* grip exhaustion,
* forearm soreness,
* elbow irritation,
* lat fatigue,
* or reduced pulling endurance.

The engine must consider grappling volume as part of total pulling exposure.

---

# Rule 19 — Kicking and Lower-Body Strength Conflict

Kicking quality may be reduced by:

* quadriceps soreness,
* hip-flexor fatigue,
* adductor fatigue,
* calf fatigue,
* reduced hip mobility,
* and heavy eccentric loading.

High-volume split squats, Nordic curls, deep squats or sprinting may require separation from kicking-intensive sessions.

The system should protect:

* speed,
* balance,
* hip control,
* and technical precision.

---

# Rule 20 — Neck Training Conflict

Neck training supports robustness but may conflict with:

* grappling,
* wrestling,
* clinch work,
* impact exposure,
* and contact sparring.

The system should avoid heavy neck fatigue before:

* live grappling,
* takedown practice,
* or contact sessions.

Neck work should not create reduced head-control capacity.

---

# Rule 21 — Same-Session Order Conflict

Exercise order creates conflict when fatigue reduces the quality of a higher-priority task.

Default order:

1. Movement Preparation
2. Technical Skill
3. Speed
4. Plyometrics
5. Ballistic Power
6. Maximum Strength
7. Secondary Strength
8. Hypertrophy
9. Robustness
10. Conditioning
11. Recovery

This order may change according to the primary objective.

The primary objective must receive the athlete’s highest-quality effort.

---

# Rule 22 — Technical Work Before Fatigue

Technical learning should generally occur before severe fatigue.

Fatigue may be used during technical work only when the explicit objective is:

* fatigue resistance,
* late-round execution,
* competition simulation,
* or controlled decision-making under fatigue.

The system must not confuse degraded technique with sport specificity.

---

# Rule 23 — Conditioning After Power

When power and conditioning are combined, power should generally occur first.

Conditioning may precede power only when:

* the explicit objective is power expression under fatigue,
* the athlete is advanced,
* technical risk is controlled,
* and the method is strategically planned.

This should not be the default.

---

# Rule 24 — Failure Training Conflict

Training to failure may conflict with:

* power,
* speed,
* technical practice,
* recovery,
* high-frequency training,
* and joint tolerance.

Failure may be used selectively in:

* low-risk hypertrophy work,
* stable exercises,
* isolated accessories,
* and phases with sufficient recovery.

Failure should generally be avoided in:

* maximal strength,
* plyometrics,
* ballistic exercises,
* sprinting,
* high-skill lifts,
* and combat-specific technical drills.

---

# Rule 25 — Redundancy Conflict

A session becomes conflicted when multiple exercises duplicate the same demand without sufficient added value.

Redundancy may involve:

* the same movement pattern,
* the same joint stress,
* the same muscle group,
* the same velocity profile,
* the same adaptation,
* and the same fatigue cost.

Examples:

* several heavy horizontal presses in one session,
* multiple high-fatigue hinge exercises,
* several maximal-impact jump variations,
* repeated bag-conditioning blocks with identical structure.

Redundant elements should be removed or differentiated.

---

# Rule 26 — Volume Conflict

Volume conflict occurs when total work exceeds the athlete’s capacity to adapt.

Signs include:

* declining performance,
* persistent soreness,
* disrupted sleep,
* reduced motivation,
* repeated pain,
* technical deterioration,
* and poor combat practice.

Volume must be evaluated across:

* the session,
* the week,
* the training phase,
* and combat practice.

The engine must not count only gym sets.

---

# Rule 27 — Intensity Conflict

Intensity conflict occurs when too many high-intensity elements are combined.

High-intensity elements may include:

* maximal strength,
* maximal sprinting,
* high-intensity intervals,
* heavy bag power,
* hard sparring,
* maximal jumps,
* and high-force grappling.

The number of high-intensity exposures must match:

* athlete level,
* recovery,
* competition phase,
* and weekly schedule.

---

# Rule 28 — Recovery Conflict

A recovery conflict exists when the planned demand exceeds current readiness.

Relevant indicators include:

* poor sleep,
* high stress,
* soreness,
* low energy,
* reduced motivation,
* elevated resting heart rate,
* pain,
* recent hard combat training,
* and incomplete recovery.

Possible resolutions include:

* volume reduction,
* intensity reduction,
* exercise substitution,
* module substitution,
* active recovery,
* or session cancellation.

---

# Rule 29 — Sleep Conflict

Poor sleep particularly affects:

* reaction time,
* coordination,
* maximal strength,
* power,
* technical learning,
* and injury risk.

When sleep is significantly reduced, the engine should penalize:

* maximal efforts,
* high-skill ballistic work,
* high-impact plyometrics,
* hard sparring preparation,
* and failure training.

Low-risk technical or aerobic work may remain appropriate.

---

# Rule 30 — Stress Conflict

High psychological stress reduces recovery capacity.

The system should consider stress when determining:

* session complexity,
* total volume,
* intensity,
* exercise novelty,
* and coaching demands.

A technically simple session may be preferable during periods of high stress.

Stress does not automatically require inactivity.

---

# Rule 31 — Time Conflict

A time conflict exists when the planned session cannot be completed without compromising:

* rest,
* exercise quality,
* safety,
* or the primary objective.

When time is reduced, the system should remove work in this order:

1. Optional accessories
2. Redundant exercises
3. Low-priority volume
4. Tertiary modules
5. Secondary modules
6. Never remove the primary objective first

Rest intervals should not be shortened when this would change the intended adaptation.

---

# Rule 32 — Equipment Conflict

Equipment conflict occurs when the planned method requires:

* unavailable equipment,
* unsafe setup,
* insufficient load,
* excessive transition time,
* or equipment incompatible with the environment.

The engine should apply substitution rules rather than preserve the exact exercise at all costs.

Unsafe improvisation is prohibited.

---

# Rule 33 — Environmental Conflict

Environmental conflicts include:

* unsafe floor,
* insufficient space,
* low ceiling,
* poor lighting,
* bad weather,
* excessive heat,
* excessive cold,
* crowding,
* and noise restrictions.

Examples:

* no sprinting in unsafe space,
* no maximal jumping on slippery surfaces,
* no medicine ball throws near others,
* no high-intensity outdoor work in dangerous heat.

Environmental safety overrides session continuity.

---

# Rule 34 — Technical-Level Conflict

A conflict exists when the planned exercise exceeds the athlete’s current technical level.

Possible resolutions include:

* regression,
* slower tempo,
* reduced load,
* reduced range,
* more support,
* simplified variation,
* or supervised practice.

The system should not select advanced exercises merely because the athlete is advanced in combat.

Combat expertise does not guarantee strength-training expertise.

---

# Rule 35 — Novelty Conflict

New exercises increase:

* coordination demand,
* soreness,
* uncertainty,
* and technical error risk.

Novelty may conflict with:

* competition proximity,
* high fatigue,
* unsupervised training,
* return from injury,
* and dense combat schedules.

New exercises should be introduced when the athlete has sufficient capacity to learn and recover.

---

# Rule 36 — Progression Conflict

A planned progression becomes conflicted when:

* previous technique was unstable,
* target repetitions were not achieved,
* pain appeared,
* recovery was poor,
* the increase is excessive,
* or another variable was already progressed.

The system should not progress load, volume, complexity and density simultaneously by default.

One primary progression variable should usually be changed at a time.

---

# Rule 37 — Exercise Continuity Conflict

Exercise continuity is valuable, but it may conflict with:

* repeated pain,
* plateau,
* excessive local stress,
* poor adherence,
* or phase change.

The engine should preserve effective exercises but must not maintain them dogmatically.

Continuity is a benefit only while adaptation remains positive.

---

# Rule 38 — Variation Conflict

Variation may improve motivation or reduce repetitive stress, but excessive variation conflicts with:

* skill learning,
* measurement,
* progression,
* tissue adaptation,
* and program clarity.

The system should not replace exercises only for novelty.

Variation must solve a problem or support a phase objective.

---

# Rule 39 — Weight-Class Conflict

For athletes competing in weight classes, some training goals may conflict with body-mass targets.

Examples:

* excessive hypertrophy,
* large calorie surplus,
* unnecessary muscle gain,
* excessive glycogen depletion near weigh-in,
* and high-sodium or dehydration strategies outside professional supervision.

The engine must distinguish between:

* useful functional mass,
* unnecessary mass,
* short-term weight manipulation,
* and performance-supporting nutrition.

CAS must not prescribe dangerous weight-cutting practices.

---

# Rule 40 — Body Composition and Performance Conflict

Aggressive fat loss may conflict with:

* strength,
* power,
* recovery,
* mood,
* sleep,
* immunity,
* and combat performance.

When performance is the priority, the system should avoid excessive energy deficits.

Body-composition goals must not override health or competition readiness.

---

# Rule 41 — Conditioning Specificity Conflict

A conditioning method may produce the correct heart-rate response but still conflict with the target due to:

* local muscular limitation,
* excessive impact,
* technical breakdown,
* inappropriate movement specificity,
* or high recovery cost.

Examples:

* burpees replacing every conditioning modality,
* running used despite lower-limb pain,
* heavy bag intervals used when striking technique collapses,
* rowing used when grip fatigue limits output before cardiovascular stress.

The system must select the modality that best expresses the intended physiological demand.

---

# Rule 42 — Heavy Bag Conflict

Heavy bag work may simultaneously create:

* technical demand,
* impact stress,
* shoulder fatigue,
* lower-body fatigue,
* trunk fatigue,
* and conditioning stress.

The engine must define the primary purpose of each bag block.

A single bag round should not be treated as:

* maximal speed work,
* maximal power work,
* technical learning,
* and conditioning work at the same time.

The dominant objective must be explicit.

---

# Rule 43 — Bag Speed and Fatigue Conflict

Bag speed work conflicts with:

* heavy fatigue,
* excessive combination length,
* high impact intent,
* muscular tension,
* and insufficient rest.

Speed rounds should stop when:

* velocity clearly decreases,
* relaxation disappears,
* technique becomes rigid,
* or balance deteriorates.

Speed work must remain fast.

---

# Rule 44 — Bag Power and Density Conflict

Bag power work requires:

* maximal intent,
* balance recovery,
* technical control,
* and sufficient rest.

Power conflicts with excessive strike density.

A high number of strikes per minute may transform power work into conditioning.

The engine must distinguish between:

* impact quality,
* strike frequency,
* and total work.

---

# Rule 45 — Bag Technique and Exhaustion Conflict

Technical bag work should prioritize:

* precision,
* position,
* balance,
* timing,
* distance,
* and movement quality.

Exhaustion that repeatedly destroys technique conflicts with technical learning.

Fatigue may be introduced gradually only when technique is stable.

---

# Rule 46 — Module Conflict

Capability Modules may conflict when their demands overlap excessively.

Common examples:

* Maximum Strength + High-Volume Hypertrophy
* Power + Exhaustive Conditioning
* Plyometrics + High-Impact Combat Practice
* Sprinting + Heavy Lower-Body Volume
* Upper-Body Strength + High-Volume Striking
* Grip Strength + Hard Grappling
* Robustness + High Local Fatigue
* Recovery + High-Intensity Intervals

Modules may coexist if volume and order remain controlled.

---

# Rule 47 — Compatible Module Pairings

Common compatible pairings include:

* Power + Maximum Strength
* Strength + Low-Volume Robustness
* Technique + Aerobic Base
* Movement + Recovery
* Hypertrophy + Low-Intensity Conditioning
* Striking Power + Rotational Power
* Sprinting + Low-Volume Lower-Body Strength
* Grappling Strength + Trunk Robustness

Compatibility is conditional.

It depends on athlete readiness and weekly context.

---

# Rule 48 — High-Risk Module Pairings

Pairings requiring caution include:

* High-Impact Plyometrics + Hard Sparring
* Heavy Lower-Body Eccentrics + Kicking
* High-Volume Pulling + Grappling
* Heavy Pressing + High-Speed Striking
* Sprinting + High-Volume Squatting
* Maximal Strength + Severe Caloric Deficit
* Hard Conditioning + Technical Learning
* Power + Muscular Failure
* Neck Fatigue + Live Grappling

These pairings are not always prohibited but require strong justification.

---

# Rule 49 — Same-Muscle Conflict

Two exercises may conflict when they overload the same tissues beyond useful volume.

The engine must identify overlapping stress on:

* muscles,
* tendons,
* joints,
* connective tissue,
* and movement patterns.

Examples:

* Nordic curls plus high-volume sprinting,
* heavy dips plus hard punching,
* repeated calf plyometrics plus kicking,
* multiple grip-intensive pulling exercises before grappling.

---

# Rule 50 — Same-Joint Conflict

Joint stress must be tracked across different exercises.

Examples:

* pressing, dips and punching all stress the shoulder differently,
* squats, lunges and kicking all stress the knee,
* pulling, curls and grappling all stress the elbow,
* running, jumping and kicking all stress the ankle.

Different exercises do not guarantee different joint stress.

---

# Rule 51 — Consecutive-Day Conflict

High-demand training on consecutive days may create conflict.

The system should monitor:

* same muscle groups,
* same joints,
* same energy systems,
* same impact demands,
* and same technical demands.

Examples:

* hard lower-body strength followed by sprinting,
* heavy pulling followed by grappling,
* hard bag work followed by high-volume pressing,
* plyometrics followed by kicking.

Consecutive-day training is possible when at least one session has controlled volume or intensity.

---

# Rule 52 — High-Intensity Day Conflict

The system should limit the number of high-intensity days according to:

* athlete level,
* sport demands,
* recovery,
* age,
* injury history,
* work stress,
* and competition phase.

A high-intensity day may include:

* hard sparring,
* maximal strength,
* sprinting,
* high-intensity intervals,
* heavy bag power,
* or high-impact plyometrics.

Multiple high-intensity elements on one day may be preferable to spreading fatigue across every day, but only when total demand remains tolerable.

---

# Rule 53 — Easy-Day Contamination

An easy day becomes conflicted when too much additional work is added.

Examples:

* recovery run plus strength accessories plus bag intervals,
* mobility day plus hard circuits,
* technique day plus failure training,
* aerobic recovery plus sprint finishers.

An easy day must remain easy enough to support recovery.

---

# Rule 54 — Recovery-Day Integrity

Recovery sessions should prioritize:

* circulation,
* movement quality,
* low joint stress,
* low fatigue,
* breathing control,
* and psychological restoration.

Recovery work must not become a hidden conditioning test.

The session should finish with the athlete feeling equal or better than at the start.

---

# Rule 55 — Weekly Priority Conflict

A microcycle may contain more valid objectives than available recovery capacity.

The engine must rank weekly priorities.

Example:

```text
Priority 1:
Combat Skill

Priority 2:
Lower-Body Power

Priority 3:
Maximum Strength Maintenance

Priority 4:
Aerobic Development

Priority 5:
Hypertrophy Accessories
```

When recovery is limited, lower priorities are reduced first.

---

# Rule 56 — Mesocycle Priority Conflict

Not every quality should be developed maximally in every phase.

A mesocycle should define:

* development qualities,
* maintenance qualities,
* exposure qualities,
* and temporarily reduced qualities.

Example:

```text
Development:
Explosive Power

Maintenance:
Maximum Strength

Exposure:
Aerobic Base

Reduced:
Hypertrophy Volume
```

The engine must prevent all qualities from receiving development-level volume simultaneously.

---

# Rule 57 — Development and Maintenance Conflict

Development requires more training resources than maintenance.

When one quality is prioritized for development, other qualities may use reduced maintenance doses.

The engine must not allocate full development volume to every quality.

This prevents:

* excessive fatigue,
* unclear adaptation,
* and chronic stagnation.

---

# Rule 58 — Short-Term and Long-Term Conflict

Short-term performance goals may conflict with long-term development.

Examples:

* maintaining freshness versus building strength,
* reducing volume versus developing work capacity,
* avoiding novelty versus learning new exercises,
* competition taper versus hypertrophy progression.

The system must identify whether the current phase prioritizes:

* immediate performance,
* long-term adaptation,
* rehabilitation,
* or transition.

---

# Rule 59 — Athlete Preference Conflict

Athlete preference supports adherence but may conflict with:

* safety,
* adaptation needs,
* recovery,
* or program balance.

Preference may influence selection when several options are valid.

Preference must not override:

* pain,
* contraindications,
* unsafe technique,
* or critical weaknesses.

---

# Rule 60 — Motivation and Discipline Conflict

Low motivation should not automatically produce high-intensity novelty.

The system should distinguish between:

* temporary reluctance,
* boredom,
* psychological fatigue,
* poor recovery,
* and true exercise incompatibility.

Sometimes the correct solution is:

* a simpler session,
* reduced volume,
* a familiar exercise,
* or recovery.

More stimulation is not always the answer.

---

# Rule 61 — Data Conflict

Athlete data may conflict.

Examples:

* athlete reports feeling good but performance is declining,
* low soreness but poor coordination,
* strong motivation but severe sleep deprivation,
* high readiness score but significant pain,
* wearable data contradict subjective feedback.

When data conflict, the system should prioritize:

1. safety data,
2. pain and neurological symptoms,
3. objective performance deterioration,
4. recent training load,
5. subjective readiness,
6. wearable estimates.

No single metric should control the decision automatically.

---

# Rule 62 — Missing Data Conflict

Missing information creates uncertainty.

Critical missing data may include:

* pain status,
* recent combat load,
* equipment,
* session duration,
* competition date,
* injury restrictions,
* and technical level.

When important data are missing, the engine should:

* reduce confidence,
* avoid high-risk options,
* select conservative variations,
* and limit complexity.

Uncertainty should not be treated as ideal readiness.

---

# Rule 63 — Score Conflict

The Scoring Model may rank an option highly while a hard rule rejects it.

Hard rules always override scores.

Examples:

```text
Exercise Score = 91
Pain Compatibility = 1
Result = Excluded
```

A high average score cannot compensate for a mandatory failure.

---

# Rule 64 — Score Tie Conflict

When two candidates have similar scores, the engine should use the following tie-breakers:

1. Higher safety
2. Better primary adaptation match
3. Better athlete compatibility
4. Lower interference risk
5. Lower fatigue cost
6. Better previous athlete response
7. Greater continuity
8. Better athlete preference
9. Lower setup burden

A difference below 3 points may be treated as a practical tie.

---

# Rule 65 — Rule Conflict

Two system rules may occasionally point toward different decisions.

Example:

* continuity favors keeping an exercise,
* pain history favors replacing it.

When rules conflict, apply this priority:

```text
Safety
↓
Medical Constraints
↓
Primary Adaptation
↓
Athlete Compatibility
↓
Recovery
↓
Continuity
↓
Preference
```

The higher-level rule wins.

---

# Rule 66 — Substitution Conflict

A substitute may preserve one feature while losing another.

Example:

* lat pulldown preserves vertical pulling,
* but loses bodyweight control compared with pull-ups.

The engine must identify:

* what is preserved,
* what is reduced,
* and whether the loss is acceptable.

A substitute must not be presented as perfectly equivalent when it is not.

---

# Rule 67 — Conflict Severity Levels

Conflicts are classified as:

## Level 0 — No Meaningful Conflict

Elements are compatible.

No action required.

## Level 1 — Minor Conflict

Small interference or redundancy.

Possible response:

* monitor,
* reduce minor volume,
* adjust order.

## Level 2 — Moderate Conflict

Likely reduction in performance or recovery.

Possible response:

* modify load,
* reduce sets,
* replace one exercise,
* separate modules.

## Level 3 — Major Conflict

High probability of poor adaptation, technical breakdown or excessive fatigue.

Possible response:

* remove a module,
* change session objective,
* move the session,
* replace the session.

## Level 4 — Critical Conflict

Immediate safety, medical or severe recovery issue.

Required response:

* stop,
* exclude,
* cancel,
* or refer for appropriate assessment.

---

# Rule 68 — Conflict Probability

Conflict severity and conflict probability are separate.

A conflict may be:

* low probability, high consequence,
* high probability, low consequence,
* high probability, high consequence,
* or low probability, low consequence.

The engine should prioritize conflicts with:

* high consequence,
* high probability,
* or poor detectability.

---

# Rule 69 — Conflict Score

The engine may calculate an internal Conflict Score.

Example:

```text
Conflict Score =
Severity × Probability × Exposure
```

Where each factor is scored from 1 to 5.

Interpretation:

| Score  | Interpretation |
| ------ | -------------- |
| 1–10   | Low            |
| 11–25  | Moderate       |
| 26–50  | High           |
| 51–125 | Critical       |

This score supports classification.

It does not replace hard safety rules.

---

# Rule 70 — Interaction Cost

The combined cost of two elements may be greater than the sum of each element individually.

Example:

```text
Heavy Squat Cost = 30
Sprint Cost = 25
Simple Total = 55

Interaction Cost = +15

Combined Cost = 70
```

Interaction cost may result from:

* shared tissues,
* similar neural demand,
* impact,
* soreness,
* technical fatigue,
* and insufficient recovery.

---

# Rule 71 — Conflict Penalties

Within the Scoring Model, compatible options may receive conflict penalties.

Examples:

| Conflict                               | Suggested Penalty |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------: |
| Minor redundancy                       |                -2 |
| Moderate local fatigue overlap         |                -5 |
| High interference with combat practice |         -8 to -15 |
| Competition proximity conflict         |         -5 to -20 |
| Same-joint overload                    |         -5 to -15 |
| Poor session-order compatibility       |         -3 to -10 |
| High novelty during fatigue            |          -3 to -8 |

Critical conflicts require exclusion, not penalties.

---

# Rule 72 — Conflict Resolution Methods

The engine may resolve conflicts through:

* removal,
* substitution,
* regression,
* volume reduction,
* intensity reduction,
* frequency reduction,
* exercise-order change,
* day change,
* module separation,
* session replacement,
* or adaptation-priority change.

The smallest effective resolution should be preferred.

---

# Rule 73 — Resolution by Removal

Removal is appropriate when an element is:

* optional,
* redundant,
* low priority,
* high fatigue,
* poorly tolerated,
* or incompatible with competition readiness.

The engine should remove the lowest-value element first.

---

# Rule 74 — Resolution by Substitution

Substitution is appropriate when the original objective remains valid but the implementation creates conflict.

Examples:

* running → cycling to reduce impact,
* heavy squat → belt squat to reduce spinal fatigue,
* bag power → medicine ball throw when the shoulder is irritated,
* weighted pull-up → supported pulldown before grappling.

Substitution must follow `15_SUBSTITUTION_RULES`.

---

# Rule 75 — Resolution by Volume Reduction

Volume reduction is appropriate when:

* adaptation remains valid,
* exercise selection remains appropriate,
* but total workload is excessive.

Possible reductions include:

* fewer sets,
* fewer repetitions,
* shorter duration,
* fewer rounds,
* fewer contacts,
* or lower weekly frequency.

The primary high-quality work should be preserved first.

---

# Rule 76 — Resolution by Intensity Reduction

Intensity reduction is appropriate when:

* readiness is reduced,
* technique deteriorates,
* competition is near,
* or maximal demand is unnecessary.

Possible methods include:

* lower load,
* lower velocity demand,
* lower impact,
* reduced RPE,
* increased RIR,
* lower heart-rate zone,
* or lower bag-power intent.

Intensity reduction must not silently change the adaptation beyond recognition.

---

# Rule 77 — Resolution by Order Change

Order change is appropriate when two elements are compatible but one requires greater quality.

Examples:

* speed before strength,
* power before conditioning,
* technique before fatigue,
* sprinting before hypertrophy,
* bag power before bag conditioning.

Order change cannot fully solve excessive total fatigue.

---

# Rule 78 — Resolution by Separation

Separation is appropriate when two valuable elements conflict in the same session.

Possible separation:

* different parts of the day,
* different days,
* at least 6 hours apart,
* or separate microcycle positions.

Separation is especially useful for:

* strength and endurance,
* sprinting and heavy lower-body work,
* combat technique and conditioning,
* power and hypertrophy.

Separation does not remove total weekly fatigue.

---

# Rule 79 — Resolution by Session Replacement

A complete session should be replaced when:

* readiness is very low,
* illness is present,
* pain creates multiple restrictions,
* competition demands freshness,
* or the primary objective is no longer appropriate.

Possible replacements include:

* high-intensity → aerobic recovery,
* maximum strength → submaximal technique strength,
* power → movement quality,
* bag power → technical shadowboxing,
* sprinting → low-impact conditioning,
* full training → recovery.

---

# Rule 80 — Resolution by Priority Change

Sometimes the correct solution is to change the training priority.

This may occur when:

* competition schedule changes,
* injury occurs,
* a major weakness is resolved,
* a new limitation appears,
* or sport practice increases.

Priority change must be explicit and recorded.

---

# Rule 81 — Resolution Must Be Recalculated

After resolving a conflict, the engine must reassess:

* session duration,
* fatigue,
* exercise order,
* adaptation coherence,
* weekly balance,
* and recovery.

Changing one element may create a new conflict elsewhere.

---

# Rule 82 — Conflict Memory

The system should record:

* conflict type,
* date,
* planned elements,
* athlete state,
* severity,
* resolution,
* athlete response,
* and whether the same conflict recurred.

Conflict history should improve future planning.

---

# Rule 83 — Recurrent Conflict

A recurrent conflict indicates a structural programming problem.

Examples:

* repeated shoulder fatigue before striking,
* repeated leg soreness before kicking,
* repeated grip fatigue before grappling,
* repeated low readiness on sprint day,
* repeated session overruns.

The engine should not solve the same conflict indefinitely through temporary substitutions.

It should change the weekly structure.

---

# Rule 84 — Athlete Feedback

Conflict detection must use athlete feedback.

Relevant feedback includes:

* pain,
* soreness,
* fatigue,
* session quality,
* technical quality,
* perceived interference,
* motivation,
* recovery,
* and combat performance.

The engine should distinguish between:

* isolated feedback,
* repeated patterns,
* and objective performance decline.

---

# Rule 85 — Performance Feedback

Performance deterioration may reveal conflict.

Indicators include:

* reduced load,
* reduced velocity,
* lower jump height,
* slower sprint time,
* lower strike quality,
* reduced work rate,
* increased RPE,
* and technical breakdown.

Repeated deterioration should trigger review of:

* volume,
* order,
* recovery,
* and weekly placement.

---

# Rule 86 — Recovery Feedback

Recovery indicators include:

* next-day soreness,
* sleep quality,
* resting fatigue,
* joint irritation,
* motivation,
* and readiness for combat practice.

A session that repeatedly harms following sessions has poor global value even if the session itself appears successful.

---

# Rule 87 — Conflict Explanation

When a significant conflict changes the plan, the system should explain:

```text
Conflict Detected:
Why It Matters:
Protected Priority:
Modified Element:
New Plan:
Expected Trade-Off:
```

Example:

```text
Conflict Detected:
Heavy lower-body hypertrophy before kick training.

Why It Matters:
High quadriceps and adductor fatigue may reduce kicking speed and control.

Protected Priority:
Technical kick session.

Modified Element:
Lower-body hypertrophy volume reduced.

New Plan:
Two strength sets instead of four, no failure work.

Expected Trade-Off:
Lower hypertrophy stimulus, better technical readiness.
```

---

# Rule 88 — No Hidden Objective Change

When conflict resolution changes the adaptation, the system must state it.

Example:

```text
Original Objective:
Maximum Strength

Adjusted Objective:
Submaximal Strength Maintenance
```

The engine must not present the adjusted session as equivalent when the physiological objective changed.

---

# Rule 89 — Manual Override

A qualified coach may override a conflict resolution when:

* strategic peaking requires it,
* sport practice demands controlled fatigue exposure,
* rehabilitation staff provide specific instructions,
* or direct observation reveals context not represented in the data.

The override must record:

* original conflict,
* chosen action,
* rationale,
* expected risk,
* and review date.

Manual override cannot bypass critical safety rules.

---

# Rule 90 — Conservative Resolution Under Uncertainty

When conflict severity is uncertain, the engine should prefer the option with:

* lower risk,
* lower fatigue,
* simpler execution,
* better recovery compatibility,
* and clearer monitoring.

Uncertainty should not justify aggressive programming.

---

# Default Weekly Conflict Checks

Before validating the microcycle, the engine should verify:

* no excessive consecutive high-intensity days,
* no heavy leg fatigue before key kicking sessions,
* no heavy grip fatigue before key grappling sessions,
* no shoulder failure work before key striking sessions,
* no major plyometric load near high-impact combat work,
* no exhaustive conditioning before technical learning,
* no unnecessary novelty near competition,
* sufficient low-intensity recovery,
* no repeated joint stress without recovery,
* and no more development priorities than the athlete can support.

---

# Default Session Conflict Checks

Before validating a session, the engine should verify:

* one clear primary objective,
* logical exercise order,
* no unresolved safety issue,
* no excessive redundancy,
* no incompatible velocity demands,
* no technical work after unnecessary exhaustion,
* no excessive same-joint stress,
* no fatigue budget overflow,
* realistic duration,
* and compatibility with the next important session.

---

# Conflict Decision Tree

```text
Planned Training Elements
          ↓
Are All Elements Individually Eligible?
      ↓ Yes                ↓ No
Check Interactions         Exclude Invalid Element
      ↓
Does a Conflict Exist?
      ↓ No                 ↓ Yes
Validate Plan              Classify Severity
                                 ↓
                      Is Safety or Medical Risk Present?
                          ↓ Yes             ↓ No
                      Stop or Exclude       Identify Protected Priority
                                                   ↓
                                         Can Minimal Change Solve It?
                                            ↓ Yes          ↓ No
                                      Modify Element     Separate, Replace
                                            ↓             or Change Priority
                                             \             /
                                              Recalculate Plan
                                                     ↓
                                               Validate Again
```

# Conflict Resolution Summary

The default resolution order is:

```text
Protect Safety
↓
Protect Competition and Combat Practice
↓
Protect the Primary Adaptation
↓
Remove Redundancy
↓
Reduce Low-Priority Volume
↓
Substitute High-Cost Elements
↓
Separate Conflicting Modules
↓
Replace the Session if Necessary
```

---

# Final Principle

The Combat Athlete System does not ask:

> Can these training elements be combined?

It asks:

> Can these elements be combined without compromising the athlete’s highest-priority adaptation, technical performance, safety and recovery?

A complete program is not the sum of individually good exercises.

It is a coordinated system of compatible demands.

When objectives conflict, CAS protects what matters most.
