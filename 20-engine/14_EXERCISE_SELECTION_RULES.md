# EXERCISE SELECTION RULES

Version 0.1

---

# Purpose

The Exercise Selection Rules define how the Combat Athlete System selects the most appropriate exercises for a training session.

Exercise selection is never random.

An exercise is selected only when it serves a clearly identified adaptation, respects the athlete’s constraints and fits the structure of the session.

The system never selects exercises because they are popular, impressive or traditionally associated with a sport.

It selects them because they are appropriate for the desired adaptation.

The fundamental rule is:

> Select the exercise that delivers the required adaptation with the lowest unnecessary cost.

The lowest unnecessary cost means minimizing:

* injury risk,
* excessive fatigue,
* technical complexity,
* recovery disruption,
* equipment constraints,
* interference with combat practice,
* and movements that do not directly support the session objective.

---

# Core Principle

Exercise selection follows this hierarchy:

1. Desired adaptation
2. Athlete profile
3. Movement function
4. Training phase
5. Fatigue and recovery status
6. Equipment availability
7. Exercise preference

Exercise preference may influence the final choice, but it never overrides safety, adaptation quality or program coherence.

---

# Selection Pipeline

Every exercise must pass through the following selection pipeline:

1. Identify the target adaptation.
2. Identify the required movement pattern.
3. Identify the required contraction or expression type.
4. Check the athlete’s readiness and constraints.
5. Generate a list of valid exercise candidates.
6. Exclude contraindicated or incompatible exercises.
7. Rank the remaining exercises.
8. Select the exercise with the highest adaptation-to-cost ratio.
9. Define the appropriate variation.
10. Assign volume, intensity, tempo, rest and execution intent.

An exercise is valid only when all ten steps are coherent.

---

# Rule 1 — Adaptation Comes First

Every selected exercise must be linked to a specific adaptation.

Examples of adaptations include:

* maximum force production,
* rate of force development,
* explosive power,
* muscular hypertrophy,
* local muscular endurance,
* repeated high-intensity effort capacity,
* aerobic power,
* tissue robustness,
* joint stability,
* mobility,
* motor control,
* striking speed,
* striking power,
* defensive movement efficiency.

An exercise must never be included solely because it is considered a “good exercise”.

There are no universally good exercises.

There are only exercises that are more or less appropriate for a specific athlete, objective and context.

---

# Rule 2 — Exercises Must Match the Capability Module

Each exercise must belong to the Capability Module being trained.

Examples:

* Maximum Strength modules require exercises that permit high force production and measurable loading.
* Power modules require exercises that can be executed with maximum intent and limited velocity loss.
* Hypertrophy modules require exercises that generate sufficient mechanical tension through a stable range of motion.
* Conditioning modules require exercises that produce the intended metabolic response without excessive technical breakdown.
* Robustness modules require controlled loading of tissues, joints and vulnerable positions.
* Movement modules require precision, coordination and quality rather than fatigue.
* Specific Skill modules require direct transfer to combat tasks.

Exercises from different modules may appear in the same session, but each exercise must have a defined function.

# Rule 4 — Exercise Categories

Exercises are classified into the following categories:

## Primary Strength Exercises

Exercises that allow high force production and progressive loading.

Examples:

* Squat variations
* Deadlift variations
* Bench Press variations
* Overhead Press variations
* Weighted Pull-Up variations
* Weighted Dip variations
* Row variations
* Split Squat variations

## Secondary Strength Exercises

Exercises used to develop force in more specific positions or movement patterns.

Examples:

* Romanian Deadlift
* Bulgarian Split Squat
* Single-Leg Squat
* Landmine Press
* Chest-Supported Row
* Hip Thrust
* Nordic Hamstring Curl

## Ballistic Exercises

Exercises in which the athlete accelerates through the entire movement.

Examples:

* Medicine Ball Throw
* Medicine Ball Slam
* Jump Squat
* Kettlebell Swing
* Olympic Lift derivative
* Plyometric Push-Up

## Plyometric Exercises

Exercises using rapid stretch-shortening cycle actions.

Examples:

* Pogos
* Hurdle Jumps
* Depth Jumps
* Bounds
* Lateral Hops
* Reactive Push-Ups

## Isolation and Local Development Exercises

Exercises targeting a muscle group or local tissue with limited systemic fatigue.

Examples:

* Lateral Raise
* Hamstring Curl
* Triceps Extension
* Biceps Curl
* Calf Raise
* Neck Flexion
* Neck Extension

## Robustness Exercises

Exercises selected primarily to improve tissue tolerance and joint resilience.

Examples:

* Copenhagen Plank
* Tibialis Raise
* External Rotation
* Soleus Raise
* Isometric Split Squat
* Adductor Strengthening
* Neck Isometrics
* Wrist Strengthening

## Movement and Control Exercises

Exercises focused on coordination, control, mobility and positional quality.

Examples:

* Controlled Articular Rotations
* Hip Airplane
* Scapular Pull-Up
* Deep Squat Hold
* Crawling variations
* Balance drills
* Landing mechanics drills

## Conditioning Exercises

Exercises used to create a targeted cardiovascular or metabolic response.

Examples:

* Running
* Cycling
* Rowing
* Ski Erg
* Jump Rope
* Sled Push
* Heavy Bag Work
* Shadowboxing
* Combat Circuits

## Combat-Specific Exercises

Exercises directly reproducing or constraining combat actions.

Examples:

* Heavy Bag Punching
* Heavy Bag Kicking
* Pad Work
* Shadowboxing
* Sprawl Drills
* Shot Entries
* Clinch Isometrics
* Ground-and-Pound Intervals
* Technical Combination Rounds

# Rule 5 — Foundational Exercises

Certain exercises are considered foundational because they offer high training value, clear progression and broad transfer.

Foundational exercises include:

* Squat
* Deadlift
* Romanian Deadlift
* Split Squat
* Bench Press
* Overhead Press
* Pull-Up
* Row
* Dip
* Push-Up
* Hip Thrust
* Loaded Carry
* Sprint
* Jump
* Medicine Ball Throw
* Heavy Bag Striking

Foundational does not mean mandatory.

A foundational exercise may be replaced when:

* it causes pain,
* it is incompatible with the athlete’s morphology,
* the athlete lacks the required technical competency,
* equipment is unavailable,
* the fatigue cost is excessive,
* another variation better serves the current objective,
* or it interferes with combat practice.

---

# Rule 6 — Exercise Variations

The system does not select only an exercise name.

It selects an exercise variation.

A variation may be modified by:

* stance,
* grip,
* range of motion,
* loading position,
* equipment,
* stability demand,
* unilateral or bilateral execution,
* tempo,
* pause position,
* movement speed,
* support,
* direction,
* technical complexity.

Examples:

* Back Squat
* Front Squat
* Safety Bar Squat
* Goblet Squat
* Box Squat
* Split Squat
* Rear-Foot-Elevated Split Squat

These are not interchangeable by default.

Each variation produces a different balance of:

* force potential,
* mobility demand,
* stability demand,
* technical demand,
* muscular emphasis,
* spinal loading,
* joint stress,
* and fatigue.

---

# Rule 7 — Athlete Compatibility

An exercise must be compatible with the athlete.

Compatibility is assessed through:

* training age,
* technical level,
* injury history,
* pain status,
* mobility,
* joint tolerance,
* coordination,
* morphology,
* current fatigue,
* confidence,
* available equipment,
* and training environment.

The system must never select an exercise solely because it is theoretically optimal.

A theoretically optimal exercise that the athlete cannot execute safely is not optimal.

---

# Rule 8 — Pain and Contraindications

Pain overrides exercise preference.

The system must distinguish between:

* normal muscular effort,
* normal fatigue,
* mild unfamiliar discomfort,
* joint pain,
* sharp pain,
* neurological symptoms,
* loss of function,
* and worsening symptoms.

An exercise must be stopped, modified or replaced when it produces:

* sharp pain,
* joint instability,
* radiating pain,
* numbness,
* loss of strength,
* altered movement caused by pain,
* or increasing symptoms across sets.

The system does not diagnose injuries.

When significant pain or neurological symptoms are reported, the athlete must be advised to seek assessment from an appropriate health professional.

---

# Rule 9 — Exercise Substitution

Every exercise must have valid substitutions.

Substitution must preserve the purpose of the original exercise.

Substitutions are ranked by similarity in:

1. adaptation,
2. movement pattern,
3. force direction,
4. contraction type,
5. range of motion,
6. loading potential,
7. velocity,
8. stability demand,
9. fatigue cost,
10. equipment requirement.

Example:

If Weighted Pull-Ups are unavailable, possible substitutions may include:

* Bodyweight Pull-Ups
* Band-Assisted Pull-Ups
* Lat Pulldown
* Chest-Supported Pulldown
* Inverted Row

However, the correct substitution depends on the original objective.

A Lat Pulldown may replace a Pull-Up for hypertrophy more easily than for relative-strength assessment.

---

# Rule 10 — Strength Exercise Selection

Maximum strength exercises should:

* allow progressive loading,
* permit stable execution,
* provide consistent technique,
* allow accurate load tracking,
* produce high force,
* and avoid unnecessary failure.

Strength exercises are prioritized according to:

1. safety,
2. loadability,
3. technical consistency,
4. adaptation relevance,
5. recovery cost.

For strength development, the system should generally prefer stable exercises over unstable exercises.

Instability may be useful for motor control or rehabilitation, but it usually reduces maximum force production.

---

# Rule 11 — Power Exercise Selection

Power exercises must allow maximal acceleration.

They should:

* be performed with explosive intent,
* have limited technical complexity relative to the athlete,
* maintain high movement quality,
* stop before significant velocity loss,
* and minimize excessive fatigue.

Power exercises are selected according to the required force-velocity profile.

## High-Force Power

Examples:

* Loaded Jump Squat
* Trap Bar Jump
* Heavy Medicine Ball Throw
* Olympic Lift derivative

## Moderate-Load Power

Examples:

* Medicine Ball Rotational Throw
* Kettlebell Swing
* Push Press
* Light Jump Squat

## High-Velocity Power

Examples:

* Unloaded Jump
* Plyometric Push-Up
* Light Medicine Ball Throw
* Band-Assisted Jump

Power exercises must not be performed under severe fatigue unless fatigue resistance is the explicit objective.

---

# Rule 12 — Hypertrophy Exercise Selection

Hypertrophy exercises should:

* provide high mechanical tension,
* maintain tension through a useful range of motion,
* allow stable execution,
* minimize unnecessary joint stress,
* permit proximity to muscular failure,
* and allow progressive overload.

For functional hypertrophy, priority is given to muscle groups that support:

* force production,
* joint stability,
* posture,
* impact tolerance,
* grappling strength,
* sprinting,
* jumping,
* and strike transmission.

Hypertrophy exercise selection must not create excessive soreness that compromises technical combat training.

---

# Rule 13 — Conditioning Exercise Selection

Conditioning exercises are selected according to the targeted energy system and movement demands.

Possible modalities include:

* running,
* cycling,
* rowing,
* swimming,
* jump rope,
* sled work,
* shadowboxing,
* heavy bag work,
* pad work,
* grappling drills,
* and mixed circuits.

The modality must match the conditioning goal.

Examples:

* Long aerobic work should use low-complexity, repeatable modalities.
* Maximum aerobic power may use running, cycling or sport-specific intervals.
* Repeated-sprint ability should use short explosive efforts with controlled recovery.
* Combat-specific conditioning may use bag, pads or grappling intervals.
* Recovery conditioning should minimize eccentric stress and joint loading.

High-skill exercises should not be selected for conditioning when fatigue would create unsafe technique.

---

# Rule 14 — Heavy Bag Exercise Selection

Heavy bag work is treated as both a conditioning tool and a combat-specific power tool.

The system must define the purpose of each bag exercise.

Possible objectives include:

* punch speed,
* punch power,
* kick speed,
* kick power,
* combination efficiency,
* repeated striking capacity,
* technical precision,
* footwork,
* distance management,
* rotational endurance,
* and fight-specific conditioning.

Bag work must specify:

* strike type,
* target intensity,
* round duration,
* rest duration,
* technical focus,
* power intent,
* speed intent,
* combination structure,
* and stopping criteria.

Examples:

## Speed Bag Round

* Low external resistance
* Maximum hand or foot velocity
* Short combinations
* Full relaxation between strikes
* Stop when speed clearly decreases

## Power Bag Round

* Maximum intent on each strike
* Low repetition density
* Full balance recovery
* Longer rest between combinations
* Stop when impact quality decreases

## Conditioning Bag Round

* Sustained output
* Controlled technique
* Defined work-to-rest ratio
* No uncontrolled striking
* Intensity adjusted to the energy system objective

Power and speed bag work must be performed before high-fatigue conditioning whenever both appear in the same session.

---

# Rule 15 — Combat Transfer

Exercise selection must consider transfer, but visual similarity alone is insufficient.

An exercise may transfer to combat through:

* force production,
* rate of force development,
* elastic qualities,
* trunk stiffness,
* rotational power,
* unilateral stability,
* tissue resilience,
* repeated-effort capacity,
* or movement coordination.

The system must avoid exercises that imitate combat movements while using inappropriate resistance patterns.

For example, heavily resisted punching may alter striking mechanics and reduce velocity.

Combat transfer is evaluated through shared physical demands, not merely similar appearance.

---

# Rule 16 — Technical Complexity

Technical complexity must match the athlete’s skill level.

Exercises are classified as:

* Low complexity
* Moderate complexity
* High complexity

Low-complexity exercises are preferred when:

* the athlete is fatigued,
* the objective is conditioning,
* the athlete is a beginner,
* the environment is unsupervised,
* or the session requires high repetition.

High-complexity exercises may be used when:

* the athlete is technically competent,
* the exercise provides a clear benefit,
* fatigue is low,
* and the movement can be executed safely.

Complexity must never be added for entertainment.

---

# Rule 17 — Fatigue Cost

Each exercise has a local and systemic fatigue cost.

Fatigue cost is influenced by:

* load,
* muscle mass involved,
* axial loading,
* eccentric stress,
* range of motion,
* technical demand,
* proximity to failure,
* total volume,
* and exercise novelty.

Exercises with high fatigue cost include, depending on context:

* heavy deadlifts,
* high-volume squats,
* high-repetition eccentric work,
* depth jumps,
* sprinting,
* and repeated maximal bag rounds.

High-cost exercises must be used only when their adaptation value justifies the recovery demand.

---

# Rule 18 — Stimulus-to-Fatigue Ratio

The system should prioritize exercises with a high stimulus-to-fatigue ratio.

The ratio is evaluated relative to the athlete and objective.

A high stimulus-to-fatigue exercise:

* strongly stimulates the target adaptation,
* produces limited unnecessary fatigue,
* is technically repeatable,
* and does not disrupt subsequent training.

The same exercise may have a high ratio for one athlete and a low ratio for another.

---

# Rule 19 — Exercise Order

Exercises are generally ordered according to neural and technical demand.

Default order:

1. Movement preparation
2. Speed
3. Plyometrics
4. Ballistic power
5. Maximum strength
6. Secondary strength
7. Hypertrophy
8. Robustness
9. Conditioning
10. Recovery work

This order may be modified according to the session objective.

Examples:

* A conditioning-focused session may begin directly with conditioning after preparation.
* A technical bag session may place bag work before strength.
* A contrast session may alternate strength and explosive exercises.
* A rehabilitation session may begin with controlled activation work.

Exercises with the highest technical and velocity requirements should generally be performed before fatigue.

---

# Rule 20 — Interference Management

Exercise selection must consider the athlete’s total training week.

The system must avoid unnecessary interference between physical preparation and combat practice.

Examples:

* Heavy lower-body eccentric work should not be placed immediately before kicking-intensive training.
* High-volume pulling should not compromise grappling practice.
* Shoulder-intensive pressing should not impair striking sessions.
* High-impact plyometrics should not be added when sprinting and combat volume are already high.
* Exhaustive conditioning should not precede technical learning.

The exercise is selected not only for the session, but for its place within the entire microcycle.

---

# Rule 21 — Exercise Novelty

Novel exercises increase technical and muscular stress.

Novelty may be useful for:

* introducing a new progression,
* solving a specific limitation,
* maintaining engagement,
* or creating a new adaptation.

However, novelty must be controlled.

The system should not frequently replace exercises without reason.

Main exercises should remain stable long enough to:

* develop skill,
* measure progression,
* adapt tissues,
* and evaluate effectiveness.

Exercise rotation must serve a purpose.

---

# Rule 22 — Progression Before Variation

When an exercise is effective, safe and progressing, the system should generally progress the exercise before replacing it.

Progression may occur through:

* load,
* repetitions,
* sets,
* range of motion,
* tempo,
* pause duration,
* movement velocity,
* density,
* stability,
* technical quality,
* or reduced assistance.

Variation is selected only when progression is no longer appropriate or when the training phase changes.

---

# Rule 23 — Equipment Constraints

The system must generate effective exercise selections for different equipment levels.

## No Equipment

Examples:

* Push-Ups
* Pull-Ups if a bar is available
* Squats
* Split Squats
* Jumps
* Sprints
* Crawls
* Shadowboxing
* Isometrics

## Minimal Equipment

Examples:

* Resistance Bands
* Kettlebell
* Dumbbells
* Pull-Up Bar
* Medicine Ball
* Jump Rope
* Heavy Bag

## Full Gym

Examples:

* Barbell lifts
* Cable exercises
* Machines
* Sled work
* Ergometer conditioning
* Advanced power exercises

The absence of equipment must not prevent the system from training the intended adaptation.

---

# Rule 24 — Environment Constraints

Exercise selection must account for:

* available space,
* floor surface,
* ceiling height,
* noise restrictions,
* weather,
* training supervision,
* and proximity to other people.

Examples:

* Sprinting should not be selected without sufficient safe space.
* Medicine ball throws should not be selected without an appropriate wall or open area.
* Olympic lift derivatives should not be selected in an unsuitable training environment.
* Jumping volume should be reduced on hard or slippery surfaces.

---

# Rule 25 — Unsupervised Training

When the athlete trains without supervision, the system should prioritize:

* simple setup,
* clear technique,
* low failure risk,
* predictable loading,
* safe stopping,
* and minimal dependence on a spotter.

Exercises such as maximal barbell bench press without safeties should not be prescribed unsupervised.

The system should select safer alternatives when necessary.

---

# Rule 26 — Exercise Scoring

Candidate exercises may be scored using the following criteria:

* Adaptation Match
* Movement Match
* Athlete Compatibility
* Safety
* Loadability
* Progression Potential
* Technical Suitability
* Equipment Compatibility
* Fatigue Cost
* Combat Transfer
* Recovery Compatibility
* Athlete Preference

Each criterion may be rated from 0 to 5.

An example weighted score:

```text
Exercise Score =
Adaptation Match × 5
+ Athlete Compatibility × 5
+ Safety × 5
+ Movement Match × 4
+ Recovery Compatibility × 4
+ Progression Potential × 3
+ Fatigue Efficiency × 3
+ Combat Transfer × 2
+ Equipment Compatibility × 2
+ Athlete Preference × 1
```

Safety and compatibility are not merely scoring criteria.

An exercise may be excluded even when its total score is high if it fails a mandatory safety rule.

---

# Rule 27 — Hard Exclusion Rules

An exercise must be excluded when:

* it produces significant pain,
* it is medically contraindicated,
* the athlete cannot execute it safely,
* required equipment is unavailable,
* the environment is unsafe,
* fatigue prevents correct execution,
* it conflicts with the primary adaptation,
* it creates unacceptable interference,
* or it exceeds the athlete’s technical level.

Hard exclusions override all preferences and scores.

---

# Rule 28 — Soft Exclusion Rules

An exercise may be deprioritized when:

* the athlete dislikes it,
* a similar exercise is already heavily used,
* it produces excessive soreness,
* setup time is disproportionate,
* progression is difficult to measure,
* or another exercise provides similar benefits at lower cost.

Soft exclusions do not automatically prohibit the exercise.

---

# Rule 30 — Scientific Reliability

Exercise selection should be informed by:

* established strength and conditioning principles,
* sports science,
* biomechanics,
* physiology,
* rehabilitation principles,
* coaching evidence,
* and practical field experience.

The system must distinguish between:

* well-supported principles,
* plausible but uncertain methods,
* emerging practices,
* and unsupported claims.

Novel or controversial exercises must not be presented as essential.

When evidence is uncertain, the system should prefer simpler, safer and better-established methods.

---

# Rule 31 — No Exercise Dogma

The system must not promote ideological exercise selection.

It must not assume that:

* barbells are always superior,
* machines are always inferior,
* free weights are always more functional,
* unstable exercises are always better for athletes,
* Olympic lifts are mandatory for power,
* sport-specific imitation guarantees transfer,
* or complex exercises are more advanced.

Exercise value depends on context.

---

# Rule 32 — Minimal Effective Exercise Selection

The system should select the minimum number of exercises required to achieve the session objective.

More exercises do not automatically create a better session.

Redundant exercises should be removed.

Two exercises are considered redundant when they create nearly identical:

* adaptations,
* movement patterns,
* muscular demands,
* fatigue profiles,
* and joint stresses.

The system should prefer a small number of high-value exercises executed with quality.

---

# Rule 33 — Session Coherence

Every exercise in a session must be compatible with the others.

The complete session should have:

* a clear primary objective,
* limited redundancy,
* controlled fatigue,
* logical exercise order,
* appropriate movement balance,
* and a realistic duration.

The system must reject sessions that are individually plausible but collectively incoherent.

---

# Rule 34 — User Feedback Loop

Exercise selection must evolve according to athlete feedback.

Relevant feedback includes:

* pain,
* perceived difficulty,
* technical confidence,
* enjoyment,
* soreness,
* fatigue,
* performance progression,
* recovery quality,
* and interference with combat training.

The engine should not automatically replace an exercise after one poor session.

It should identify whether the issue comes from:

* excessive load,
* inappropriate volume,
* poor technique,
* insufficient recovery,
* an unsuitable variation,
* or the exercise itself.

---

# Rule 35 — Evaluation of Exercise Effectiveness

An exercise is considered effective when it produces measurable progress without unacceptable cost.

Possible indicators include:

* increased load,
* increased repetitions,
* increased velocity,
* improved technical quality,
* greater range of motion,
* reduced pain,
* improved power output,
* improved conditioning,
* improved combat performance,
* or improved recovery tolerance.

An exercise should be reconsidered when:

* progression repeatedly stalls,
* pain persists,
* fatigue cost becomes excessive,
* transfer is poor,
* or a better alternative becomes available.

---

# Decision Summary

The exercise-selection decision can be summarized as:

```text
Desired Adaptation
        ↓
Required Movement Function
        ↓
Athlete Constraints
        ↓
Valid Exercise Candidates
        ↓
Safety and Contraindication Filter
        ↓
Adaptation-to-Cost Ranking
        ↓
Exercise Variation Selection
        ↓
Prescription Parameters
        ↓
Athlete Feedback
        ↓
Future Adjustment
```

---

# Final Principle

The Combat Athlete System does not ask:

> What exercise should the athlete do?

It asks:

> What adaptation is required, and which exercise can produce it most effectively for this athlete, at this moment, with the lowest unnecessary cost?

Exercises are tools.

The adaptation is the objective.

The athlete is the priority.
