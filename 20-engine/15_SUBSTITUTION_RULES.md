# SUBSTITUTION RULES

Version 0.1

---

# Purpose

The Substitution Rules define how the Combat Athlete System replaces an exercise, module, training method or session component when the original prescription cannot or should not be performed.

A substitution is not a random alternative.

It is a controlled replacement designed to preserve the intended adaptation while respecting the athlete’s current constraints.

The fundamental principle is:

> Replace the implementation, not the objective.

The system must preserve the purpose of the original prescription whenever possible.

---

# Core Principle

A valid substitution should preserve, in order of priority:

1. Safety
2. Primary adaptation
3. Movement function
4. Force direction
5. Contraction type
6. Velocity intent
7. Range of motion
8. Loading potential
9. Technical demand
10. Fatigue cost
11. Equipment compatibility
12. Athlete preference

Not all criteria can always be preserved.

When compromise is necessary, the system must protect the highest-priority criteria first.

---

# Scope

The substitution system applies to:

* individual exercises,
* exercise variations,
* equipment,
* movement patterns,
* loading methods,
* contraction types,
* conditioning modalities,
* combat-specific drills,
* session modules,
* session order,
* volume,
* intensity,
* and complete sessions.

Substitution does not always mean replacing one exercise with another.

Sometimes the correct substitution is to modify:

* the load,
* the range of motion,
* the tempo,
* the exercise position,
* the number of sets,
* the training modality,
* or the entire session objective.

---

# Substitution Pipeline

Every substitution must follow this process:

1. Identify why the original prescription cannot be performed.
2. Identify the primary adaptation of the original prescription.
3. Identify the non-negotiable characteristics.
4. Identify the athlete’s current constraints.
5. Generate valid substitution candidates.
6. Remove contraindicated candidates.
7. Rank remaining candidates by similarity and cost.
8. Select the most appropriate substitute.
9. Adjust prescription parameters.
10. Record the reason for substitution.
11. Evaluate the result after execution.

The system must never select a substitute before identifying the reason for replacement.

---

# Rule 1 — Identify the Cause

Substitution causes are classified into categories.

## Pain or Injury

Examples:

* joint pain,
* muscle strain,
* tendon irritation,
* instability,
* neurological symptoms,
* return-to-training restrictions.

## Equipment Limitation

Examples:

* no barbell,
* no pull-up bar,
* no heavy bag,
* no medicine ball,
* no cable machine,
* insufficient load.

## Environmental Limitation

Examples:

* limited space,
* unsuitable floor,
* low ceiling,
* noise restriction,
* bad weather,
* crowded gym.

## Technical Limitation

Examples:

* athlete cannot perform the movement safely,
* insufficient coordination,
* insufficient mobility,
* excessive complexity,
* unstable technique.

## Fatigue or Recovery Limitation

Examples:

* poor sleep,
* residual soreness,
* low readiness,
* recent combat session,
* excessive weekly load.

## Scheduling Limitation

Examples:

* reduced available time,
* unexpected session interruption,
* competition schedule change,
* additional combat training.

## Preference or Adherence Limitation

Examples:

* strong dislike,
* fear,
* lack of confidence,
* low motivation,
* repeated refusal.

## Performance Limitation

Examples:

* progression plateau,
* poor stimulus,
* excessive fatigue,
* low transfer,
* inability to load effectively.

The cause determines the appropriate substitution strategy.

---

# Rule 2 — Preserve the Primary Adaptation

The substitute must first preserve the primary adaptation.

Examples:

* Maximum Strength must remain high-force training.
* Power must preserve explosive intent.
* Hypertrophy must preserve sufficient mechanical tension.
* Aerobic conditioning must preserve the intended cardiovascular demand.
* Robustness must preserve controlled tissue loading.
* Movement work must preserve coordination and positional quality.
* Combat-specific training must preserve the relevant technical or physical demand.

A substitute that matches the movement but changes the adaptation is not equivalent.

Example:

Replacing heavy weighted pull-ups with high-repetition band pulldowns may preserve vertical pulling, but not maximum strength.

---

# Rule 3 — Preserve the Movement Function

When possible, the substitute should preserve the original movement function.

Examples:

* Vertical Pull should be replaced by another Vertical Pull.
* Horizontal Push should be replaced by another Horizontal Push.
* Knee-Dominant Lower Body work should remain knee-dominant.
* Hip-Dominant Lower Body work should remain hip-dominant.
* Anti-Rotation work should remain anti-rotation.
* Rotational Power should remain rotational power.
* Striking Power should remain a high-intent striking or ballistic action.

Movement function is more important than visual similarity.

---

# Rule 4 — Preserve Force Direction

The substitute should preserve the dominant force direction whenever relevant.

Force directions include:

* vertical,
* horizontal,
* diagonal,
* rotational,
* lateral,
* anterior-posterior,
* upward,
* downward.

Examples:

* A horizontal medicine ball throw is a better substitute for a punch-power drill than a vertical jump.
* A row is a better substitute for another horizontal pull than a lat pulldown.
* A horizontal sled push may better preserve acceleration demands than a vertical leg press.

Force direction is especially important for power and combat transfer.

---

# Rule 5 — Preserve Contraction Type

The system should preserve the dominant contraction type where possible.

Contraction types include:

* concentric,
* eccentric,
* isometric,
* stretch-shortening cycle,
* ballistic,
* quasi-isometric,
* yielding isometric,
* overcoming isometric.

Examples:

* An isometric split squat should preferably be replaced by another isometric knee-dominant exercise.
* A Nordic hamstring curl should not automatically be replaced by a hip thrust because the eccentric knee-flexion stimulus is different.
* Reactive jumps should be replaced by another low-contact-time plyometric rather than a slow strength exercise.

---

# Rule 6 — Preserve Velocity Intent

Velocity intent is critical.

Exercises may be classified as:

* maximal velocity,
* high velocity,
* moderate velocity,
* controlled velocity,
* slow tempo,
* isometric.

A power exercise must not be replaced by a slow strength movement unless the training objective is intentionally changed.

Examples:

* Jump squat → trap bar jump
* Medicine ball throw → explosive cable punch
* Plyometric push-up → band-assisted explosive push-up
* Sprint → resisted acceleration drill

Power substitutions must preserve maximal intent even when external load changes.

---

# Rule 7 — Preserve Relative Intensity

The substitute should recreate the intended relative intensity, not necessarily the same external load.

Examples:

* A 100 kg barbell squat cannot be translated directly into dumbbell loading.
* A weighted pull-up cannot be matched solely by total kilograms on a pulldown machine.
* Heavy bag intensity cannot be defined only by strike count.

The system should instead preserve:

* repetition range,
* repetitions in reserve,
* perceived exertion,
* movement velocity,
* time under tension,
* or estimated percentage of maximum.

---

# Rule 8 — Preserve Fatigue Profile

A substitute should have a similar or lower fatigue cost unless greater fatigue is intentional.

Fatigue must be evaluated as:

* local muscular fatigue,
* central fatigue,
* cardiovascular fatigue,
* eccentric damage,
* joint stress,
* technical fatigue,
* and psychological fatigue.

Examples:

* Replacing front squats with high-volume walking lunges may excessively increase soreness.
* Replacing cycling intervals with hill sprints may increase eccentric and impact stress.
* Replacing bag rounds with burpees may reproduce exhaustion but not the same technical or muscular demands.

Equivalent exhaustion does not mean equivalent training.

---

# Rule 9 — Minimal Change First

When an exercise must be modified, the system should use the smallest effective change.

The substitution hierarchy is:

1. Adjust technique cue
2. Adjust load
3. Adjust repetitions
4. Adjust range of motion
5. Adjust tempo
6. Adjust stance or grip
7. Adjust support or stability
8. Select a different variation
9. Select a different exercise in the same pattern
10. Select a different pattern serving the same adaptation
11. Replace the module
12. Replace the session

The system should avoid replacing an entire exercise when a small modification is sufficient.

---

# Rule 10 — Regression Before Removal

When an athlete cannot perform an exercise because of technical difficulty, the system should first consider a regression.

Possible regression methods include:

* reduced load,
* assisted variation,
* reduced range of motion,
* increased external support,
* slower tempo,
* simplified coordination,
* bilateral version instead of unilateral,
* stable surface instead of unstable surface,
* machine version instead of free-weight version.

Examples:

* Pull-Up → Band-Assisted Pull-Up
* Pistol Squat → Box-Assisted Pistol Squat
* Handstand Push-Up → Pike Push-Up
* Depth Jump → Low Box Drop Landing
* Single-Leg Romanian Deadlift → Supported Single-Leg Romanian Deadlift

Regression must preserve the learning pathway when technical progression is still relevant.

---

# Rule 11 — Progression Substitution

A substitution may also be used to progress an exercise.

Progression may involve:

* greater load,
* greater range of motion,
* less assistance,
* higher velocity,
* greater instability,
* unilateral execution,
* more complex coordination,
* increased density,
* reduced rest,
* or more sport-specific execution.

Examples:

* Bodyweight Pull-Up → Weighted Pull-Up
* Goblet Squat → Front Squat
* Bilateral Jump → Single-Leg Jump
* Basic Bag Combination → Reactive Combination Round

Progression must be earned through technical consistency and adaptation readiness.

---

# Rule 12 — Pain-Based Substitution

When pain occurs, the system must not search only for a similar exercise.

It must first determine which characteristics may provoke symptoms.

Potential aggravating characteristics include:

* joint angle,
* range of motion,
* grip,
* stance,
* loading position,
* movement speed,
* instability,
* impact,
* compression,
* stretch,
* and fatigue.

Pain-based substitution options include:

* reducing range of motion,
* changing grip,
* changing stance,
* reducing load,
* reducing speed,
* adding support,
* switching from dynamic to isometric work,
* changing the plane of motion,
* or replacing the movement entirely.

Pain-free execution does not guarantee medical safety.

The system must not diagnose injury.

Significant, persistent or worsening symptoms require evaluation by an appropriate health professional.

---

# Rule 13 — Equipment-Based Substitution

Equipment substitutions must preserve the training effect rather than the appearance of the exercise.

## Barbell to Dumbbell

Examples:

* Barbell Bench Press → Dumbbell Bench Press
* Barbell Romanian Deadlift → Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift
* Barbell Split Squat → Dumbbell Split Squat

## Machine to Free Weight

Examples:

* Leg Press → Goblet Squat or Split Squat
* Chest Press Machine → Dumbbell Bench Press
* Lat Pulldown → Pull-Up or Band Pulldown

## Free Weight to Bodyweight

Examples:

* Bench Press → Push-Up progression
* Back Squat → Split Squat progression
* Romanian Deadlift → Single-Leg Hip Hinge
* Cable Row → Inverted Row

## Medicine Ball Replacement

Examples:

* Rotational Throw → Explosive Band Rotation
* Chest Pass → Plyometric Push-Up
* Overhead Slam → Explosive Kettlebell Swing or Band Slam pattern

## Heavy Bag Replacement

Examples:

* Power Bag Round → Pad Work
* Speed Bag Round → Shadowboxing with maximal speed intent
* Conditioning Bag Round → Combat-specific shadowboxing intervals
* Kick Power Round → Controlled pad kicking or resisted chamber drills

Substitutions must account for the fact that equipment changes may alter stability, loadability and velocity.

---

# Rule 14 — No-Equipment Substitution

When no equipment is available, the system may manipulate:

* leverage,
* unilateral loading,
* tempo,
* pauses,
* range of motion,
* density,
* isometrics,
* plyometrics,
* and repetition number.

Examples:

* Weighted Squat → Rear-Foot-Elevated Split Squat
* Bench Press → Deficit Push-Up
* Lat Pulldown → Doorway Row only if setup is safe
* Hip Thrust → Single-Leg Hip Thrust
* Leg Curl → Sliding Hamstring Curl
* Cable Anti-Rotation Press → Side Plank variation

The system must not prescribe improvised setups that are structurally unsafe.

---

# Rule 15 — Time-Based Substitution

When session time is reduced, the system should preserve the highest-priority adaptation.

Default reduction order:

1. Remove optional accessories
2. Reduce redundant exercises
3. Reduce low-priority volume
4. Combine compatible exercises
5. Reduce rest only when it does not change the adaptation
6. Replace long setup exercises
7. Remove secondary modules
8. Preserve the primary module

Examples:

* A strength session should preserve primary strength work before accessories.
* A power session should preserve quality and rest rather than compressing explosive work.
* An aerobic session may reduce duration while preserving target intensity.
* A mixed session may remove hypertrophy work before power or technical work.

The system must not transform the session into a circuit merely because time is limited.

---

# Rule 16 — Fatigue-Based Substitution

When readiness is low, the system may modify the session rather than cancel it.

Possible fatigue-based substitutions include:

* reduce load,
* reduce sets,
* increase repetitions in reserve,
* replace high-eccentric exercises,
* replace high-impact plyometrics,
* use machine-supported exercises,
* replace maximal intervals with aerobic work,
* replace high-intensity bag rounds with technical rounds,
* replace dynamic work with controlled isometrics,
* or perform recovery work.

The system must distinguish between:

* mild fatigue,
* moderate fatigue,
* severe fatigue,
* and illness or abnormal symptoms.

Severe fatigue or illness may require session cancellation.

---

# Rule 17 — Technical Failure Substitution

When technique is unstable, the system should first reduce task difficulty.

Possible responses include:

* reduce load,
* reduce speed,
* reduce range of motion,
* increase rest,
* use external support,
* select a simpler variation,
* or stop the exercise.

The system must not use repeated technical failure as a training method.

Technique failure is especially unacceptable in:

* maximal strength work,
* ballistic exercises,
* plyometrics,
* sprinting,
* and high-impact combat drills.

---

# Rule 18 — Strength Substitution Rules

A maximum strength substitute should preserve:

* high force production,
* low-to-moderate repetition range,
* stable execution,
* measurable loading,
* and sufficient rest.

Examples:

## Squat Pattern

* Back Squat
* Front Squat
* Safety Bar Squat
* Box Squat
* Belt Squat
* Heavy Split Squat
* Leg Press

## Hinge Pattern

* Conventional Deadlift
* Trap Bar Deadlift
* Sumo Deadlift
* Romanian Deadlift
* Block Pull
* Heavy Hip Thrust

## Horizontal Push

* Bench Press
* Dumbbell Bench Press
* Floor Press
* Weighted Push-Up
* Machine Chest Press

## Vertical Pull

* Weighted Pull-Up
* Weighted Chin-Up
* Heavy Lat Pulldown
* Assisted One-Arm Pull-Up progression

The substitute must not automatically be considered load-equivalent.

New loading parameters must be established.

---

# Rule 19 — Power Substitution Rules

A power substitute should preserve:

* maximal intent,
* low fatigue,
* high movement quality,
* appropriate force direction,
* and similar force-velocity demands.

Examples:

## Vertical Power

* Countermovement Jump
* Box Jump
* Trap Bar Jump
* Jump Squat
* Olympic Lift derivative

## Horizontal Power

* Broad Jump
* Bound
* Sprint Start
* Sled Acceleration
* Horizontal Medicine Ball Throw

## Upper-Body Power

* Medicine Ball Chest Pass
* Plyometric Push-Up
* Push Press
* Explosive Band Press

## Rotational Power

* Rotational Medicine Ball Throw
* Scoop Toss
* Explosive Cable Rotation
* Explosive Band Rotation

A non-ballistic strength exercise is not a direct substitute for ballistic power.

---

# Rule 20 — Plyometric Substitution Rules

Plyometric substitutions must consider:

* landing forces,
* contact time,
* jump height,
* direction,
* unilateral or bilateral execution,
* and athlete competency.

Plyometric levels may be classified as:

## Low Intensity

* Pogos
* Line Hops
* Snap Downs
* Landing Drills

## Moderate Intensity

* Box Jumps
* Bounds
* Lateral Hops
* Repeated Jumps

## High Intensity

* Depth Jumps
* Drop Jumps
* Maximal Single-Leg Bounds
* High-Speed Reactive Jumps

A high-intensity plyometric should not be replaced by another high-intensity drill when pain, fatigue or poor landing mechanics caused the substitution.

---

# Rule 21 — Hypertrophy Substitution Rules

A hypertrophy substitute should preserve:

* target tissue,
* useful range of motion,
* mechanical tension,
* stable execution,
* and proximity-to-failure potential.

Examples:

## Chest

* Bench Press
* Dumbbell Press
* Machine Press
* Weighted Push-Up
* Cable Press

## Back

* Pull-Up
* Lat Pulldown
* Row
* Machine Row
* Cable Row

## Quadriceps

* Squat
* Front Squat
* Split Squat
* Leg Press
* Leg Extension

## Hamstrings

* Romanian Deadlift
* Nordic Curl
* Leg Curl
* Sliding Leg Curl
* Good Morning

Exercises targeting the same broad muscle group are not always equivalent.

The system must consider muscle length, joint action and fatigue cost.

---

# Rule 22 — Conditioning Substitution Rules

Conditioning substitution must preserve the intended energy-system demand.

The system should preserve:

* interval duration,
* work-to-rest ratio,
* target intensity,
* total work duration,
* movement complexity,
* and muscular limitations.

## Aerobic Base

Possible modalities:

* running,
* cycling,
* rowing,
* swimming,
* brisk walking,
* low-intensity shadowboxing.

## Maximum Aerobic Power

Possible modalities:

* running intervals,
* bike intervals,
* rowing intervals,
* bag rounds,
* pad rounds,
* combat circuits.

## Repeated Sprint Ability

Possible modalities:

* sprinting,
* bike sprints,
* rower sprints,
* sled pushes,
* short bag flurries.

## Recovery Conditioning

Possible modalities:

* walking,
* cycling,
* light rowing,
* mobility circuits,
* easy shadowboxing.

A substitute modality must not create a new local muscular limitation that prevents the intended cardiovascular stimulus.

---

# Rule 23 — Running Substitution Rules

Running may be substituted when:

* impact is poorly tolerated,
* weather is unsafe,
* space is unavailable,
* lower-body soreness is excessive,
* or injury restrictions apply.

Possible substitutes include:

* cycling,
* rowing,
* elliptical,
* swimming,
* sled work,
* or low-impact combat intervals.

The substitute should preserve:

* session duration,
* effort zone,
* interval structure,
* and cardiovascular objective.

Running-specific adaptations cannot be fully replaced by non-running modalities.

When running performance is the goal, substitution should be temporary or partial.

---

# Rule 24 — Combat-Specific Substitution Rules

Combat-specific substitutions should preserve the relevant combat demand.

Examples:

## Heavy Bag Unavailable

* Pad work
* Shadowboxing
* Partner touch drills
* Band-resisted chamber work
* Technical combination drills

## Partner Unavailable

* Heavy bag
* Dummy drills
* Shadowboxing
* Solo footwork
* Isometric grappling positions

## Grappling Space Unavailable

* Grip work
* Clinch isometrics
* Sprawl patterns
* Ground transition drills
* Rotational strength work

## Striking Prohibited Due to Noise

* Shadowboxing
* Isometric strike positions
* Slow technical combinations
* Footwork drills
* Band drills

The system must acknowledge loss of specificity when a direct combat stimulus cannot be reproduced.

---

# Rule 25 — Heavy Bag Substitution Rules

The substitute depends on the bag objective.

## Bag Speed Work

Preferred substitutes:

* maximal-speed shadowboxing,
* speed pad work,
* light-band assisted striking,
* short technical flurries.

## Bag Power Work

Preferred substitutes:

* power pad work,
* medicine ball throws,
* explosive landmine press,
* rotational ballistic work,
* controlled maximal-intent shadowboxing.

## Bag Conditioning

Preferred substitutes:

* shadowboxing intervals,
* pad intervals,
* battle rope intervals,
* bike sprints,
* combat circuits.

## Bag Technical Work

Preferred substitutes:

* mirror shadowboxing,
* recorded technical rounds,
* partner drill,
* slow combination rehearsal.

The system must not use general conditioning as a complete substitute for striking technique.

---

# Rule 26 — Unilateral and Bilateral Substitutions

Unilateral and bilateral exercises may substitute for one another only when the adaptation remains coherent.

## Bilateral to Unilateral

Useful when:

* equipment load is limited,
* asymmetry needs attention,
* spinal loading must be reduced,
* balance and unilateral force are relevant.

## Unilateral to Bilateral

Useful when:

* stability limits force production,
* fatigue is high,
* technique is insufficient,
* greater total loading is required.

The system must adjust volume carefully.

One set per leg is not always equivalent to one bilateral set.

---

# Rule 27 — Open-Chain and Closed-Chain Substitutions

Open-chain and closed-chain exercises are not automatically equivalent.

Examples:

* Leg Extension and Squat both train knee extension but differ in total movement demand.
* Lat Pulldown and Pull-Up share a vertical pulling pattern but differ in body stabilization.
* Cable Punch and Push-Up differ in force direction and trunk contribution.

The system may substitute between them when the primary adaptation is preserved and the differences are acceptable.

---

# Rule 28 — Stable and Unstable Substitutions

Stable exercises generally allow greater force production.

Unstable exercises may increase coordination or stabilization demand.

The system should not replace a stable strength exercise with an unstable variation when maximum force is the objective.

Examples of inappropriate substitution:

* Bench Press → Swiss Ball Dumbbell Press for maximum strength
* Back Squat → Balance Board Squat for lower-body force
* Heavy Row → Unstable Suspension Row for maximal loading

Instability is selected only when it serves the adaptation.

---

# Rule 29 — Machine and Free-Weight Substitutions

Machines may be preferable when:

* fatigue is high,
* technical demand must be reduced,
* local hypertrophy is the objective,
* injury risk must be minimized,
* or training is unsupervised.

Free weights may be preferable when:

* stabilization is relevant,
* movement freedom is required,
* equipment specificity matters,
* or total-body force transmission is important.

Neither category is inherently superior.

---

# Rule 30 — Volume Adjustment After Substitution

A substitute does not automatically use the same sets and repetitions.

Volume must be adjusted according to:

* exercise difficulty,
* unilateral or bilateral execution,
* range of motion,
* loadability,
* muscle mass involved,
* fatigue cost,
* technical demand,
* and novelty.

Examples:

* Replacing barbell squats with split squats may require fewer total sets.
* Replacing machine rows with inverted rows may require repetition adjustments.
* Replacing sprints with cycling may require longer work intervals.
* Replacing bag power rounds with medicine ball throws requires lower repetition density.

---

# Rule 31 — Intensity Adjustment After Substitution

Intensity must be recalibrated.

Possible methods include:

* percentage of estimated maximum,
* repetitions in reserve,
* rate of perceived exertion,
* movement velocity,
* technical quality,
* heart-rate zone,
* breathing pattern,
* or power-output target.

The system must never copy a numerical load from one exercise to another without recalibration.

---

# Rule 32 — Rest Adjustment After Substitution

Rest duration must match the substitute and the objective.

Examples:

* Power substitutions may require full recovery.
* Unilateral exercises may require additional transition time.
* Conditioning substitutions may require adjusted work-to-rest ratios.
* Technically simpler exercises may permit slightly shorter rest.
* High-local-fatigue exercises may require longer recovery.

Rest is part of the prescription and must be substituted deliberately.

---

# Rule 33 — Exercise Order After Substitution

A substitution may change the correct exercise order.

Examples:

* A low-fatigue activation exercise may remain early.
* A high-skill substitute should move earlier.
* A high-fatigue hypertrophy substitute may need to move later.
* A combat-specific technical substitute should precede conditioning.
* A machine-based substitute may remain later in the session.

The system must reassess the whole session after substitution.

---

# Rule 34 — Session-Level Substitution

A full session may be replaced when the original objective is no longer appropriate.

Examples:

* Maximum Strength → Submaximal Strength
* Power → Technique and Movement
* High-Intensity Conditioning → Aerobic Recovery
* Heavy Bag Power → Shadowboxing Technique
* Sprint Session → Low-Impact Conditioning
* High-Load Lower Body → Upper Body and Trunk
* Full Session → Recovery Session

Session-level substitution is appropriate when modifying one exercise would not sufficiently reduce risk or fatigue.

---

# Rule 35 — Module-Level Substitution

Capability Modules may be substituted according to readiness and priority.

Possible substitutions include:

* Power Module → Movement Module
* Maximum Strength Module → Technique Strength Module
* High-Intensity Conditioning Module → Aerobic Base Module
* Combat-Specific Conditioning Module → Technical Skill Module
* Plyometric Module → Landing and Deceleration Module
* Hypertrophy Module → Robustness Module

A module substitution must be recorded as a change in session objective.

It must not be presented as an equivalent session when the adaptation has materially changed.

---

# Rule 36 — Temporary and Permanent Substitution

Substitutions are classified as:

## Temporary

Used because of:

* short-term pain,
* equipment availability,
* fatigue,
* travel,
* weather,
* or scheduling.

## Permanent

Used because of:

* chronic incompatibility,
* structural limitation,
* repeated pain,
* poor progression,
* low adherence,
* or consistently poor adaptation-to-cost ratio.

Temporary substitutions should preserve the return path to the original exercise when appropriate.

Permanent substitutions should become part of the athlete’s updated exercise profile.

---

# Rule 37 — Substitution Frequency

Frequent substitutions reduce measurement quality and technical learning.

The system should avoid unnecessary variation.

An exercise should not be replaced simply because:

* the athlete had one difficult session,
* performance fluctuated once,
* the exercise feels repetitive,
* or a new exercise appears more interesting.

Repeated substitution is justified when:

* the same problem persists,
* progression is blocked,
* symptoms recur,
* the exercise is consistently poorly tolerated,
* or training context has changed.

---

# Rule 38 — Substitution Memory

The system should record:

* original prescription,
* substitute selected,
* reason,
* date,
* athlete feedback,
* pain response,
* performance result,
* fatigue result,
* and whether the substitution should be reused.

The substitution history allows CAS to learn which alternatives work best for the athlete.

The system should avoid repeatedly suggesting a previously unsuccessful substitute.

---

# Rule 39 — Substitution Confidence

Each substitution should receive a confidence level.

## High Confidence

The substitute closely matches:

* adaptation,
* movement,
* force direction,
* contraction type,
* and fatigue profile.

## Moderate Confidence

The substitute preserves the main adaptation but changes one or more secondary characteristics.

## Low Confidence

The substitute only partially preserves the original objective.

Low-confidence substitutions must be clearly identified.

The system must not present partial equivalence as complete equivalence.

---

# Rule 40 — Substitution Scoring

Candidate substitutes may be scored using:

* Safety
* Adaptation Match
* Movement Match
* Force Direction Match
* Contraction Match
* Velocity Match
* Range of Motion Match
* Intensity Compatibility
* Fatigue Compatibility
* Equipment Compatibility
* Technical Compatibility
* Combat Transfer
* Athlete Preference
* Previous Athlete Response

Example:

```text
Substitution Score =
Safety × 5
+ Adaptation Match × 5
+ Athlete Compatibility × 5
+ Movement Match × 4
+ Force Direction Match × 3
+ Contraction Match × 3
+ Velocity Match × 3
+ Fatigue Compatibility × 3
+ Technical Compatibility × 3
+ Equipment Compatibility × 2
+ Combat Transfer × 2
+ Previous Athlete Response × 2
+ Athlete Preference × 1
```

Safety failures create automatic exclusion regardless of total score.

---

# Rule 41 — Hard Exclusion Rules

A substitution must be rejected when:

* it causes significant pain,
* it is contraindicated,
* it exceeds the athlete’s technical ability,
* it requires unavailable or unsafe equipment,
* it changes the adaptation beyond acceptable limits,
* it creates excessive fatigue,
* it conflicts with current recovery needs,
* or it increases injury risk.

---

# Rule 42 — Soft Exclusion Rules

A substitution may be deprioritized when:

* the athlete dislikes it,
* setup is inconvenient,
* progression is difficult to measure,
* it produces unnecessary soreness,
* it duplicates another exercise,
* or previous athlete response was poor.

Soft exclusions do not automatically prohibit use.

---

# Rule 43 — No False Equivalence

The system must never imply that all exercises within the same muscle group are equivalent.

Examples:

* Deadlift is not identical to Romanian Deadlift.
* Pull-Up is not identical to Lat Pulldown.
* Sprinting is not identical to cycling.
* Heavy Bag Power is not identical to Medicine Ball Power.
* Squat is not identical to Leg Extension.
* Shadowboxing is not identical to Pad Work.

A substitution may be valid without being identical.

The system must communicate meaningful differences when relevant.

---

# Rule 44 — Adaptation Loss

When no substitute can preserve the original objective, the system must identify the loss.

Examples:

* No heavy bag or partner may reduce striking-impact specificity.
* No sprinting may reduce running-specific adaptation.
* No heavy load may reduce maximum-strength stimulus.
* No reactive surface may reduce reactive plyometric specificity.

The system should state:

* what is preserved,
* what is reduced,
* and whether the loss is acceptable temporarily.

---

# Rule 45 — User-Initiated Substitution

When the athlete requests a different exercise, the system should evaluate the reason.

The request may be accepted when the substitute:

* remains safe,
* preserves the objective,
* improves adherence,
* fits available equipment,
* or has worked well previously.

The request may be rejected or modified when it:

* changes the objective,
* increases risk,
* adds unnecessary complexity,
* creates interference,
* or conflicts with current constraints.

Athlete preference matters, but it does not override safety or program coherence.

---

# Rule 46 — Coaching Communication

When a substitution is made, the system should explain it concisely.

Recommended structure:

```text
Original exercise:
Substitute:
Reason:
What remains the same:
What changes:
New prescription:
```

Example:

```text
Original exercise: Weighted Pull-Up
Substitute: Heavy Lat Pulldown
Reason: No pull-up bar available
What remains the same: Vertical pulling and high-force upper-back work
What changes: Lower stabilization and bodyweight-control demand
New prescription: 4 sets of 5 repetitions at RPE 8
```

The athlete should understand why the substitution was made.

---

# Rule 47 — Review After Execution

After the substitute is performed, the system should evaluate:

* pain,
* technique,
* perceived effort,
* target-muscle stimulus,
* performance quality,
* fatigue,
* enjoyment,
* and next-day recovery.

The result determines whether the substitute should be:

* retained,
* adjusted,
* rejected,
* or used only in specific contexts.

# Substitution Decision Tree

```text
Original Prescription Cannot Be Performed
                ↓
Identify the Cause
                ↓
Is the Athlete Safe to Train?
        ↓ Yes                    ↓ No
Identify Primary Adaptation     Stop or Replace Session
        ↓
Can the Original Exercise Be Modified?
        ↓ Yes                    ↓ No
Use Minimal Modification        Generate Substitute Candidates
        ↓                               ↓
Reassess Safety                 Apply Hard Exclusion Rules
                                        ↓
                              Rank by Adaptation Similarity
                                        ↓
                              Adjust Load, Volume and Rest
                                        ↓
                                  Execute Substitute
                                        ↓
                                 Record Athlete Response
```

# Final Principle

The Combat Athlete System does not ask:

> What exercise looks similar?

It asks:

> Which available option preserves the intended adaptation most effectively, safely and efficiently for this athlete under the current constraints?

A substitution is not a compromise by default.

When selected correctly, it is an intelligent adjustment that protects progress.
